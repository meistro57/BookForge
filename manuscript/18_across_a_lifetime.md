# CHAPTER 18
## Across a Lifetime

Here's something nobody told me at twenty-five, and I want to lead with it because if
you're in a bad stretch it's the only thing in this chapter that matters:

**This changes.**

Not cured. Not outgrown — that's a myth and we'll get to it. But the *shape* of it changes
across a life, and for a great many people the hardest part is not the part they're
currently in.

I'm fifty-four. I've been through most of the arc. Let me report back from further down the
road.

---

### Childhood: the worst fit you will ever face

I want to say this plainly because I've never seen it written down:

**School is the single worst-matched environment most of us will ever be placed in, and
it's compulsory, and it lasts thirteen years.**

Go back to Chapter 15's six factors. Same room every day. Same schedule. Slow feedback —
weeks between doing the work and hearing about it. Body required to be still. No control
over sequence. Almost entirely procedure rather than problems.

That's zero out of six. There is no adult job that scores that badly, and if there were,
you could quit.

So if you're a parent watching your kid struggle and wondering whether this is what the
rest of their life looks like: **no. Statistically, this is the bottom.** They are in the
most hostile environment they will ever be legally required to attend, and it ends.

That's not optimism, it's structure.

---

### The twenties: when the scaffolding comes off

Here's a pattern I've watched play out over and over, including in myself.

A lot of us do adequately in high school and then fall apart at university, or in the first
real job, and nobody can work out why. The demands didn't get *harder*, exactly. What
happened was that all the external structure disappeared at once.

School supplies structure constantly — bells, a fixed timetable, someone noticing you're
absent, homework due tomorrow rather than in three months. It's a bad fit and it's also a
massive external scaffold, and Chapter 13 already told you that external scaffolding is the
entire method.

Then at eighteen you get handed total freedom and a long-horizon deadline and nobody
checking on you, and the thing that was holding you up is gone.

If that was you, you weren't lazy and you didn't "waste your potential." You had the
scaffolding removed and hadn't yet learned to build your own — which is a thing nobody
teaches, because most people don't need it taught.

---

### The thirties and forties: the crunch

This is where it usually gets hardest, and it's where most late diagnoses happen. Mine was
in there.

Look at what lands in that window. Career complexity increasing. Mortgage. Kids, maybe.
Aging parents, maybe. Multiple competing systems that all require tracking, none of which
you can drop.

**Executive function demand peaks in midlife.** All of it lands at once, and it's the exact
resource that's in short supply.

That's why people who "managed fine" for years suddenly can't. Nothing got worse. The load
went up past what the compensating strategies could carry, and the strategies were never
built for this weight.

If you're in that stretch right now and you feel like you're failing at things you used to
handle: you're not deteriorating. You're at the peak of the demand curve. It comes down.

---

### Fifty and after: the part I can actually report

I'm going to be careful here and tell you only what I've lived.

**It got better.** Substantially. Not because the wiring changed — I still lose things,
still can't feel time, still surface at 3am from work nobody asked for. The traits are
exactly where they were.

Four things changed, and none of them are inspirational.

**One: I found the placement.** Thirty years in one trade, two roles that fit for different
reasons. That's Chapter 5's whole thesis playing out slowly. Most of us find it eventually,
just by elimination — you accumulate enough evidence about what doesn't work that you drift
toward what does.

**Two: I built the systems.** Decades of accumulated scaffolding. Not one good system —
dozens of ugly ones, replaced as they died. That's Chapter 13, and it compounds.

**Three: expertise started doing the work.** This one is underrated. When you know a domain
deeply, you stop running on working memory and start running on pattern recognition. I can
look at a connection detail and know it's wrong without consciously checking, and that
costs me almost nothing. Twenty years of accumulated pattern is a form of external memory
that happens to live inside your head.

**Four: I stopped spending energy trying to be normal.** That budget was enormous and I'd
been paying it since third grade. Getting it back was like a raise.

Note that three of those four are things you build, not things that happen to you. **The
improvement isn't age. It's accumulation.** Which is genuinely good news, because it means
it isn't luck and you can start now.

---

### What doesn't improve, honestly

Two corrections so this doesn't read as a promise.

**Hyperactivity doesn't disappear — it goes internal.** The kid who couldn't stay in his
seat becomes the adult who looks perfectly still and has a mind that will not stop. This is
why people think they outgrew it. They didn't. It stopped being visible, which is not the
same thing, and the 2am version is often worse than the classroom version because nobody
can see it to sympathize.

**And in older age it gets misread.** Losing things, forgetting names, difficulty focusing —
in a seventy-year-old, everybody including the seventy-year-old assumes that's aging, or
worse, and gets frightened. Sometimes it's just the same lifelong pattern in a body that's
older. Older adults with this wiring are badly underdiagnosed, partly because the whole
field was built around children and partly because everyone stops asking.

If that's you or your parent: it's worth asking whether this has been the pattern all
along, rather than assuming the worst.

---

### The accounting

I don't want to end this chapter with a clean upward line, because that's not what a life
looks like.

Getting better doesn't undo what happened on the way. The jobs that ended badly, the money
that went, the friendships that thinned out, the years spent believing something was
fundamentally wrong with me. Those are real and I don't get them back, and if you're
further along than twenty-five you've got your own version of that list.

There's a particular grief in working something out at fifty that would have been useful at
fifteen. I've felt it writing this book. Every chapter has had a moment where I thought:
*this would have changed everything, and nobody knew to tell me.*

That's allowed. Feel it. It's a legitimate loss.

And then — and this is the only useful thing to do with it — **remember that the version of
you reading this is the earliest version that will ever exist again.** Whatever age that
is. Twenty-five is early. Fifty is early. I was fifty-one when I started writing any of
this down, and it was early enough to be worth doing.

---

### To the twenty-five-year-old

If you're in the part where nothing works and you're watching everyone your age get
traction, here's what I'd tell you.

You are in the hardest stretch, structurally — freedom without scaffolding, before you've
learned to build your own, and before you've found the room where the wiring pays.

Both of those are things you acquire, not things you're born with. Most of us get them.
It's slow and unglamorous and it mostly happens by accumulation, which is the least
satisfying answer available and happens to be the true one.

It got better for me. Not because I improved. Because I stopped fighting the wiring and
started building around it — and thirty years of building around something adds up to a
life that mostly fits.

You've got the instructions now. That's roughly thirty years earlier than I had them.
