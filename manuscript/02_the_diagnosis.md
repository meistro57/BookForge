# CHAPTER 2
## The Diagnosis That Explained Everything and Fixed Nothing

Fluorescent lights. That antiseptic smell every medical building has, the one that
somehow gets into your clothes. A chair with a vinyl seat. Somebody two doors down
laughing at something.

I'm in my early thirties. And a doctor is telling me I have Attention Deficit
Hyperactivity Disorder.

For about three seconds, everything in my life makes sense.

Third grade in the hallway. The Constitution test. Every job where I did brilliant work
for four months and then quietly fell apart on the paperwork. Every conversation where
somebody said *I already told you this* and meant it, because they had. Every book I
started. Every system I built for myself that worked great for two weeks. Thirty years of
evidence, and here's the through-line, and it has a name, and the name is in a book that
doctors use.

Three seconds of the cleanest relief I have ever felt.

Then I felt completely broken, and that lasted about three years.

---

Nobody warns you about that part.

The story everybody tells about diagnosis is the relief story. *Finally, an explanation.
Finally, it wasn't my fault.* And that's real — I'm not taking it away from anyone. But
it's the first three seconds. It's not the next three years.

Here's what happens in the next three years, at least if you're built like me.

You go home. You look it up. And the internet is extremely helpful when what you're
trying to do is catastrophize.

Within twenty minutes I had learned that people like me have worse career outcomes,
higher divorce rates, more accidents, more debt, shorter lifespans, and a substantially
elevated risk of basically every bad ending on offer. Every article was written in that
flat clinical register that makes it sound less like information and more like a verdict.
I sat there with a screen full of statistics about people who were, apparently, me.

And here's the thing I hadn't seen coming.

Before that appointment, all my failures were mine. That sounds worse than it was. If
the mess was my fault, then it was also mine to fix — I could try harder, get up
earlier, want it more. I'd been running that program for thirty years. It didn't work
very well, but it was *mine*, and it left the door open.

After that appointment, my failures belonged to a condition. Permanent. Documented.
Named. In a book.

I didn't get an explanation. I got a life sentence with a citation.

---

The word "disorder" does a lot of heavy lifting in that acronym.

Sit with it a second. *Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder.* Three of those four
words are telling you something's missing or something's too much, and the fourth one
tells you it's the wrong kind of thing to be.

**Deficit.** You're short. There's a normal amount of attention and you didn't get it.

**Disorder.** Not different. Not unusual. *Disordered.* Out of order. Broken order.
Order that failed.

Nobody sat me down and said *you are fundamentally defective.* They didn't have to. The
name says it, quietly, every time you say it out loud. I said it out loud a lot in those
three years, and every time, some small part of me heard the sentence underneath.

I want to be precise about this, because it's the hinge the whole book turns on: the
diagnosis was correct. I'm not going to spend two hundred pages telling you ADHD isn't
real, because it's real, and the thing it describes is the thing I have.

The diagnosis was correct. **The framing was a choice.** And the framing is what nearly
took me out.

---

So what were those three years actually like?

Mostly, they were quieter than you'd think. I didn't fall apart dramatically. I went to
work. I did my job. From the outside nothing much changed.

What changed was the story running underneath everything.

I stopped trying things. Not consciously — you don't decide to stop trying. But when you
know the ending, why start? I'd get an idea, feel the old spark, and then some voice with
a clipboard would point out that I don't finish things, that this is documented, that
we've been through this. And the spark would go out.

I got worse at asking for help, because now asking meant admitting the condition rather
than admitting a bad week.

I got fluent in a certain kind of joke. You know the one. Self-deprecating, gets a laugh,
lands first so nobody else can land it. I made a lot of those jokes. Every one of them
was true and every one of them cost me something small that I didn't notice at the time.

And underneath it all, the thing I couldn't say out loud: *I finally know what's wrong
with me and it turns out the answer is: me. That's what's wrong. There's no fixing that.*

If you're in that stretch right now, I want you to know two things. It's extremely
common — most of the people I've since met who got diagnosed as adults describe some
version of it. And it is not the end of the story. It's a stage. It has an exit, and I'm
going to spend the rest of this book showing you where the door is.

---

What got me out wasn't a technique. I wish it were, because I could put it in a box at
the end of the chapter and you could go do it.

What got me out was a slow accumulation of a single piece of evidence:

**In some environments, I was excellent.**

Not "coping well." Not "managing my symptoms." *Excellent.* Better than the people around
me who had no trouble sitting still.

I'd already seen it in the shop and hadn't known what to do with it. Hands on the metal,
eleven things moving at once, consequences immediate — I was good there. I was fast. I
caught things other people missed. And then I'd go home and read about my elevated risk
of poor life outcomes.

Both of those things were true at once. That was the crack in the wall.

Because a deficit doesn't work that way. If I'm short on attention — if there's a normal
amount and I got less — then I should be worse everywhere. Deficits don't take days off.
A guy who's missing a leg is missing it in every room he walks into.

But mine came and went depending on what was in front of me. Give me a form to fill out
and I'm a disaster. Give me a structure that has to fit together right, or a problem
nobody's solved, or a shop floor at seven in the morning, and I'm the guy you want.

That's not a deficit. **That's a mismatch.**

I didn't have the words for it then. It took me years to work it out properly, and this
book is where I finally wrote it down. But that was the first loose thread, and once I
pulled it, the whole thing came apart in the best possible way.

---

Here's where we're going next.

The diagnosis gave me a real description wrapped in a bad story. Chapter 3 is about the
bad story — where "deficit" and "disorder" came from, who benefits from that framing,
and why a name that describes what you *lack* is a terrible tool for building a life.

Then we throw it out and start over with a better one.

The doctor was right about the wiring. Everything after that was interpretation — and
interpretation is the part you get to change.
