# CHAPTER 9
## Time Blindness

You have plenty of time.

You have plenty of time, you have plenty of time, you have plenty of time — and then you
are twenty minutes late and you have no idea what happened. Nothing went wrong. You didn't
get distracted by anything dramatic. The time simply wasn't where you left it.

This is the one that has cost me the most. Not the distractibility, not the unfinished
projects. This one. Because everyone else experiences it as a statement about how much you
respect them, and there is no explanation you can give that doesn't sound like an excuse.

Welcome to Part Three. We're done describing the wiring. From here on it's what to
actually do, and we start with the thing that does the most damage.

---

### Now, and Not Now

Here's the single most useful sentence I've found on this subject:

**People with this wiring experience two times. Now, and Not Now.**

That's it. That's the whole mechanism.

Most people carry an internal sense of time as a continuous line with graduations on it —
they can feel three days from now as closer than three weeks from now, without doing any
arithmetic. It's a felt sense, like balance. They don't think about it because they've
never had to.

We don't have that. We have a very bright, very loud **Now**, and then an undifferentiated
fog called **Not Now** where everything else lives. Your dentist appointment tomorrow and
your taxes in April are both in the fog. They feel about the same distance away, which is
to say: not real yet.

And then tomorrow arrives, and the dentist appointment crosses out of the fog into Now,
and it isn't "approaching" — it's suddenly, urgently *here*, with no warning, as though it
had been sprung on you.

It wasn't sprung on you. You knew about it for three weeks. You just couldn't *feel* it
getting closer, because you don't have the instrument that does that.

Everything else in this chapter follows from that one fact.

Underestimating how long things take? You're estimating from inside Now, where duration
has no reliable scale. Deadlines that are far away right up until they're on fire? Fog,
then Now, nothing in between. Task paralysis? You're being asked to sequence and prioritize
using a sense you don't have. Hyperfocus eating four hours? Now expanded and swallowed the
afternoon.

One mechanism. All of it.

---

### Why knowing doesn't fix it

The standard advice is "use a calendar," and if you're anything like me you have used a
calendar, several calendars, and about nine apps, and you are still late.

Here's why that advice fails, and understanding this saves you years of thinking you're
uniquely bad at a solved problem.

**A calendar tells you when something is. It does not tell you how much time is left.**

Those are completely different pieces of information, and it's the second one you're
missing. You have never once been late because you didn't *know* the appointment was at
two. You were late because at 1:20, twenty minutes did not feel like twenty minutes — it
felt like a comfortable amount of Not Now.

This is not an information problem. It's a *perception* problem, and you cannot solve a
perception problem by writing the information down somewhere else.

Which points directly at what does work.

---

### The two things that actually work

I could give you seven strategies here. Most books do. But we both know what happens when
you hand somebody with this wiring a list of seven things: they do none of them.

So here are two. Do these. Add the rest later if you want.

---

**ONE: Make time physically visible.**

If you can't feel time passing, you have to *see* it passing. Not read it — see it.

This is why a digital clock is nearly useless and an analog one isn't. `1:47` is a fact
you have to do math on. A hand moving toward a mark is a *quantity you can watch shrinking*.

The single best tool I've found is a visual timer — the kind with a colored disc that
disappears as the time runs out. You set it for twenty minutes, and the red wedge gets
visibly smaller, and some part of your brain that never learned to track duration
internally can suddenly just... look at it. The information gets in through the eyes
instead of through a sense you don't have.

It feels absurdly simple. It's the highest-leverage thing in this chapter.

Put one where you actually are. Not an app — an app is behind a screen you'll get lost in.
A physical object, in the room, in your eyeline.

---

**TWO: Multiply your estimate.**

You underestimate. Consistently, predictably, in a direction that never varies. So stop
trying to estimate better, because you've been trying that your whole life and your
accuracy has not improved.

Instead: **estimate, then multiply.**

Your source material says double or triple, and that's the right instinct. My own number
is two and a half, arrived at by being wrong for twenty years. Yours might be different.
Find it the boring way — for two weeks, write down your estimate before a task and the
actual time after. Divide. You'll have your number, and it'll be depressingly consistent.

Then just use it. Every time. Don't argue with it — the whole point is that your gut is
the thing that's broken here, so overriding the multiplier with a feeling defeats the
exercise.

This one costs nothing and it fixed more of my life than anything else on the list.

---

### Secondary tactics, once those are working

Briefly, because they're real but they're not where to start:

**Anchor to events, not clock times.** "After lunch" is something you'll notice. "2pm"
requires you to check. Hook tasks to things that already happen.

**Work backward from the deadline** and write down the steps with dates. This won't make
you feel the time, but it converts a fog into a list, and a list you can act on.

**Break big tasks into chunks with time limits.** "Clean the garage" has no shape and lives
in the fog forever. "Sort tools, twenty minutes" is a thing with edges.

**Use other people.** Telling someone your plan borrows their sense of time. This isn't a
crutch. It's the same as borrowing a level — you don't feel bad about using a level.

---

### The part I won't tell you

Your draft had a section called "The Gift in Time Blindness." It says this trait reflects
an ability to live deeply in the moment, untethered by rigid schedules, free from societal
norms about productivity.

I'm cutting it, and I want to explain why rather than just deleting it.

Time blindness made me miss things. Not appointments — *things*. Moments I don't get back,
with people who noticed I wasn't there. It has made people who love me feel like they were
low on my list, when the truth is they were the whole list and I simply could not feel the
clock.

There is no version of that I'm willing to call a gift.

And here's the practical reason, not just the emotional one: **if you tell yourself it's a
gift, you won't put the timer on the desk.** The reframe becomes a reason not to do the
boring thing that actually helps. I've watched people do this — turn an honest limitation
into a personality, and then defend the personality instead of fixing the problem.

Now — is there something real in there? Yes. Living hard in Now is genuinely valuable. It's
part of why hyperfocus works, why I can lose myself in a build, why I'm decent in a crisis
when everyone else is thinking about consequences.

But it's not compensation. **It's the same trait.** The presence and the lateness are one
thing, not a gift with a drawback attached. You don't get to keep one and return the other,
and pretending otherwise is how you end up with the cost and none of the tools.

Own the presence. Set the timer anyway.

---

### What to do this week

Buy a visual timer. The physical kind. Twenty dollars.

For two weeks, write your estimate before a task and the real number after. Get your
multiplier.

That's it. Two things, and the first one is a purchase.
