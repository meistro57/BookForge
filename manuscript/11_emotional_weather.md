# CHAPTER 11
## Emotional Weather

Somebody sends a two-line email. Neutral words. Something like *"Can we talk about the
Henderson job?"*

And the floor drops out.

Within about four seconds you have constructed a complete narrative in which you have
screwed up badly, they've been discussing it without you, and this is the beginning of the
end. Your chest is tight. You've read the sentence eleven times looking for the tone. You
are, in a very real physical sense, in danger.

It's a two-line email. The Henderson job is fine. They want to move a delivery date.

This chapter is about that, and it's the one that has cost me the most in relationships —
not because of what I feel, but because of what I've done in the ninety seconds after.

---

### What's actually happening

Two things, and the second one gets less attention than it should.

**One: the volume is higher.** Emotions arrive at greater intensity than the situation
calls for. Not metaphorically — you get the physical package too. Heart rate, chest,
adrenaline. A minor frustration produces the body-state of a real threat. Good news lands
euphoric. Bad news lands catastrophic. There's no proportioning stage.

**Two — and this is the one that actually causes the damage: recovery is slow.**

Everybody's feelings spike. Most people's come back down in a couple of minutes. Ours can
stay up for hours, sometimes a whole day, long after we've intellectually accepted that
the email was about a delivery date.

That gap between *knowing it's fine* and *feeling fine* is where the trouble lives. It's
why "just calm down" has never once worked on you, and why you can be simultaneously
certain you're overreacting and completely unable to stop.

*(The standard explanation involves the amygdala firing hard while the prefrontal cortex
does a weaker job of moderating it. Same caveat as always — that's the simplified version,
I'm not qualified to referee it, and the pattern is what matters. You can check the pattern
in your own life. You cannot check your amygdala.)*

---

### Rejection sensitivity

There's a specific version of this that deserves its own name, and a lot of people find
enormous relief the first time they hear it described.

**Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria.** The short version: perceived criticism or rejection
produces pain out of all proportion — genuine, physical, hard-to-breathe pain. And
crucially, *perceived* is doing real work in that sentence. It doesn't need to be actual
rejection. A tone. A delayed reply. A face that didn't light up the way you expected.

If you've ever been called dramatic for a reaction that felt, from the inside, like being
hit — that's what this is describing.

**Two honest notes.** First: RSD isn't a formal diagnosis. It isn't in the DSM. It's a
clinical description that a lot of practitioners and a great many patients find useful, and
that's a different status than "established medical fact." I'd rather tell you that than
have you find out later.

Second: the fact that it isn't official doesn't mean you're imagining it. Something real is
being described. It just hasn't been formalized, and you should know the difference when
somebody challenges you on it.

What the name does — and this is the whole value — is turn an inexplicable personal
failing into a known pattern with a shape. You stop asking *what is wrong with me* and
start asking *is this that thing again?* Which is a question you can actually answer.

---

### The honest cost

I'm not going to soften this part.

The intensity is mine to carry, but the ninety seconds afterward have hurt people who
didn't deserve it. I've sent messages I'd give a lot to have back. I've been sharp with
people over things that turned out to be nothing. I've withdrawn from people for two days
over an interpretation I invented in four seconds and never checked.

"I feel things strongly" is an explanation. It is not a permission slip. Everybody in your
life is doing their own regulating too, and none of them signed up to manage the weather
system.

So the goal of this chapter isn't to feel less. That isn't on offer, and I'd be lying if I
sold it. **The goal is to put distance between the feeling and the action.** That's the
only part that's actually under your control, and fortunately it's the part that matters
most to everyone around you.

---

### The ninety seconds

Here's the practical core. When the drop happens:

**One: name it, out loud if you can.** *"This is the thing. It's arriving at four times
scale."* Sounds absurd. Works anyway — putting language on it engages a different gear than
the one currently driving, and it converts *I am in danger* into *I am having a reaction.*
Those are different rooms.

**Two: do not respond. Not yet.** No reply, no message, no walking into their office. This
is the single highest-return move in this book for relationships, and it's free.

The rule I use: **one hour minimum before responding to anything that landed hard.**
Overnight if it's serious. Not because you're avoiding it — you're going to respond. But
you're going to respond after the volume drops, and it *will* drop, and the thing you write
then will be a thing you can live with.

Almost every message I regret was sent inside that first hour.

**Three: check your body before you trust the feeling.** Ask: am I hungry, tired, have I
been in a loud place for three hours, is this the fourth thing today? Those states amplify
everything, and if two of them are true you're not receiving accurate information about the
situation. You're receiving accurate information about your blood sugar.

**Four: ask what the evidence actually is.** The reaction generates a story that arrives
feeling like established fact — *they're angry, I'm being pushed out, they've been talking.*
Ask plainly: what do I actually know? Usually the answer is "I know they sent nine words."

**Five, and this is your own tool** — when the surge hits, ask: **is this a spark or a fire
alarm?** Spark is creative energy wanting out. Fire alarm is threat. They feel remarkably
similar from inside and they need opposite responses. Just labeling which one you're in
buys you the ten seconds that everything else in this chapter depends on.

---

### What I'm not going to suggest

There's advice floating around this space involving ice cubes, snapping rubber bands, cold
water — using physical shock to interrupt an emotional spike.

I'm leaving it out. Teaching yourself that pain is the tool you reach for when feelings get
big is a road with a bad end, and I'm not putting it in a book that people in rough shape
are going to read.

If you need to interrupt the state physically, **move.** Walk outside. Go up the stairs.
Do something with your hands. Movement does the job without teaching you anything you'll
have to unlearn.

---

### When it's bigger than a chapter

I need to be straight with you here, because this is the chapter where a book can do harm
by being too confident.

If your emotional swings are wrecking your relationships or your work, if you're spending
days rather than hours in the aftermath, if the drop takes you somewhere genuinely dark —
**the techniques above are not the right size of tool, and using them instead of getting
help is a mistake I've watched people make.**

There's real, well-established therapeutic work for exactly this. Dialectical Behavior
Therapy was built for emotional regulation specifically and has a strong track record. CBT
helps a lot of people with the interpretation problem. An ADHD coach or therapist who knows
this territory will get you further in six months than this chapter will.

I'm a steel guy who worked out some things that helped him. That's the whole warranty, and
this is the chapter where the limits of it matter most. If you're struggling badly, please
talk to someone qualified. That's not a failure of self-reliance. It's the same as calling
an engineer when the load exceeds what you can calculate.

---

### The same trait, again

You know where this is going by now.

The intensity that makes a two-line email devastating is the same intensity that makes you
care hard, notice when someone in the room is quietly struggling, and feel genuine delight
at things other people walk past.

I'm not offering that as compensation. Chapter 5's rule holds: **it's one trait, not a gift
with a drawback stapled on.** You can't keep the depth and return the devastation. Anyone
selling you that hasn't got it.

What you can do is get the gap. Feel it at full volume, and don't act for an hour.

That's it. That's the skill. It's unglamorous and it's most of the game, and if you take
one thing from this chapter, take the hour.
