# CHAPTER 20
## Finding Your Tribe

The loneliest part was never the symptoms.

It was the conviction that I was the only one. Everyone else had received an instruction
manual at some point — some briefing on how to be a person that I'd missed, probably
because I was in the hallway. They all seemed to know how to file things and arrive on time
and finish what they started, and they did it without apparent effort, and I could not work
out where they'd learned it.

Thirty years of that. And I want to be clear that this wasn't sadness exactly. It was more
like a low-grade background fact, the way you know what room you're in.

Then, in my thirties, I started talking to people who had it.

---

### What recognition actually does

I don't mean a support group. I mean the first time I described something I'd never said
out loud — some small ridiculous thing, standing in a room with no idea why I'd walked into
it — and somebody said *oh, yeah, that.*

Not sympathy. **Recognition.** A completely different substance.

Here's what it does, mechanically, and why it matters more than encouragement: it moves the
thing out of your character and into a category. As long as you're the only one, every
failure is evidence about *you*. The moment you find out that thousands of people do the
identical strange specific thing, it stops being a personal defect and becomes a property
of a type of system.

That's the same move as everything else in this book. Externalize it. Get it out of your
head and into the world where you can look at it.

I've said in this book that I'm not big on affirmations, and I'm not. But finding people
who share the wiring isn't affirmation — it's data. It's how you find out which of your
problems are yours and which ones came with the equipment.

---

### Where to find them, practically

**Online is the easiest and it's genuinely useful**, with the caveat from Chapter 19 —
notice whether a place leaves you better equipped or just more identified. Some corners
are workshops and some are waiting rooms. You'll know within a week.

**In your trade or field is better.** The people who share your wiring *and* your work are
worth ten general forums, because their solutions are already shaped for your actual
problems. There's a reason I learned more from other detailers who lose things than from
anything written for a general audience.

**And the most valuable ones are the people slightly ahead of you** — Chapter 8's point.
Not the famous ones. The person who found their room five years before you did, who can
tell you exactly what they changed and what it cost.

**You will also find them by accident**, once you can see it. It's everywhere in the
trades. It's everywhere in kitchens and ambulances and startups and anywhere the day
doesn't repeat. Say one true thing about yourself out loud in the right room and somebody
always looks up.

---

### Advocacy, honestly scaled

My earlier notes had a whole program here — raise awareness, reform workplaces, push
policy, host events during ADHD Awareness Month.

All good. And I want to say something about it that advocacy chapters usually don't:

**You are not obligated to become a spokesman for this.**

Some of you are in a bad enough stretch that getting through Tuesday is the whole project,
and a chapter telling you to also go educate your employer and lobby for insurance reform
is one more thing to fail at. Skip it. That's allowed. Come back in five years if you want.

For the rest, it scales, and the small end matters more than the large end.

**Self-advocacy is the floor and it's mostly asking for the thing rather than explaining
the reason.** Chapter 15 covered this: "I do my best work in the morning, can we move the
check-in" beats a diagnosis conversation almost every time. Know what accommodations exist
in your country and workplace. Use them. They're not charity — they're the equivalent of
the ramp, and nobody thinks a ramp is charity.

**Then there's the version almost anyone can do, and it's the one I'd push:** be the person
who says it out loud.

Not a campaign. Just — when you're in a room and somebody describes the thing, say *yeah,
me too, here's what helped.* When a colleague's kid is struggling and they mention it, ask
whether anyone's ever looked at it. When somebody calls themselves lazy in front of you, be
the one who offers a different explanation.

That's it. That's the whole advocacy program for most people, and it's not nothing —
because for a lot of us, one person saying one sentence at the right moment was the entire
turning point. It was for me. A woman on a screen at 2am who I've never met said one
sentence about fiber-optic and dial-up, and it took me twenty years to work out what to do
with it, but it started there.

You could be somebody's 2am. It costs you almost nothing and you'll usually never find out
it happened.

---

### Back to the hallway

I want to finish where we started.

There was a desk in the hallway outside a third-grade classroom in Grayslake, Illinois, and
a kid sitting at it who had worked out — correctly, from the available evidence — that he
was the kind of person who couldn't be in the room.

That kid failed the Constitution test. Everybody else went on the field trip and he sat
alone in the library to retake it, and there was a television on, and he watched Challenger
come apart at seventy-three seconds while every other eighth grader in his school was on a
bus somewhere.

I've spent forty years turning that over. I told you in Chapter 1 what I take from it, and
I'll say it once more because everything in this book is downstream of it:

**The reward for following the program is not the same thing as being where the truth is.**

That kid grew up and went into steel, first with his hands and then with drawings, and it
turned out that a brain that couldn't pass a test on how the government fits together was
extremely good at working out how buildings fit together. He put up parts of a city. His
railings ended up in a collector's book across the page from Monte Carlo. He never got a
diploma.

He also spent three years after his diagnosis believing he'd received a life sentence, made
a lot of jokes at his own expense, hurt people he loved with ninety seconds of unregulated
reaction, and lost more sleep to 2am than he'd care to add up.

Both of those are the same guy and the same wiring. That's been the argument the whole way
through: **you don't get to keep one and return the other.** There is no version where the
part that built the canopies is separated from the part that can't file the paperwork. It's
one piece of equipment.

The only thing that was ever actually available — the only lever, in twenty years of
looking — is **what you plug it into.**

---

### The last thing

I'm not going to tell you you're a superpower. You've read twenty chapters of me refusing
to.

Here's what I'll tell you instead.

You are running fast, wide, capable hardware in a world that mostly built its rooms for a
different machine. That's a real problem and it has cost you real things, and I'm not going
to pretend otherwise or ask you to feel good about the bill.

But it was never a defect. It was a mismatch. And mismatches can be worked — slowly, ugly,
one system and one room at a time, with tools you build yourself because the ones on the
shelf were made for somebody else.

That's not inspiring. It's just true, and it's what I've got.

Thirty years detailing steel taught me one thing above everything else: you cannot make a
piece fit by wanting it to. You measure what you've actually got, you work out where it
goes, and then it holds.

You've been measured wrong your whole life, by people who were reading the wrong drawing.

Go find out what you're actually for.

**You're not broken.**

**You're broadband.**

Now go find something worth all that bandwidth.

*— Mark J. Hubrich*
*Chicago, 2026*
