## The Manuscript Is Finished. Is the Book?

Imagine opening a folder on your computer.

Inside are the chapters you've been working on for months.

Every story has been written.

The Table of Contents is complete.

You've reviewed the memories.

Corrected mistakes.

And finally reached the end of the manuscript.

You sit back.

Look at the files.

And think:

"Well, that's it. I've written a book."

And you have.

At least, you've written the manuscript.

Now comes another part of the process.

Turning those words into something people can actually read.

Perhaps a paperback.

A hardcover.

An ebook.

Or an audiobook.

Maybe all four.

You might imagine this is a simple matter of pressing Export.

And sometimes, for a very small private project, producing a basic document really can be straightforward.

But professional book production introduces additional responsibilities.

The pages need to be readable.

The chapters need to appear in the correct order.

The cover needs to fit the finished book.

Photographs need suitable resolution.

The Table of Contents needs to work.

And the final files need to match the edition you actually approved.

A successful export doesn't guarantee any of those things.

A PDF can open perfectly while containing missing paragraphs.

An ebook can display text while navigating chapters incorrectly.

An audiobook can play without errors while mispronouncing someone's name.

And a printed book can arrive with the cover title disappearing into the spine.

Those aren't necessarily failures of the writing.

They're production problems.

And they're precisely what this appendix is designed to help you prevent.

We're going to approach book production in manageable stages.

Each stage has a particular purpose.

Each includes practical checks.

And each ends with a decision about whether the project is ready to continue.

Because there's one distinction worth establishing immediately.

**A completed manuscript is the source of the book. A finished edition is a particular, reviewed presentation of that manuscript.**

Those aren't quite the same thing.

Let's see how to turn one into the other.

---

# Part One: Decide What You're Actually Making

Before choosing fonts or designing a cover, decide what kind of edition you intend to produce.

This matters because different editions require different preparation.

A private family book may have different requirements from a publicly distributed paperback.

A reflowable ebook needs different layout decisions from a printed book.

And an audiobook is a separate production project.

You don't have to create every format.

In fact, beginning with one may be much more manageable.

## Your Edition Choices

### Private Digital Book

A PDF intended for personal use or sharing with a limited group.

This may be the simplest option for a first project.

### Printed Family Book

A paperback or hardcover containing selected stories and perhaps photographs.

This requires attention to physical page dimensions, margins, binding, and cover production.

### Publicly Distributed Book

A print or digital edition made available through a publisher, bookstore, or distribution service.

This introduces additional questions about metadata, identifiers, rights, distribution, and applicable platform requirements.

### Reflowable Ebook

An EPUB or similar format that allows readers to adjust text size and presentation.

This requires dependable document structure and navigation.

### Audiobook

A narrated version of the approved manuscript.

This involves script preparation, recording or authorised voice production, editing, quality control, and audio-specific distribution requirements.

You may create several editions eventually.

But choose your first target.

## Checklist F-1: Define the Edition

- [ ] I know who the intended readers are.
- [ ] I have chosen the first publication format.
- [ ] I know whether the book will remain private or be distributed publicly.
- [ ] I understand whether photographs or illustrations will be included.
- [ ] I have identified any special accessibility needs.
- [ ] I understand which production requirements still need checking.

**Production decision:** What edition are we preparing first?

Don't proceed until you can answer that in ordinary language.

For example:

"A six-by-nine-inch family paperback with a few black-and-white photographs."

That's a proposed specification, not a requirement.

The exact format should be selected based on the intended reader and the printer's actual offerings.

# Part Two: Establish the Approved Manuscript

Now let's protect the writing.

This step is particularly important for AI-assisted books.

During drafting, you may have created numerous versions.

Some contain different introductions.

Others include revised chapter titles.

Perhaps you made corrections in one file but not another.

Before typesetting, you need to identify the authoritative manuscript.

The version that will become the finished edition.

## The Publication Candidate

Create a clearly identified working copy.

For example:

**My_Book_Publication_Candidate_v1**

This is the manuscript being prepared for production.

It is not automatically the final edition.

But it is the version against which production changes will be checked.

Keep your original source records and earlier drafts separately.

Don't allow the typesetting process to overwrite them.

## What Must Be Included?

A production candidate should contain the complete intended manuscript.

Not just the chapters.

Also consider:

The title information.

Introduction or foreword.

Acknowledgements, if any.

Appendices.

Photo captions.

Reference notes.

And other approved material.

If something hasn't been approved, identify it as pending.

Don't silently include it because it happens to exist in the folder.

## Checklist F-2: Manuscript Control

- [ ] The current approved manuscript version is identified.
- [ ] Every intended chapter is present.
- [ ] Chapter numbers and titles match the approved Table of Contents.
- [ ] The intended front matter is present.
- [ ] All intended appendices are included.
- [ ] No working notes or assistant-to-author messages remain in the publication text.
- [ ] Unapproved drafts are excluded.
- [ ] The original source materials remain preserved separately.
- [ ] There is a record of unresolved editorial issues.
- [ ] The production candidate is saved as a distinct version.

**Production decision:** Is this the correct manuscript to prepare?

A book production system should never need to guess which draft is current.

# Part Three: Perform the Final Source and Accuracy Review

Now let's examine the content itself.

This is not primarily proofreading.

It's the final check that the manuscript responsibly represents its sources.

Perhaps the book contains personal memories.

Those should remain recognisable as recollections.

Perhaps a family story has an uncertain date.

That uncertainty should remain visible.

Or perhaps a chapter contains a historical or scientific claim.

That claim may require independent verification.

These issues should be addressed before the final layout is approved.

Why?

Because correcting a sentence in a manuscript is generally easier than correcting that sentence across a print PDF, ebook, audiobook script, and published edition.

## A Final Claims Review

Look for:

Precise dates.

Historical statements.

Scientific explanations.

Direct quotations.

Claims about other people.

Technical measurements.

And statements that may have originated as AI interpretations.

For each significant claim, ask:

Where did this information come from?

Does the source actually support it?

Is the level of certainty appropriate?

And is the wording fair to the people involved?

You don't need to independently prove every detail of a personal recollection.

But the manuscript should distinguish what someone remembers from what has been verified through additional evidence.

## Checklist F-3: Source Integrity

- [ ] Important memories have been compared with their source records.
- [ ] Direct quotations have been checked against available originals.
- [ ] Uncertain dates remain appropriately qualified.
- [ ] Names and important factual details have been reviewed.
- [ ] Scientific or historical claims have appropriate supporting evidence where needed.
- [ ] Working models and interpretations aren't presented as established facts.
- [ ] Invented dialogue or unsupported narrative details have been removed or clearly identified as creative reconstruction where appropriate to the work.
- [ ] Contradictory accounts have not been silently merged.
- [ ] Outstanding claims are listed for author review.
- [ ] The author has approved the factual treatment of the material.

**Production decision:** Can the manuscript's significant claims be presented responsibly?

If not, resolve the relevant issues before release.

# Part Four: Review Privacy, Permissions, and Rights

This stage deserves careful attention.

A personal book may contain:

Stories about living relatives.

Private conversations.

Photographs taken by other people.

Letters.

Song lyrics.

Published excerpts.

Or recordings contributed by family members.

Possessing a copy of that material doesn't automatically establish permission for every possible use.

A private family edition and a publicly distributed book can involve different considerations.

Likewise, recording someone doesn't automatically authorise reproducing their voice in an audiobook.

The relevant permissions depend on the source, intended use, applicable law, and any agreements involved.

## What to Examine

For every third-party contribution, identify the source.

Determine what use has been authorised.

And flag anything unresolved.

If a story contains sensitive details about another person, consider whether those details are necessary.

Perhaps a name can be omitted.

Or a private incident excluded.

But remember that changing someone's name does not always prevent identification.

The surrounding circumstances may still reveal who they are.

## Copyright and Publication Identification

Before public distribution, review applicable copyright and publishing requirements in your jurisdiction.

In the United States, original human-authored expression generally receives copyright protection upon fixation, but questions involving AI-generated material, registration, ownership, and third-party content can require closer examination.

An ISBN may be useful or required by a particular distribution channel, but not every private or digital project needs one.

Different editions and formats may also have different identifier requirements.

Check the requirements of your intended printer, publisher, or distributor rather than relying on assumptions.

## Checklist F-4: Rights and Privacy

- [ ] Third-party quotations have been reviewed for appropriate use.
- [ ] Photograph ownership and usage rights have been considered.
- [ ] Contributor permissions are documented where needed.
- [ ] Sensitive information has received an appropriate privacy review.
- [ ] Material involving children has been handled carefully.
- [ ] Public distribution has not been assumed from private-use permission.
- [ ] Audio narration and voice permissions have been considered separately.
- [ ] Any copyrighted material requiring permission has been addressed.
- [ ] Relevant publishing and identification requirements have been checked.
- [ ] The intended release has received author approval.

**Production decision:** Is the material authorised for this particular edition and intended audience?

If permission is unresolved, the safest practical approach may be to exclude or hold the affected material while the issue is reviewed.

# Part Five: Assemble the Front Matter

Now we're ready to examine the book's opening pages.

Front matter is the material that appears before the main chapters.

It may contain several elements.

But not every book needs all of them.

A short family memoir may require very little.

A larger nonfiction book may benefit from additional introductory material.

The objective is to orient the reader.

Not overwhelm them before Chapter One.

## Common Front-Matter Elements

### Half-Title Page

Often contains only the book title.

Optional depending on the format and design.

### Title Page

Usually identifies the title, subtitle if any, and author.

### Copyright and Publication Information

May include rights statements, edition information, acknowledgements of permissions, and other appropriate details.

### Dedication

A short personal dedication, if desired.

### Table of Contents

A navigation guide to the book.

### Foreword

Often written by someone introducing the author or work, though the terminology and arrangement can vary.

### Preface

Usually explains aspects of the author's approach or reasons for creating the book.

### Introduction

Introduces the subject and prepares the reader for the main text.

You don't need every one of these.

And you shouldn't add them merely to make the book look longer.

## Avoid Overlapping Introductions

This was one of the practical lessons from *The Meistro Manual*.

A manuscript may accumulate several opening sections during drafting.

An introductory note.

A foreword.

A preface.

And perhaps another introduction embedded in the title material.

Individually, they may sound reasonable.

Together, they can become repetitive.

Review what each section actually contributes.

Combine or remove redundant material when appropriate and approved.

## Checklist F-5: Front Matter

- [ ] The title and subtitle are final for this edition.
- [ ] The author name is correct and consistently presented.
- [ ] Publication and rights information has been reviewed.
- [ ] The dedication, if any, is approved.
- [ ] The foreword, preface, and introduction have distinct purposes.
- [ ] Repeated introductory material has been addressed.
- [ ] The Table of Contents matches the actual book structure.
- [ ] The front-matter order is intentional.
- [ ] No temporary production notes remain.
- [ ] The opening pages have been reviewed as a reader would encounter them.

**Production decision:** Does the book begin clearly and professionally without unnecessary repetition?

# Part Six: Prepare the Main Text

Now let's examine the chapters themselves.

A well-written manuscript may still contain structural problems.

Perhaps every chapter repeats the complete book title.

Or a part heading has become attached to the preceding chapter.

Maybe the heading levels are inconsistent.

Those problems can become particularly troublesome when converting the manuscript into different formats.

## Heading Structure

A publishing system needs to identify major sections.

Book title.

Parts.

Chapters.

Subsections.

And any subordinate headings.

The structure should be logical and consistent.

For a Markdown-based workflow, that means using heading levels deliberately.

For a word processor, it may mean applying actual paragraph styles rather than merely making text larger or bold.

The exact conventions depend on the publishing method.

But the principle remains the same.

**Structure should be encoded as structure, not merely implied by appearance.**

## Chapter Openings

Check that every chapter begins appropriately.

A chapter number.

A title.

Perhaps an epigraph or opening quotation.

And the actual prose.

Avoid repeating unnecessary book-level information in every chapter.

## Chapter Endings

Check for accidental production notes.

Phrases announcing that the chapter has been completed.

Instructions to request the next chapter.

And formal headings that belong to the following section.

A natural transition is fine.

But a manuscript should not contain leftover instructions from the AI drafting session.

## Checklist F-6: Chapter Structure

- [ ] All chapter headings are correctly identified.
- [ ] Chapter numbering is consistent.
- [ ] Part headings appear in the correct locations.
- [ ] Subheading levels follow a logical hierarchy.
- [ ] The book title isn't unnecessarily repeated in the chapter bodies.
- [ ] Chapter openings are consistent with the approved design.
- [ ] Chapter endings contain no drafting-session chatter.
- [ ] Cross-references point to the correct chapters or sections.
- [ ] Special formatting such as quotations or lists is consistent.
- [ ] All chapters appear in the intended reading order.

**Production decision:** Can the production system interpret the manuscript structure correctly?

---

# Part Seven: The Final Editorial Pass

Now let's examine the actual prose.

Even after several revisions, a complete book can contain problems that aren't obvious when reading individual chapters.

Repetition.

Inconsistent terminology.

Awkward transitions.

And paragraphs that explain something the reader already understands.

That's why a complete-manuscript read matters.

## What to Look For

Does the opening establish the book's purpose?

Do the chapters follow one another naturally?

Are important concepts introduced before they're used?

Does the same example appear repeatedly without good reason?

Are certain explanations unnecessarily long?

Does the voice remain consistent?

And does the ending suit the book's actual purpose?

A final editorial pass should consider the reading experience.

Not simply isolated grammar problems.

## Don't Rewrite for the Sake of Rewriting

A useful editing principle is to identify the actual problem before changing the prose.

Perhaps a sentence is awkward.

Fix it.

Perhaps a paragraph repeats an earlier explanation.

Condense it.

But don't rewrite a successful chapter merely because a different wording is possible.

AI can always generate another version.

That doesn't mean another version is needed.

## Checklist F-7: Final Editorial Review

- [ ] The complete manuscript has received a fresh read.
- [ ] Unnecessary repetition has been addressed.
- [ ] Terminology is consistent.
- [ ] Chapter transitions make sense.
- [ ] The author's intended voice remains recognisable.
- [ ] No unsupported emotional or causal interpretations have been introduced.
- [ ] Important stories retain their distinctive details.
- [ ] The conclusion fulfils the book's purpose.
- [ ] Remaining editorial concerns have been resolved or explicitly accepted.
- [ ] The author has approved the edited manuscript.

**Production decision:** Is the content ready to become a publication candidate?

# Part Eight: Establish a Production Freeze

Now we're going to introduce a useful practice.

A production freeze.

That simply means identifying a particular version of the manuscript as the approved basis for formatting.

For example:

**Family_Stories_Approved_Text_v1**

Now the layout work can proceed from a known version.

You can still make corrections later.

But they should be tracked deliberately.

Why?

Because uncontrolled editing during typesetting can create confusion.

Perhaps a paragraph changes in the manuscript file.

But the print PDF still contains the earlier version.

Meanwhile, the ebook includes a third version.

And the audiobook script was prepared before either correction.

Now the editions disagree.

A production freeze helps prevent that.

## Checklist F-8: Production Freeze

- [ ] The approved manuscript version is identified.
- [ ] The approved Table of Contents is recorded.
- [ ] The source files remain available.
- [ ] The editorial change log is preserved.
- [ ] All production formats will use the same approved content baseline.
- [ ] Any later corrections will be tracked and applied to all affected editions.
- [ ] The author knows which version is current.

**Production decision:** Are we ready to produce editions from this baseline?

---

# Part Nine: Preparing the Printed Interior

Now let's imagine you've chosen a paperback.

The printed interior is the collection of pages that will appear inside the physical book.

This includes the text.

Chapter openings.

Page numbers.

Photographs.

And any other interior elements.

The exact layout depends on your chosen trim size and printing service.

But several principles apply broadly.

## Trim Size

Trim size means the finished page dimensions.

Different sizes create different reading experiences.

A smaller book may feel compact.

A larger format may be useful for photographs or technical content.

Choose based on the material and reader.

Don't select a size merely because another book uses it.

## Margins

The text needs breathing room.

The inside margin must also account for the binding.

Text placed too close to the spine may be difficult to read.

Margins should follow the actual requirements of the selected printing method.

## Typography

Choose readable type.

A book's interior isn't the place to demonstrate every font installed on your computer.

Consistency matters.

The type size, line spacing, paragraph design, and heading hierarchy should support comfortable reading.

If the intended readers include people who prefer larger text, account for that in the design.

## Page Numbers

Check where numbering begins.

Front matter may use a different numbering convention from the main chapters.

Make sure the numbering is consistent with the chosen style.

## Chapter Starts

Chapters may begin on new pages.

Some designs begin major parts on particular page positions.

Those choices should be applied consistently.

But they should be deliberate.

Not accidental side effects of blank paragraphs or manual spaces.

## Checklist F-9: Printed Interior

- [ ] The trim size has been selected.
- [ ] The printer's actual file specifications have been checked.
- [ ] Margins and binding allowances are appropriate.
- [ ] Body text is comfortable to read.
- [ ] Heading styles are consistent.
- [ ] Paragraph spacing and indentation are intentional.
- [ ] Chapter opening pages follow the approved design.
- [ ] Page numbers appear correctly.
- [ ] Headers and footers are consistent, if used.
- [ ] The Table of Contents matches the final pagination where applicable.
- [ ] No text is clipped, crowded, or unexpectedly separated.
- [ ] Widows, orphans, and awkward page breaks have been reviewed.
- [ ] The final interior PDF has been visually inspected.

**Production decision:** Is the printed interior readable and structurally sound?

---

# Part Ten: Photographs and Illustrations

Now let's consider images.

Perhaps your book contains family photographs.

Old letters.

Scanned documents.

Or illustrations created specifically for the book.

Images require additional production checks.

A photograph may look perfectly good on a phone but appear blurry when printed at a larger size.

And an old scanned image may contain scratches, dust, or low contrast.

Those issues can often be improved.

But don't alter the historical content of an image without making an appropriate distinction.

For example, removing dust from a scan is different from adding a person who wasn't in the photograph.

## Image Resolution

Print services generally specify image quality requirements.

The appropriate resolution depends on the physical printed size and production method.

Check the actual requirements of your chosen provider.

Don't assume that enlarging a low-resolution image magically creates genuine missing detail.

AI enlargement may produce visually plausible detail that wasn't present in the source.

That can be acceptable for certain creative illustrations.

But it should not be confused with faithful restoration of a historical photograph.

## Captions

Check every caption.

Names.

Dates.

Locations.

And relationships.

A caption can introduce a false historical claim just as easily as a paragraph.

If a date is uncertain, keep it uncertain.

## Colour and Grayscale

Your printer may offer different production options.

Some books use black-and-white interiors.

Others use colour.

These choices affect appearance, cost, and image preparation.

Check actual provider specifications.

## Checklist F-10: Images

- [ ] Every image included in the book has an identified source.
- [ ] Rights and permissions have been reviewed.
- [ ] Original images remain preserved separately.
- [ ] Working images have been prepared for their intended printed size.
- [ ] Resolution and colour requirements match the printer's specifications.
- [ ] Cropping has been reviewed.
- [ ] No important part of an image is accidentally obscured.
- [ ] Captions are accurate.
- [ ] Uncertain identifications remain appropriately qualified.
- [ ] Image placement supports the reading experience.
- [ ] Accessible descriptions have been considered for digital editions.

**Production decision:** Are the images ready for the intended formats and uses?

# Part Eleven: Preparing the Cover

Now we reach one of the most visible parts of production.

The cover.

It's tempting to begin here.

And there's nothing wrong with exploring cover ideas early.

But final production dimensions usually depend on decisions made later.

The trim size.

Binding.

Page count.

Paper.

And chosen printer.

For a printed book, the cover may need to include:

Front cover.

Spine.

Back cover.

And any required bleed or safe areas.

The exact dimensions depend on the provider's specifications.

So don't guess.

## The Spine Problem

Imagine designing a beautiful cover.

The title is centred perfectly on the spine.

Everything looks wonderful on your screen.

Then the printed book arrives.

And the title is slightly off-centre.

Or part of the text disappears into a fold.

That's why the cover needs to be designed against the correct production template.

The spine width may depend on the final page count and paper choice.

If the page count changes, the cover dimensions may also need updating.

## Cover Content

Check:

Title.

Subtitle.

Author name.

Any series information.

Back-cover description.

Images.

Logos.

And any required publisher or distribution marks.

Don't invent endorsements.

Reviews.

Awards.

Or publishing credentials.

And don't automatically place a barcode or identifier in a location without checking the provider's requirements.

## Checklist F-11: Cover

- [ ] The final title is correct.
- [ ] The subtitle is correct, if used.
- [ ] The author name matches the interior.
- [ ] The design suits the intended audience.
- [ ] Image rights have been reviewed.
- [ ] The actual printer's cover template has been obtained.
- [ ] Trim, spine, and bleed dimensions are correct.
- [ ] Important text remains within safe areas.
- [ ] Spine text is appropriate for the available width.
- [ ] Back-cover information is accurate.
- [ ] Any required barcode or identifier area has been accounted for.
- [ ] The cover proof has been inspected.

**Production decision:** Is the cover ready for this specific print edition?

# Part Twelve: Preparing an Ebook

Now let's consider digital reading.

A reflowable ebook is different from a printed PDF.

In a printed document, pages have fixed dimensions.

In a reflowable ebook, readers may adjust:

Text size.

Font.

Screen orientation.

Line spacing.

And other display preferences.

That means the layout must adapt.

A printed page design cannot simply be copied unchanged and expected to behave correctly.

## Structure Matters

The ebook needs a logical document hierarchy.

Chapters.

Sections.

Headings.

And navigation.

A Table of Contents should point to the correct locations.

Images should behave appropriately on different screen sizes.

And content should remain readable when the text is enlarged.

## Accessibility

Accessibility isn't merely a final decorative feature.

The structure should help readers who use assistive technologies.

For example:

Heading hierarchy.

Meaningful image descriptions where needed.

Clear links.

Readable text.

And properly identified language.

The relevant EPUB specifications and accessibility requirements may evolve.

Use appropriate current validation tools and distribution requirements for the edition you're producing.

## Checklist F-12: Ebook

- [ ] The digital edition uses the approved manuscript text.
- [ ] Chapter order is correct.
- [ ] The Table of Contents works.
- [ ] Heading structure is logical.
- [ ] Navigation links work.
- [ ] Images display appropriately.
- [ ] Meaningful images have suitable accessible descriptions where needed.
- [ ] Text remains readable when resized.
- [ ] Unnecessary fixed-layout assumptions have been removed.
- [ ] The ebook has been checked with appropriate validation tools.
- [ ] It has been tested in actual reading software.
- [ ] Accessibility concerns have been reviewed.

**Production decision:** Can readers navigate and understand the ebook across appropriate devices and reading methods?

# Part Thirteen: Preparing an Audiobook

Now let's discuss audio.

Perhaps you want the book narrated.

Maybe in your own voice.

Perhaps by another narrator.

Or using an authorised synthetic voice system.

Those are different approaches.

But all require preparation.

## Start With the Approved Manuscript

The audiobook script should be derived from the approved edition.

Not from an old draft.

And not from an unreviewed collection of interview transcripts.

You may need to adapt certain elements for audio.

For example:

Tables.

Charts.

Footnotes.

Visual illustrations.

And references such as "see the image below."

The narration should remain understandable without relying on visual layout.

## Pronunciation

Create a pronunciation guide.

Especially for:

People's names.

Places.

Technical terms.

Foreign-language expressions.

And unusual words.

Don't allow an automated voice to guess important names without review.

## Narration Rights

If you're recording yourself, you still need to consider the rights attached to the manuscript and any third-party material.

If using someone else's voice, recording, or synthetic voice model, obtain appropriate authorisation for the specific use.

Do not assume possession of a sample recording gives permission to create a synthetic imitation.

## Audio Quality

Listen to the actual output.

Check:

Missing sentences.

Repeated passages.

Mispronunciations.

Volume changes.

Clipped beginnings or endings.

Incorrect chapter order.

And unexpected pauses.

A file that plays is not necessarily a properly reviewed audiobook.

## Checklist F-13: Audiobook

- [ ] The narration script is based on the approved manuscript.
- [ ] Visual-only content has been appropriately adapted.
- [ ] A pronunciation guide has been prepared.
- [ ] The narrator or voice-production method is authorised.
- [ ] All necessary rights have been reviewed.
- [ ] Chapter boundaries are correct.
- [ ] Every intended passage is present.
- [ ] No unintended repetitions occur.
- [ ] Names and important terms are pronounced appropriately.
- [ ] Audio levels and playback quality have been reviewed.
- [ ] Metadata matches the book.
- [ ] The final audio files meet the intended distributor's verified specifications.

**Production decision:** Is the audiobook understandable, complete, and authorised for its intended use?

# Part Fourteen: Proofing the Actual Edition

Now we reach one of the most important production stages.

Proofing.

You've created the files.

They appear ready.

Perhaps the PDF opens.

The EPUB passes a validator.

Or the audiobook has been exported successfully.

Wonderful.

But now you need to inspect the actual reader experience.

Not simply confirm that the software reported success.

This was a central lesson from the BookForge manuscript-editor workflow.

Automated checks are useful.

But they cannot establish every aspect of quality.

## The Printed Proof

If you're preparing a physical book, obtain a proof copy through your chosen printing process when possible.

Hold it.

Open it.

Read several pages.

Inspect the cover.

Examine the photographs.

And check the binding.

Look for problems that may not have been obvious on the computer screen.

Perhaps the text is too small.

The inner margins feel cramped.

Or an image is darker than expected.

Those observations matter.

## The Digital Proof

Open the ebook in more than one appropriate reading environment when practical.

Test navigation.

Text resizing.

Images.

And links.

Don't rely only on the file-export message.

## The Audio Proof

Listen.

Preferably from beginning to end before release, with focused checks of names, chapter boundaries, and other known risk areas.

Automated transcription comparison can help detect certain mismatches.

But it doesn't replace listening for quality and meaning.

## Checklist F-14: Proof Review

- [ ] The proof corresponds to the correct approved manuscript version.
- [ ] The title and author information are correct.
- [ ] Front matter is complete.
- [ ] Every chapter is present.
- [ ] Chapter order is correct.
- [ ] No unexpected blank pages appear.
- [ ] No text is missing or clipped.
- [ ] Page breaks and chapter starts are acceptable.
- [ ] Images and captions are correct.
- [ ] Navigation works where relevant.
- [ ] Typography is comfortable.
- [ ] Edition-specific problems have been recorded.
- [ ] The author has reviewed the proof.
- [ ] All critical defects have been addressed before release.

**Production decision:** Has the actual edition been examined, not merely generated?

# Part Fifteen: The Correction Log

Now imagine the proof reveals several problems.

Perhaps Chapter Four begins on the wrong page.

One photograph appears too dark.

And a person's name is misspelled in a caption.

You need to correct those issues.

But the corrections should remain organised.

Create a simple log.

## Template F-1: Production Correction Log

| Issue ID | Edition | Location | Problem | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FIX-001 | Print | Chapter 4 opening | Incorrect page break | Open |
| FIX-002 | Print | Photograph 3 | Image too dark | Open |
| FIX-003 | All editions | Chapter 6 caption | Name spelling error | Open |

These are fictional examples.

The important thing is distinguishing format-specific problems from source-content problems.

A page break may affect only the print edition.

But a misspelled name may affect the manuscript master, ebook, print edition, and audiobook script.

## Correct the Right Source

This is crucial.

If a factual error appears in the print proof, don't correct only the final PDF while leaving the authoritative manuscript wrong.

Update the approved source through the project's change-control process.

Then regenerate every affected edition.

# Part Sixteen: The Final Release Gate

Now we arrive at the moment when the book may be ready.

Not because the last export succeeded.

Not because the cover looks attractive.

And not because the AI has declared everything perfect.

Because the necessary work has been completed and reviewed.

Let's define the final gate.

## The Five Release Questions

**1. Is the content approved?**

The author has reviewed the intended manuscript.

**2. Are the sources and rights appropriately addressed?**

Relevant factual, privacy, and permission issues have been resolved to the level required for the edition.

**3. Is the production technically sound?**

The files meet the actual requirements of the intended format and provider.

**4. Has the edition been proofed?**

Someone has examined the real output.

**5. Has the author authorised this release?**

The intended audience and distribution method are approved.

If any critical answer is no, the edition may not be ready.

And that's fine.

Release can wait until the necessary issue is resolved.

## Checklist F-16: Release Approval

- [ ] The exact edition is identified.

- [ ] Final cover and interior files are approved.
- [ ] The distribution plan is approved.
- [ ] An authoritative release copy is preserved.
- [ ] The author has explicitly authorised publication or distribution.

**Production decision:** This specific edition is approved for this specific release.

That's the gate.

And it's worth treating seriously.

# Part Twenty-Two: What If Something Goes Wrong After Release?

Now let's consider an ordinary possibility.

The books have been distributed.

And someone notices a mistake.

Perhaps a family member's name is misspelled.

Or a historical detail turns out to be incorrect.

What should you do?

First, establish the actual issue.

Check the source.

Determine what needs correction.

Then decide whether a revised edition is appropriate.

For a small private book, perhaps the correction can be documented for future copies.

For a publicly distributed edition, the process may be more involved.

The appropriate response depends on the significance of the error, the distribution method, and the available correction options.

## Don't Silently Rewrite the Archive

The source material should remain intact.

If a memory was recorded inaccurately in the book, correct the manuscript's treatment of it.

Don't alter the original recording to match the corrected narrative.

Preserve the evidence.

## Record the Edition Change

For example:

**Edition 1.1**

Corrected the spelling of a contributor's name in Chapter Four.

Or:

**Second Edition**

Contains substantive revisions and additional source material.

The exact versioning convention is less important than maintaining a clear record.

**A correction should improve the published work without disguising the history of the change.**

That's the principle.

# Part Twenty-Four: The Most Common Production Mistakes

Let's finish the technical guidance with a few recurring problems.

## Mistake One: Formatting an Unapproved Draft

The manuscript is still changing.

But someone begins final typesetting.

Now every major edit creates additional layout work.

**Better practice:** Establish an approved text baseline before final production.

## Mistake Two: Treating a Successful Export as Proof of Quality

The PDF opens.

Therefore, the book must be finished.

Not necessarily.

**Better practice:** Examine the actual output.

## Mistake Three: Using the Wrong Cover Dimensions

The cover was designed before the final page count and paper choice were established.

**Better practice:** Use the provider's correct final template.

## Mistake Four: Ignoring Photographs Until the End

Images are blurry, incorrectly cropped, or lack appropriate permission.

**Better practice:** Review them during production planning.

## Mistake Five: Allowing Editions to Diverge

The print edition contains one correction.

The ebook contains another.

The audio script uses an older draft.

**Better practice:** Maintain one authoritative content baseline and track changes across formats.

## Mistake Six: Skipping the Physical Proof

Everything looks correct on the computer.

The actual book reveals layout problems.

**Better practice:** Review a proof copy when practical.

## Mistake Seven: Treating Private Sharing as Permission-Free

A family book includes another person's sensitive story without appropriate consideration.

**Better practice:** Review intended use and contributor rights before distribution.

## Mistake Eight: Losing the Production Files

The book was printed.

But nobody remembers which manuscript or cover version was used.

**Better practice:** Archive the released edition and its supporting files.

Those mistakes are avoidable.

Not through a perfect prompt.

But through a dependable process.
