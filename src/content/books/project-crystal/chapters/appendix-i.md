## The Book May Be Finished. The Memories Still Need Looking After.

Imagine finding an old recording.

Perhaps it's on a phone.

An external hard drive.

Or a computer that hasn't been switched on in years.

You recognise the filename.

It's a conversation with your grandfather.

One afternoon, you sat together and talked about his childhood.

He told you stories about work.

His family.

An old car.

And a project that went spectacularly wrong.

At the time, you were collecting material for a book.

You recorded the conversation.

Created a transcript.

And eventually used part of it in a chapter.

The book was finished.

Copies were printed.

And the project moved into the background of everyday life.

Years passed.

Now you've found the recording again.

You open the file.

Press Play.

And hear his voice.

Not a written imitation.

Not a synthetic reconstruction.

The actual recorded conversation.

He laughs.

Pauses.

Corrects himself.

And continues telling the story.

It's a remarkable thing to have preserved.

But imagine a slightly different outcome.

You find the same filename.

Press Play.

And nothing happens.

The file is corrupted.

Or the application that created it no longer works.

Perhaps the recording was stored in an account you can no longer access.

Or the only copy disappeared when an old computer failed.

Now you have a title.

Maybe a transcript.

But not the original recording.

And perhaps you can't recreate it.

That's the problem this appendix is designed to help prevent.

Throughout Project Crystal, we've concentrated on turning genuine memories into books.

We've explored conversations.

Recording.

Source organisation.

AI collaboration.

Writing.

Editing.

And production.

But a finished book is only one part of the larger project.

The underlying memories may have a life beyond that publication.

They may become material for another book.

A family archive.

An oral-history collection.

Or simply something you want to keep.

And those materials deserve appropriate care.

At the same time, some memories are private.

Some involve people who never agreed to public distribution.

And some contain information that shouldn't be stored in every software service you happen to use.

So our final task has two sides.

**Protect the information from people who shouldn't have access to it.**

**Preserve the information so authorised people can still access it in the future.**

Those goals are related.

But they're not identical.

A file can be securely encrypted and still be lost forever.

A recording can be backed up in five places and still be exposed to people who shouldn't hear it.

We need both privacy and preservation.

And we need a method ordinary people can actually maintain.

That's where we're going to finish Project Crystal.

# Part One: Privacy and Preservation Are Different Jobs

Let's begin with two simple questions.

**Privacy asks: Who should be allowed to access or use this material?**

**Preservation asks: Will the material remain available and understandable over time?**

Imagine a private family interview.

You've stored the recording in an encrypted folder.

Only you can open it.

That's useful privacy protection.

But suppose you forget the encryption password.

Or lose the recovery key.

Now nobody can open the file.

The privacy system has become a preservation problem.

Now imagine the opposite.

You upload the recording to several online storage services.

The copies are recoverable.

But the recording includes confidential family information, and you haven't established whether those services are appropriate places to keep it.

You've improved availability.

But perhaps created unnecessary privacy exposure.

So we need to make the decisions together.

Not simply ask whether the file is backed up.

Or whether it is locked.

We need to know:

What the material contains.

Why we're keeping it.

Who may use it.

Where it is stored.

And how it can be recovered.

That's the foundation.

**Preservation without appropriate access control can expose private information. Privacy without a recovery plan can make valuable material permanently inaccessible.**

A dependable archive considers both.

# Part Two: Not Every Memory Needs the Same Protection

Let's imagine your Memory Library contains twenty-five records.

One describes a childhood bicycle repair.

Another concerns a public construction project.

A third contains an interview with a family member.

And a fourth includes a deeply private conversation that was never intended for publication.

Should all four records be handled identically?

Probably not.

They have different sensitivities.

And they may have different permissions.

Let's introduce a simple classification system.

## Category One: Public

Material you've deliberately approved for public distribution, with the necessary rights and permissions addressed.

Perhaps a finished book chapter.

Or an approved photograph.

Public status applies to the approved version, not automatically to every original source behind it.

## Category Two: Shareable With a Defined Group

Material approved for particular people.

For example:

A family-only edition.

A selected collection of stories.

Or an interview excerpt authorised for specific relatives.

This is not the same as unrestricted public access.

## Category Three: Private Working Material

Original notes.

Drafts.

Unpublished recollections.

And recordings intended only for the author's own use.

These should be protected from unnecessary access or disclosure.

## Category Four: Restricted or Sensitive

Material involving confidential information, sensitive third-party details, unresolved permissions, or other reasons for limiting access.

A restricted record may require additional protections.

Or it may need to remain outside AI processing and publication workflows entirely.

## Template I-1: Information Classification

| Classification | Typical Material | General Handling |
|---|---|---|
| Public | Approved published chapter | Distribute within approved scope |
| Limited Sharing | Family-only collection | Restrict to intended recipients |
| Private | Original notes and interviews | Author-controlled access |
| Restricted | Sensitive or permission-limited records | Additional protection and specific approval |

These are suggested categories.

You can rename them.

Or simplify them.

The important thing is knowing that not all material has the same intended audience.

**A memory being included in your archive doesn't mean it's ready for inclusion in your book.**

And a memory appearing in a book doesn't mean its entire original recording should become public.

That's the distinction.

# Part Three: Separate the Source From the Published Story

Now let's consider a practical example.

Suppose you've recorded a conversation with your father.

The recording lasts forty-five minutes.

During that time, he tells a funny story about an old truck.

But the conversation also includes private family matters.

Later, you decide the truck story belongs in your book.

What should you share with your AI writing assistant?

Perhaps a relevant excerpt.

Perhaps a reviewed transcript segment.

Or a Memory Record containing the approved details.

You may not need to provide the entire forty-five-minute recording.

And when publishing the book, you may include only the selected story.

That's an example of **data minimisation**.

Using the information required for the task.

Without unnecessarily distributing the rest.

## The Three-Layer Archive

For Project Crystal, a useful arrangement is:

**Layer One: Original Sources**

Untouched recordings, photographs, letters, and conversations.

Protected according to their sensitivity.

**Layer Two: Working Material**

Reviewed transcripts, Memory Records, source packets, and editorial notes.

Limited to the information needed for the relevant work.

**Layer Three: Approved Publication Material**

Finished chapters, approved images, captions, and production files.

Reviewed for the intended audience.

These layers serve different purposes.

The original source may contain more information than the manuscript requires.

The working material helps organise the source.

And the published material communicates the selected story.

You should be able to move from the publication back toward its source when appropriate.

But access to the publication shouldn't automatically grant access to the private archive.

**Keep the complete source where it belongs. Share only the material authorised and needed for the task.**

That's a useful starting rule.

# Part Four: Permission Is About Use, Not Just Possession

Here's a situation that deserves careful thought.

Someone agrees to tell you a story.

You record it with their permission.

You save the file.

And perhaps they help correct the transcript.

Does that mean you can do anything you like with it?

No.

The person's agreement may have been limited.

Perhaps they approved private recording.

But not public distribution.

Perhaps they agreed to have their words included in a family book.

But not to upload their voice to an online transcription service.

Or perhaps they approved a written excerpt but never considered audiobook production.

Those are separate uses.

## The Permission Questions

For contributed material, distinguish:

**Recording**

Was the recording itself authorised under applicable rules?

**Storage**

Were the participants informed about how and where the material would be kept when relevant?

**AI Processing**

Is the proposed processing appropriate under the contributor's agreement and the service's data-handling arrangements?

**Private Drafting**

May the material be used to develop a working manuscript?

**Family Distribution**

May the selected material be shared with particular relatives?

**Public Publication**

May the content appear in a publicly distributed edition?

**Audio Publication**

May the person's original recording or spoken words be distributed in an audio production?

**Synthetic Voice Use**

Has the person specifically authorised any proposed synthetic voice generation or imitation?

That last category is especially important.

A recording isn't permission to recreate someone's voice synthetically.

And permission for one purpose doesn't automatically establish permission for another.

# Part Five: Be Especially Careful With Other People's Stories

A personal memoir may be written from one person's perspective.

But it rarely involves only one person.

Friends appear.

Relatives.

Coworkers.

Neighbours.

And strangers encountered along the way.

Some stories are harmless.

Others contain details another person might reasonably prefer to keep private.

You need to think about those differences.

Imagine telling a funny story about a childhood friend.

Perhaps you use their first name.

And describe an incident that happened fifty years ago.

That may be straightforward.

Now imagine describing another person's confidential medical experience.

Or presenting an unverified allegation as fact.

Those require different consideration.

The fact that you remember an event doesn't automatically resolve the rights, privacy, accuracy, or fairness concerns associated with publishing it.

## Anonymisation Isn't a Magic Trick

Perhaps you replace someone's name with a pseudonym.

That's sometimes useful.

But think about the surrounding details.

If you describe their exact job, relationship, location, and a highly distinctive event, readers may still identify them.

So review the entire passage.

Not merely the name.

And when a detail isn't essential to the story, consider leaving it out.

**The purpose of responsible editing isn't to erase other people from your life. It's to communicate your experiences without unnecessarily exposing theirs.**

That's the balance.

# Part Six: What Happens When You Use an Online AI Service?

Now let's discuss the technology itself.

Perhaps you've been collecting memories through an AI application.

You type stories.

Upload photographs.

Or provide transcripts for summarisation.

That's convenient.

But it means some information may be processed or stored outside your own device.

Different services have different practices.

They may differ in:

Retention.

Model training settings.

Human review procedures.

Connected applications.

Administrative access.

Data export.

Deletion.

And account recovery.

Those practices can change.

So don't assume one platform's policies apply to another.

## Before Uploading Sensitive Material

Consider these questions.

What information am I sharing?

Does it involve another person?

Is the entire file necessary?

What does the service say about retention and usage?

Can I control whether the material is used for model improvement?

Are there settings governing sharing or connected applications?

Can the information be exported?

What happens if I delete it?

And is this service appropriate for the material's sensitivity?

You don't need to become a cybersecurity specialist to ask those questions.

But they're worth considering.

Especially when the source contains private conversations or irreplaceable recordings.

## An Important Limitation

Deleting a chat or file from an application doesn't necessarily mean every previously created copy, backup, or derivative disappears immediately.

The applicable retention policies and technical behaviour matter.

Likewise, an AI's statement that it won't remember something is not a substitute for understanding the service's actual storage controls.

For sensitive information, rely on documented product controls and appropriate technical safeguards.

Not merely a conversational promise.

**AI instructions can guide how information is handled within a task. They don't replace the platform's security and data-management systems.**

# Part Seven: When Local AI May Be Appropriate

Some people prefer to process their memories on a computer they control.

Perhaps using local transcription software.

Or an AI model operating without sending the material to a remote service.

That can be a useful privacy choice.

But local processing isn't automatically secure.

A local computer can still be compromised.

Files may be copied into cloud-synchronised folders.

Applications may contact external services for updates, telemetry, or processing.

Other people may have access to the machine.

And a poorly configured system may expose services across a network.

So the correct question isn't simply:

"Is the AI local?"

It's:

**Where does the information actually travel, and who can access it?**

If you use local tools for sensitive material, understand their behaviour.

Check relevant network and storage settings.

Use appropriate device protections.

And maintain a dependable backup plan.

For most readers, this appendix isn't asking you to install a local language model.

It's simply identifying local processing as one possible option when the privacy requirements justify it.

A private notebook can be another option.

So can a recorder that never uploads audio.

The method doesn't require one particular technical arrangement.

**Choose the simplest workflow that meets the privacy needs of the material.**

That's the standard.

# Part Eight: Protecting Accounts and Devices

Now let's turn toward everyday security.

Even a carefully organised archive can become vulnerable if the account or computer storing it is compromised.

You don't need to build an elaborate security system for a small family book.

But several basic protections are worth having.

## Use Strong, Unique Authentication

Protect the accounts that hold your archive.

Use strong, unique passwords.

A reputable password manager can help.

Enable multifactor authentication where available and appropriate.

Passkeys may also be useful when supported.

## Keep Devices Updated

Security updates help address known software vulnerabilities.

The exact maintenance process depends on your device and operating system.

## Limit Sharing Permissions

Don't give edit access to everyone simply because you're collaborating on a book.

A contributor may need access to one interview.

An editor may need access to selected manuscript files.

A printer may need the final production PDF.

Those aren't the same access requirements.

## Remove Unnecessary Access

When a collaboration ends, review whether the person or application still needs access.

If not, revoke it where appropriate.

## Protect Portable Devices

Phones, laptops, and removable drives can be lost or stolen.

Consider appropriate device encryption, locking, and secure backup arrangements.

And remember that a protected laptop isn't much help if every sensitive file is also sitting in an unprotected shared folder.

**Give people and applications the access they need for the task, not automatic access to the entire archive.**

That's a practical principle.

And it becomes more important as the project grows.

# Part Nine: The Difference Between a Copy and a Backup

Now let's move to preservation.

Imagine your recordings are stored on your computer.

You copy them to an external drive.

Wonderful.

You have another copy.

But is it a dependable backup?

That depends.

If the external drive remains connected all the time, certain failures or attacks could affect both the computer and the backup.

If the drive is stored in the same location, a fire or flood could damage both.

And if you never test the files, you may discover too late that the copy was incomplete.

So a backup needs more than the existence of another file.

It needs a recovery plan.

## A Common Starting Rule: 3-2-1

A useful backup guideline is commonly described as:

**Three copies of important data.**

**Two types of storage media or otherwise independent storage arrangements.**

**One copy stored off-site or otherwise isolated from the primary environment.**

This is a rule of thumb.

Not a guarantee.

And not every personal project needs the same infrastructure.

But it encourages independent copies.

For example:

Your working files on a computer.

A backup on an external drive.

And another protected copy stored elsewhere.

The exact method depends on the sensitivity of the material and the available resources.

For particularly valuable recordings, an offline or otherwise protected copy can reduce the risk that one incident affects everything.

## Beware of Synchronisation

Suppose your archive synchronises between your laptop and cloud storage.

That's convenient.

But if you accidentally delete a file, the deletion may synchronise.

If a file becomes corrupted, that change may also propagate.

Version history and recovery features vary by service.

So don't assume synchronisation alone provides the backup protection you need.

**A backup should let you recover from mistakes and failures, not merely reproduce them elsewhere.**

# Part Ten: Test the Backup

Now here's the part people often forget.

They create a backup.

Feel reassured.

And never open it.

Months pass.

Then the original drive fails.

They connect the backup.

And discover that the folder is empty.

Or the files are corrupted.

Or they can't remember the encryption password.

That's an unpleasant discovery.

And one worth preventing.

## The Recovery Test

Choose a small sample of archived files.

Perhaps:

One recording.

One photograph.

One transcript.

And the current manuscript.

Recover those files from the backup into a separate test location.

Open them.

Confirm that the contents are readable.

And make sure you're not accidentally opening the original files instead.

For a larger archive, perform more systematic checks.

But even a small recovery test can reveal important problems.

## How Often?

Choose a schedule appropriate to the collection.

For a modest personal archive, an annual review is a practical baseline for checking readability and storage health.

More active or valuable collections may justify more frequent testing.

If a storage device is nearing the end of its useful life, don't wait for a calendar date.

Migrate the data when necessary.

**A backup becomes trustworthy when you can demonstrate that recovery works.**

That's much more useful than simply seeing a reassuring green checkmark.

# Part Eleven: Choosing File Formats That Can Last

Now let's consider another problem.

Technology changes.

Applications disappear.

Devices become obsolete.

And proprietary file formats may become difficult to open.

A recording that worked perfectly when it was created might later require software that no longer exists.

That's one reason digital preservation considers file formats.

## Text

For ordinary notes, plain text and Markdown can be useful.

They don't depend on a particular word processor for basic readability.

But if your document contains complex formatting, tables, or images, a more structured format may be appropriate.

For a book project, keeping an editable manuscript and a readable export can be useful.

## Audio

Keep the original recording in its actual source format.

Don't discard it merely because you've created another version.

For some preservation workflows, an uncompressed or lossless audio copy may also be useful.

But transcoding doesn't recreate information lost in the original recording.

## Photographs

Preserve the original image file.

Keep the highest-quality available version when appropriate.

If you make a crop, restoration, or publication copy, label it separately.

## Documents

PDF can provide a convenient fixed-layout reading copy.

PDF/A is a family of standards designed with long-term document preservation in mind.

But an export to PDF/A should be validated rather than assumed to conform.

And a PDF copy shouldn't necessarily replace the editable source manuscript.

## Video

If your archive includes video, keep the original files.

You may also create widely playable access copies.

But remember that codecs and container formats are different things.

A familiar filename extension doesn't guarantee that every future device will decode the contents.

## A Practical Preservation Principle

Keep the original.

Keep a useful editable or archival derivative when appropriate.

And keep enough information to understand the relationship between them.

**A preservation copy can increase future accessibility. It should not silently replace the original source.**

# Part Twelve: Use Filenames That Explain What You're Looking At

Now imagine opening a folder ten years from today.

It contains:

`recording1.m4a`

`recording2final.m4a`

`newfile.txt`

`finalfinal.pdf`

And:

`IMG_4827.jpg`

Can you identify everything?

Perhaps.

But it's going to take more work than necessary.

A descriptive filename makes the archive easier to understand.

For example:

`2026-11-11_Grandpa_Workshop_Interview01.m4a`

Or:

`MEM-014_Bicycle_Repair.md`

Or:

`Book_Manuscript_v1.3.md`

The exact convention matters less than consistency.

## A Good Filename Often Contains

A date, when known.

A descriptive subject.

A source or memory identifier.

And a meaningful version indicator when relevant.

Avoid unnecessary sensitive information in filenames.

And don't invent dates or identities simply to complete the pattern.

## The Date Problem

A file's creation date isn't always the date an event occurred.

Perhaps you scanned a photograph in 2026.

But the photograph depicts an event from the 1970s.

Those are different dates.

Record them separately.

For example:

**Scan Date:** 2026-11-11

**Photograph Date:** Approximately 1974, not independently verified

That keeps the metadata honest.

**A file's technical timestamp and the historical date of its contents are not necessarily the same thing.**

# Part Fourteen: What Is a Checksum?

Now let's introduce a technical concept that can be useful even for nontechnical readers.

A checksum, or cryptographic hash, is a value calculated from a file's contents.

For example, a tool using the SHA-256 algorithm can produce a distinctive hexadecimal value for a particular file.

If the file changes, the calculated value will usually change.

That means a stored hash can help you detect whether the file's bytes are different from those previously recorded.

This is particularly useful for original recordings and archival files.

## What a Checksum Can Tell You

Suppose you calculate a hash for an original recording.

Later, you calculate it again.

If the values match, that provides strong evidence that the file's contents haven't changed between those checks.

If the values differ, investigate.

Perhaps someone intentionally edited the file.

Or perhaps corruption occurred.

## What a Checksum Cannot Tell You

A checksum doesn't prove that the original recording was accurate.

It doesn't prove that the speaker had permission to be recorded.

It doesn't prove who created the file.

And it doesn't establish that the content is historically true.

It only helps establish byte-level consistency relative to the recorded value.

Also, if someone can modify both the file and its stored checksum, the comparison can be defeated.

So protect the integrity records as appropriate.

## When Should You Use Checksums?

For a small family archive, they're optional but useful.

For a large collection of irreplaceable recordings, they can become an important part of preservation.

You don't need to memorise the algorithm.

Just understand the purpose.

**Checksums help detect change. They do not replace source verification, permission review, or editorial judgment.**

# Part Fifteen: Encryption and the Problem of the Lost Key

Now let's return to privacy.

Perhaps you've decided to encrypt the restricted part of your archive.

That's reasonable.

Encryption can help protect files against unauthorised access.

But it introduces another responsibility.

Someone needs the appropriate key or recovery method to open the material.

Imagine preserving a collection for your grandchildren.

You encrypt everything.

Store the files safely.

And never record a recovery procedure.

Years later, the archive survives.

But nobody can open it.

That's not the outcome you intended.

## A Balanced Plan

For sensitive files, use appropriate encryption.

Protect the credentials or keys.

Maintain a secure recovery method.

And determine whether a trusted person should be able to access the archive under defined circumstances.

Don't store the only recovery password inside the same unprotected folder as the encrypted material.

But don't rely entirely on your own memory either.

The appropriate arrangement depends on the collection's sensitivity and your intended access rules.

A password manager with an established emergency-access arrangement, a securely stored recovery document, or another trustworthy method may be suitable.

The important thing is testing and documenting the plan.

**Security should protect the archive without accidentally making it impossible for its intended custodians to recover.**

# Part Sixteen: Protect the Source Archive From Editing

Here's a useful preservation practice.

Treat original sources differently from working copies.

For example:

An original interview recording should ordinarily remain unchanged.

If you need to improve the sound, work on a copy.

If you need to correct a transcript, create or update a working transcript while preserving the source history.

If you need to crop a photograph for the book, save a separate image.

This protects the original evidence.

You can add appropriate file permissions or other controls to reduce accidental modification.

But remember that marking a file read-only isn't the same as making it secure or immutable.

For a small project, careful folder organisation may be sufficient.

For larger or especially sensitive archives, stronger controls may be justified.

## The Rule of Two Files

At minimum, distinguish:

**Original**

The source as received or recorded.

**Working Copy**

The file you're allowed to edit.

Now you're less likely to destroy the only version.

That's a small habit.

And a very useful one.

# Part Seventeen: What Should You Keep, and What Should You Delete?

Now let's examine a question that rarely receives enough attention.

Does every file need to be preserved forever?

Not necessarily.

Perhaps the archive contains:

Original interviews.

Approved photographs.

Working transcripts.

Temporary audio-processing segments.

Failed exports.

Duplicate files.

And dozens of obsolete drafts.

Some of these may have enduring value.

Others may not.

Keeping everything indefinitely can create privacy and management problems.

But deleting carelessly can destroy irreplaceable evidence.

So we need a retention plan.

## Keep When There Is a Clear Purpose

For example:

Original source recordings you have permission to preserve.

Important transcripts.

Final manuscripts.

Released editions.

Permission records.

And documentation required to understand or defend the history of the work.

## Review Before Deleting

Temporary processing files.

Unnecessary duplicates.

Intermediate exports.

And material no longer needed for the intended project.

## Don't Delete Merely Because a File Is Old

A ten-year-old recording may be far more valuable than a temporary file created yesterday.

Age isn't the deciding factor.

Purpose, rights, sensitivity, and future value matter.

## Remember the Backup Copies

Deleting a file from the working folder doesn't necessarily remove every backup or synchronised copy.

If deletion is required, the relevant retention arrangements need to be considered.

And some records may be subject to legal or contractual retention requirements.

For sensitive or disputed material, don't improvise a deletion policy without understanding the applicable obligations.

**Preservation is about intentional retention, not indiscriminately keeping everything forever.**

# Part Nineteen: What Happens If Someone Withdraws Permission?

Now let's examine a difficult but realistic situation.

A relative agreed to contribute a memory.

You recorded the interview.

Later, they decide they no longer want the story included in a public book.

What happens?

The answer depends on the original agreement, applicable rights, publication status, and circumstances.

You shouldn't assume that consent is legally revocable in every situation or that an already distributed book can be retrieved completely.

But you can respond responsibly.

For example:

Review the contributor's request.

Identify the affected materials.

Determine which uses can be stopped or modified.

Consider whether future editions should exclude the content.

And document the decision.

If relevant legal obligations are unclear, obtain appropriate advice.

## The Important Distinction

Removing something from a working draft may be straightforward.

Removing it from a publicly distributed edition can be much more complicated.

And copies already received by other people may remain outside your control.

That's one reason to clarify permissions before publication.

**It's easier to decide carefully before releasing private material than to retrieve every copy afterward.**

# Part Twenty: Protecting Children and Highly Sensitive Information

Some source material deserves additional care.

Perhaps an interview involves a child.

Or a memory includes medical information.

Financial details.

Private addresses.

Or information that could expose someone to harm.

The purpose of the book may not require those details.

So consider whether they need to be recorded, uploaded, or published at all.

For children's participation, applicable consent and safeguarding requirements may involve parents or guardians, depending on age, location, and circumstances.

For highly sensitive personal information, stronger access restrictions may be appropriate.

And some material may be better excluded from AI processing entirely.

This isn't about treating every memory as dangerous.

# Part Twenty-Four: Avoiding the Synthetic Memory Problem

Imagine you have an old photograph.

# Part Twenty-Five: When an AI Persona Uses Archived Memories

Does it respect restricted materials?

For Project Crystal, the distinction should remain clear.

It shouldn't be presented as the actual person returning to communicate.

Preserve uncertainty.

Respect permissions.

**The purpose is access to preserved evidence, not the creation of a fictional authority over someone else's life.**

# Part Twenty-Eight: How Often Should You Check the Archive?

Digital preservation is not a one-time activity.

Files remain dependent on storage systems, software, formats, and people.

Those things change.

So create a manageable review routine.

For a small personal collection, a periodic check may be enough.

## A Practical Annual Review

Once a year, consider examining:

The archive manifest.

A sample of original recordings.

Important photographs.

Current manuscript files.

Backup health.

Recovery procedures.

Account access.

Encryption-key recovery.

And any changes in contributor permissions.

You might also check whether the storage devices remain reliable and whether the files are still readable with available software.

If a device is becoming obsolete or unreliable, plan a migration.

Don't wait for failure.

## Avoid Constant Unnecessary Recopying

Repeatedly moving entire archives without a clear need can introduce its own risks and consume resources.

Preservation should be proportionate.

You may need to refresh ageing storage media.

Or migrate an obsolete format.

But not every file requires conversion every year.

For larger collections, established digital preservation frameworks can help assess storage, integrity, metadata, and other responsibilities.

For ordinary readers, the basic principle is sufficient.

**Check that the files are still there, still readable, still protected, and still recoverable.**

# Part Thirty-Three: The Privacy and Preservation Audit Prompt

Now let's create the final reusable AI prompt in Project Crystal.

## PROJECT CRYSTAL: PRIVACY AND DIGITAL PRESERVATION AUDITOR

I have a personal memory archive containing source materials, transcripts, photographs, Memory Records, manuscript files, and possibly released book editions.

I want to evaluate its privacy protections and long-term preservation arrangements.

**1. Confirm Actual Access**

Identify which archive records, manifests, and file information you can actually examine.

Do not claim to inspect unavailable storage systems.

**2. Inventory Important Material**

Identify original sources, working derivatives, published editions, and permission records.

**3. Review Classification**

Determine whether material is appropriately classified as public, limited sharing, private, or restricted.

**4. Review Permissions**

Identify missing or unclear permissions for recording, AI processing, publication, and audio use.

Do not invent consent.

**5. Review Data Minimisation**

Identify situations where a task may require only a selected source excerpt rather than the full private archive.

**6. Review Access Controls**

Using the configuration information available, identify potential overexposure or unnecessary access.

Do not claim to verify technical settings you cannot inspect.

**7. Review Backup Arrangements**

Identify how many independent copies exist and whether they are protected against common failures.

**8. Review Recovery Testing**

Determine whether actual restoration has been demonstrated.

Do not treat a backup-status message as proof of recoverability.

**9. Review File Formats**

Identify material that may depend on obsolete or inaccessible software.

**10. Review Source Integrity**

Check whether original and derivative files remain distinguishable.

Review checksum records when available.

**11. Review Retention**

Identify material that may require retention, review, or authorised disposal.

**12. Review Synthetic Media**

Identify generated images, audio, or text that could be mistaken for original historical records.

**13. Review Digital Legacy**

Determine whether an authorised future custodian could understand and recover the archive.

**14. Report Findings**

Separate confirmed defects, possible risks, and matters you could not verify.

**15. Recommend Proportionate Improvements**

Prioritise the simplest controls that address the most important risks.

**16. Do Not Modify, Upload, Share, or Delete Material**

Propose actions for approval before performing any operation affecting the archive.

The goal is a collection that remains appropriately private, understandable, and recoverable.

# Final Quick Reference: The Five Principles of Privacy and Preservation

**1. Preserve the Original**

Keep genuine recordings, documents, and photographs distinguishable from edited and generated material.

**2. Respect the People**

Treat permission to record, process, share, and publish as separate decisions.

**3. Protect Access**

Use appropriate privacy classification, authentication, and access controls.

**4. Make Recovery Possible**

Maintain independent backups, test restoration, and protect encryption recovery information.

**5. Leave the Archive Understandable**

Use a manifest, clear filenames, appropriate metadata, and future-access instructions.

**Preserve what matters, protect what is private, distinguish what is original, and make sure the people authorised to use the archive can understand and recover it.**
