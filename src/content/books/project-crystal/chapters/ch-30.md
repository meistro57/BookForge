## The Book on the Kitchen Table

Imagine a book sitting on your kitchen table.

Not a manuscript.

Not a draft.

Not a collection of notes waiting to be organised.

A finished book.

The cover has been printed.

The photographs are in place.

The chapters have been reviewed.

The stories sound the way you intended.

And you've held the proof copy in your hands.

You've checked everything.

Twice, probably.

Perhaps three times.

Because after spending so long working on something, you develop a rather personal relationship with every page.

You know where the funny stories are.

You remember which chapter caused the most trouble.

You know which photograph was nearly left out.

And you can probably explain why one particular sentence took seven attempts before it finally sounded right.

But now the book is finished.

And something interesting happens.

You realise you've been so focused on creating it that you haven't thought very much about what happens next.

Who gets a copy?

Do you give one to everyone in the family?

Should you ask before sending it?

What about the people who contributed memories?

What if someone disagrees with a story?

What if a family member reads it and remembers something you didn't include?

And what if you simply don't feel ready to share it yet?

Those are the questions we're going to explore.

Because there's a difference between creating a book and deciding how that book enters the world.

**Finishing a manuscript is a creative milestone. Sharing it is a separate, personal decision.**

And you deserve to make that decision deliberately.

Not because a publishing service encourages you to press a button.

Not because an AI insists the project should reach as many people as possible.

And not because every book must become a public product.

Perhaps you've created something intended for only a handful of people.

Perhaps you have a wider audience in mind.

Or perhaps you haven't decided.

All of those possibilities are perfectly reasonable.

Project Crystal has always been about helping you preserve and communicate genuine memories.

Now we're going to consider who should encounter those memories, in what form, and under what conditions.

And perhaps we'll discover something else along the way.

The book may be finished.

But the conversations it inspires might only be beginning.

# The First Question Isn't How to Share

## It's Why

Let's begin with something simple.

Why did you create this book?

Return to your original Book Purpose Statement.

Not the one you think would sound impressive on a back cover.

The actual reason you began.

Perhaps you wanted to preserve stories about your son.

Perhaps you hoped younger family members would have a way to know him through memories.

Maybe you wanted to gather photographs, conversations, and familiar expressions in one place.

Or perhaps you simply realised that the stories were worth writing down before more details disappeared.

Now ask:

**Who would benefit from the book as you intended it?**

That's a much more useful starting point than immediately deciding how to distribute a hundred copies.

Imagine you created the book primarily for family members.

Then your immediate goal may be fulfilled by producing a small private edition.

You don't necessarily need a website.

An online shop.

A marketing campaign.

Or a public announcement.

Perhaps a few carefully chosen copies are enough.

Now imagine your book has developed into something broader.

Maybe it contains a personal story that could interest readers outside the family.

Perhaps it explores a particular period of history, a community, or a shared experience.

In that case, wider distribution may make sense.

But that should be a deliberate expansion of the project's purpose.

Not an automatic consequence of having completed a manuscript.

**The audience should follow the purpose of the book, not the other way around.**

That's our starting principle.

## A Book Doesn't Need an Audience of Thousands

Let's get this out of the way.

A book isn't less successful because only ten people read it.

Imagine those ten people are family members.

One is a grandmother.

Another is a brother.

Perhaps a childhood friend.

And perhaps a grandchild who never had the opportunity to meet the person being remembered.

The book may mean something different to each of them.

One recognises the jokes.

Another remembers the places.

Someone discovers an incident they'd never heard about.

And someone else simply enjoys the stories.

That's a meaningful outcome.

It doesn't require sales figures.

Reviews.

Or public recognition.

A private book can fulfil its purpose without ever appearing in a bookstore.

And a publicly available memoir can be worthwhile even if its audience remains modest.

The point isn't the number of readers.

It's whether the book reaches the people you intended to share it with, in a form they can use.

# Choosing the Circle of Readers

Let's introduce a useful idea.

We'll call it the **Reader Circle**.

It's simply a way to think about the different audiences who might encounter your book.

You don't need to share the same material with everyone.

And you don't have to make every decision immediately.

## The Personal Circle

This may include only you.

Perhaps you've created the book for your own preservation.

You want a printed copy.

Maybe an audiobook.

Perhaps something you can return to privately.

That's enough.

You are allowed to complete a project without distributing it.

And you can change your mind later.

## The Family Circle

This includes the relatives you choose.

Perhaps parents.

Siblings.

Children.

Grandchildren.

Or other family members who have a meaningful connection to the stories.

A private family edition may be appropriate.

But remember that family relationships aren't automatically simple.

Different people may have different preferences about sharing personal material.

And being related doesn't automatically grant someone unlimited access to every recording or recollection in your archive.

The book should contain material appropriate for the intended family audience.

## The Contributor Circle

These are the people who helped create the memory collection.

Perhaps a sister contributed a story.

A childhood friend provided photographs.

Or a neighbour recalled a funny incident.

You may wish to offer them a copy.

But their contribution doesn't automatically mean they want a finished book delivered without warning.

Perhaps they'd appreciate being asked first.

Or perhaps they'd like to review the passages involving them.

Those are reasonable considerations.

## The Wider Circle

This might include friends, community members, or interested readers.

Perhaps people who share an interest in the subject.

Or others who may appreciate the book's experiences.

Now you're moving beyond the original private audience.

That doesn't necessarily make the book unsuitable for sharing.

But it does mean reviewing the material for that broader use.

Some stories may be appropriate.

Others may require changes or additional permissions.

## The Public Circle

This is the broadest audience.

A publicly distributed book.

Potentially available to people you've never met.

Once material becomes public, you may have limited ability to control how it is copied, quoted, reviewed, or discussed.

That doesn't mean public publication is a bad choice.

It means the decision deserves attention.

**The wider the audience, the more carefully you should consider what information the book reveals and what permissions support that use.**

And remember, you don't have to move through every circle.

You can remain in the personal or family circle indefinitely.

That's your decision.

# The Book Can Have More Than One Edition

We've touched on this earlier, but now it becomes particularly practical.

Imagine you've created a book containing twenty chapters.

Some include ordinary family stories.

Others involve sensitive information.

Perhaps several photographs show people who haven't approved public use.

Now you decide to share the book with a wider audience.

Do you need to publish everything?

No.

You can create different editions.

Perhaps:

**A Private Family Edition**

Containing material selected for that audience, with appropriate permissions and reasonable privacy protections.

**A Public Edition**

Containing stories and photographs suitable for wider distribution.

**An Audio Edition**

Using approved narration and material adapted for listening.

The editions may overlap substantially.

But they don't have to be identical.

This is one of the reasons Project Crystal keeps the source archive separate from the finished manuscript.

You can choose what belongs in each edition.

And preserve the broader memory collection without releasing it.

But there's an important warning.

A private edition isn't guaranteed to stay private.

Someone may lend the book.

Photograph a page.

Forward a digital file.

Or share a recording.

So don't include highly sensitive material on the assumption that the words *private family edition* will prevent it from circulating.

Consider the likely consequences if the material is shared beyond the original audience.

That's part of responsible release planning.

# The Release Decision

Before distributing the book, establish which edition you're sharing.

Let's create a simple record.

### Project Crystal Release Record

**Book:** The Stories We Remember

**Edition:** Family Edition, Version 1

**Format:** Printed paperback

**Intended Audience:** Selected family members and invited contributors

**Current Master:** Family-Master-v04

**Print Proof:** Reviewed

**Accuracy Review:** Completed against available sources

**Contributor Permissions:** Confirmed for included material and intended use

**Photograph Rights:** Reviewed for included images

**Outstanding Concerns:** None identified that prevent this planned release

**Approved Distribution:** Limited family copies

**Public Distribution:** Not authorised by this release decision

This is a fictional example.

Now the intended use is clear.

You aren't simply distributing something called *Final Book*.

You're sharing a particular edition.

For a particular audience.

Based on a particular approved master.

And if you later decide to create a public version, that becomes another release decision.

It doesn't automatically inherit every permission or assumption from the family edition.

**A release decision should identify what you're sharing, with whom, and for what intended use.**

That's a small administrative step with substantial practical value.

# The First Reader

## Start With Someone You Trust

Now let's imagine the book is ready.

You've completed the production review.

The edition is appropriate for its intended audience.

And you're considering how to begin sharing it.

You don't necessarily have to distribute every copy at once.

Perhaps you begin with one trusted reader.

Someone who understands the nature of the project.

Maybe a family member who contributed memories.

Or someone whose feedback you value.

You offer them a copy.

Not as an assignment.

Not as a demand for a detailed review.

Simply as an invitation.

You might say:

"I've finished putting the stories together. I'd love to share a copy with you if you'd like one."

That's enough.

You don't need an elaborate speech.

And you don't need to explain every technical step involved in creating the manuscript.

The finished book can stand on its own.

## Don't Turn Reading Into an Obligation

Here's something worth remembering.

A personal book may be meaningful to you.

But other people may have different relationships with the subject.

Perhaps they're excited.

Perhaps they're busy.

Perhaps they're not interested in reading a full manuscript.

Or perhaps the timing isn't right for them.

That doesn't necessarily say anything about the value of your work.

So don't make receiving the book feel like an obligation.

You can offer it.

Allow the person to decide.

And respect their response.

You might also make clear that they don't need to provide feedback.

Some people may simply want to keep the book.

Others may read it gradually.

And some may prefer to wait.

**Sharing a personal story should be an invitation, not a test of how much someone cares.**

That applies whether the book is about someone living, someone who has died, or the author's own experiences.

# How to Introduce the Book

Perhaps you're wondering what to say when handing someone a copy.

You don't need to deliver a formal presentation.

Unless, of course, you'd enjoy one.

Some people do.

And there's nothing wrong with celebrating a completed project.

But for a private family edition, a few simple words may be more appropriate.

For example:

"I've been collecting these stories for quite a while. Some came from my own memories, and others came from conversations with family. I wanted to put them somewhere they could be kept and enjoyed. This is the finished book."

That's straightforward.

It explains what the book is.

Without telling the recipient how they should feel.

You could also include a short personal note.

Perhaps written inside the cover.

Or on a separate card.

But make the message genuinely yours.

Don't let AI manufacture a dramatic declaration simply because it thinks book gifts require emotional language.

A simple explanation can be more effective.

## A Note Inside the Book

Some authors like to include a short note to readers.

It might explain the purpose of the collection.

For example:

"This book brings together memories collected from family conversations, recordings, photographs, and the stories we continued telling over the years.

"Some are funny. Some are ordinary. A few contain details we remember differently.

"They're presented here because they are part of the life we remember, and because we wanted those stories to remain available to the people who come after us."

That's an illustrative note.

Your actual wording should reflect your intentions.

Perhaps you want something humorous.

Or something very short.

Or no note at all.

That's fine.

The book doesn't require a grand introduction.

It requires an appropriate one.

# Sharing the Printed Book

Now let's consider the practical side.

Perhaps you've printed ten copies.

You want to distribute them among family members.

You could hand them over personally.

Mail them.

Or arrange a small gathering.

The method is your choice.

But it's helpful to keep track of which edition was distributed.

Especially if different versions exist.

A simple record might identify:

**Family Edition v1**

Ten copies printed.

Eight distributed.

Two retained.

You don't need to record everyone's reading habits.

And you don't need to monitor what people do with the book.

The record is simply useful for managing editions and possible corrections.

Imagine you later discover an important factual error.

You'll know which version was distributed.

And if appropriate, you can inform recipients or provide a corrected replacement.

That becomes much easier when the files and editions are clearly identified.

## What If Someone Finds a Mistake?

Let's imagine your aunt opens the book.

She notices that a family surname is misspelled.

Or a photograph caption contains the wrong year.

She tells you.

What happens?

First, thank her for identifying the issue.

Then check the underlying source.

Don't change the manuscript automatically.

Perhaps she's correct.

Perhaps the original caption was right.

Or perhaps the date remains uncertain.

Once you've established the correction, record it.

Update the master manuscript.

And preserve the change in the editorial history.

For future copies, use the corrected version.

If the error is significant, you may wish to notify recipients of the earlier edition.

But not every minor typo requires a dramatic announcement.

Use judgment.

**A printed book can be finished without becoming immune to correction.**

That's an important distinction.

# Sharing a Digital Edition

Now let's consider another possibility.

Perhaps you've created a PDF or ebook.

You want to send it to relatives.

That's convenient.

But digital sharing introduces a different set of considerations.

A file can be copied.

Forwarded.

Uploaded elsewhere.

Or stored on devices and services outside your control.

So before distributing a digital edition, consider its contents and intended audience.

For material containing private information, you may prefer a controlled sharing arrangement.

Perhaps an invitation-only folder.

A secure file-sharing service.

Or another method appropriate to the sensitivity of the material.

Some services allow access restrictions.

Some offer link expiration or permission controls.

But those features aren't universal guarantees.

An authorised recipient may still download, photograph, or otherwise copy the contents.

And removing access to a shared link doesn't necessarily remove copies already obtained.

That's why the initial edition review matters.

**Technical restrictions can reduce accidental exposure, but they cannot guarantee that a distributed digital file will remain private.**

The safest approach is to avoid including information that would be seriously harmful if shared beyond the intended circle.

If you need to preserve highly sensitive source material, keep it in the protected archive rather than the distributed edition.

## Make the File Easy to Use

Now let's talk about the reader's experience.

A digital book should be easy to open.

The text should be readable.

The Table of Contents should work where supported.

And photographs should display appropriately.

For a PDF, the pages have a fixed layout.

That may work well on a computer or tablet.

But a small phone screen can make fixed pages difficult to read.

A reflowable ebook can adapt to different screen sizes and text preferences, though image placement and visual design may behave differently.

You don't need to create every digital format.

Choose what suits your readers.

If you're sharing with relatives who primarily use phones, consider whether the file is comfortable on a phone.

If they're using tablets or computers, a PDF may be perfectly suitable.

Test the actual file.

Don't assume that because it opens on your computer, it will be convenient for everyone else.

# Sharing the Audiobook

Now let's consider the listening edition.

Perhaps you've created an audiobook.

Maybe you narrated it yourself.

Or used an authorised synthetic voice.

Perhaps the chapters have been carefully reviewed.

Names are pronounced correctly.

The humour works.

And the audio has passed your listening checks.

How do you share it?

Again, begin with the intended audience.

A private family audiobook may be distributed through a suitable file-sharing arrangement or a listening system your recipients can use.

A public audiobook may require a distributor or platform with its own technical, rights, and synthetic-voice policies.

Those requirements can change.

So verify them before release.

And don't assume that permission to distribute the printed book automatically covers every recording or voice used in the audio edition.

A contributed recording.

A voice performance.

Music.

Or a synthetic voice model may involve separate rights and restrictions.

Review the actual materials.

## Make the Listening Experience Simple

Here's something easy to overlook.

Imagine sending your grandmother a folder containing thirty-seven audio files with names like:

**output_final_ch4_segment03_revisionB.mp3**

She may not be delighted.

So organise the listening edition.

Use clear chapter names.

For example:

**01 - The Things He Tried**

**02 - Afternoons With Grandpa**

**03 - The Family Dog**

And so on.

If the files are intended to play sequentially, make the order obvious.

Include simple listening instructions when necessary.

And test the files on the kinds of devices your recipients use.

The point is to remove unnecessary obstacles.

The recipient shouldn't need to become an audio engineer to hear the memories.

# What Happens When Someone Remembers Another Story?

Now we arrive at one of the most interesting possibilities.

Imagine your uncle receives the book.

He opens it.

Reads the chapter about Grandpa's workshop.

Then calls you.

"I remember something else about that place."

And suddenly, there's another story.

One you hadn't collected.

Perhaps it involves an old tool.

A project.

A familiar expression.

Or a funny argument.

You listen.

And realise the book has prompted a memory.

That's wonderful.

But what do you do with it?

Do you immediately add it to the manuscript?

Not necessarily.

Remember the method we've developed.

Start with the source.

Capture the recollection with appropriate permission.

Preserve the original.

Create a memory record.

Check the details.

And decide whether it belongs in the collection.

The book may already be finished.

That's fine.

The new memory can join the archive without immediately changing the published edition.

**The archive can continue growing even when the book remains unchanged.**

That's an important principle.

And it gives the project a future without turning every new recollection into another emergency revision.

# The Book as a Memory Trigger

Let's explore that idea.

One reason people forget particular memories is that they haven't encountered the right reminder.

A photograph.

A familiar place.

A name.

Or a story told by someone else.

Those things can prompt additional recollections.

A finished book may do the same.

Perhaps a family member reads about a childhood bicycle.

Then remembers another afternoon involving the same bicycle.

Someone reads about the family dog.

And recalls an incident involving a missing sandwich.

Or a friend sees a photograph and recognises the location.

Now the memory collection has gained new information.

This doesn't mean the new recollection is automatically more accurate than the old one.

It still needs its own source record.

But it's valuable.

And the book may have helped bring it forward.

That's a lovely possibility.

Because the finished work can become more than a collection of stories.

It can become an invitation to remember.

But we're going to handle those new contributions carefully.

Not by allowing everyone to overwrite the existing manuscript.

And not by assuming that every new detail belongs in the book.

We're going to preserve the new material.

Then evaluate it.

Just as we did from the beginning.

# Inviting Additional Memories

Perhaps you'd like to encourage family members to contribute more stories after reading.

You can include a short note.

Or simply mention it when sharing the book.

For example:

"If reading this brings back another memory, I'd love to hear it. I'm continuing to preserve the stories, even though this edition is finished."

That's enough.

You don't need to launch a formal collection campaign.

Unless you want to.

Perhaps your family enjoys sharing stories in a group conversation.

Or perhaps one person prefers recording voice notes.

Another might write something down.

Someone else may send a photograph.

Those can all become source material.

But remember to establish the intended use.

If someone sends you a private memory, don't assume they want it published.

If they provide a photograph, don't assume you have the rights needed for wider distribution.

And if they tell you an event differently from the version in the book, preserve the difference.

Don't silently replace the earlier account.

The method remains the same.

**Capture. Organise. Verify. Attribute. Decide.**

We've been using those principles since the beginning.

And they remain useful after the book is finished.

# A Fictional Example: The Book That Started Another Conversation

Let's return to our fictional family.

Ben is the son.

Anna is his sister.

Their mother has completed a private family edition of *The Stories We Remember*.

Everything in this example is fictional.

The book includes the kitchen table museum.

The cardboard ferry.

The biscuit delivery system.

And several other childhood memories.

The mother gives Anna a copy.

Anna reads the museum story.

Then says:

"You know, I remember something else about that day."

The mother asks what she means.

Anna explains that she remembers Ben insisting nobody could touch the exhibits.

That's a detail already present in Anna's original recorded recollection.

But perhaps it wasn't included in the printed family edition.

Now the mother has a decision.

She could leave the book as it is.

The story already works.

Or perhaps she wants to include the detail in a future edition.

If so, she should check the source record and the permissions associated with Anna's contribution.

The information isn't automatically available for every edition simply because Anna mentioned it again.

Now imagine Anna remembers something genuinely new.

Perhaps she says:

"I think there was another sign beside the entrance."

That recollection would need its own review.

What did the sign say?

Is Anna certain?

Does another source support it?

And does it matter to the story?

The mother doesn't need to invent an answer.

She can record the additional recollection as uncertain if that's appropriate.

Then decide whether it's useful.

The first edition remains unchanged.

The archive becomes richer.

And perhaps a later edition will include the new material.

That's a perfectly sensible outcome.

**A new memory doesn't invalidate the completed book. It creates another piece of source material that may inform future work.**

# The First Edition Is Allowed to Be Finished

Here's something important.

Once you begin collecting new memories, there's a temptation to keep revising the book forever.

Someone remembers another story.

You add it.

Then someone sends a photograph.

You add that too.

Another person corrects a date.

Then another anecdote appears.

Before long, the book is no longer finished.

It's back in development.

There may be times when that's exactly what you want.

Perhaps you're intentionally producing an evolving family history.

But perhaps you'd prefer to preserve the first edition as a completed work.

That's reasonable.

You can do both.

Keep the published edition unchanged.

Continue collecting new source material.

Then decide whether a revised edition is worthwhile.

Perhaps:

**Family Edition, First Edition**

And later:

**Family Edition, Revised Edition**

Or:

**Expanded Edition**

The exact labels are your choice.

The important thing is being clear.

A new edition should have its own version record.

And significant factual changes should remain traceable.

You don't need to continually overwrite the past.

You can preserve it.

Then build something new when you're ready.

**The book can reach a stopping point even when the memory collection remains open.**

That is a very useful distinction.

# When a Reader Disagrees With the Book

Now let's return to a situation we touched on in Chapter 26.

Someone reads a story and says:

"That's not what happened."

What should you do?

First, listen.

Perhaps the person has a valid correction.

Perhaps they remember a different version.

Or perhaps they were present for another part of the event.

You don't have to immediately agree.

And you don't have to defend every sentence as though the book were on trial.

The purpose is understanding.

Ask what they remember.

If they're willing, preserve their account with appropriate permission.

Then compare it with the original sources.

Perhaps a date was wrong.

That's a straightforward correction.

Perhaps the disagreement involves an uncertain location.

You may choose to clarify the wording in a future edition.

Or perhaps both accounts remain plausible.

Then you can preserve the difference.

What matters is not treating the newest voice as automatically correct.

Nor treating the printed book as permanently unquestionable.

**A completed book can be reviewed honestly without requiring every disagreement to have one definitive winner.**

That's particularly important in family history.

Different people may remember the same incident differently.

And sometimes those differences are part of the story itself.

# Feedback Isn't a Vote on Your Memories

Here's another important distinction.

Readers may have opinions about the book.

Perhaps someone prefers shorter chapters.

Another wishes there were more photographs.

One person loves the humour.

Someone else prefers the quieter memories.

That's normal.

But not every preference requires a revision.

You don't have to redesign the entire book because one relative prefers a different font.

Or because someone wishes a favourite memory had appeared earlier.

Feedback can be useful.

But the author remains responsible for editorial decisions.

Consider what kind of feedback you're receiving.

**Factual Correction**

This deserves source review.

**Privacy Concern**

This deserves careful consideration, particularly if identifiable people or sensitive material are involved.

**Clarity Concern**

Perhaps the reader couldn't follow a relationship or event.

That's useful information.

**Stylistic Preference**

This may or may not justify a change.

**Additional Memory**

Preserve it as new source material.

These are different categories.

Treating them separately helps prevent confusion.

And it gives you a manageable way to respond without reopening every decision you've already made.

# Sharing Without Creating a Public Campaign

Let's discuss something that may be reassuring.

You don't have to become a promoter.

Perhaps you love creating things.

You enjoy exploring ideas.

You spent months developing a book.

But the thought of posting announcements, asking for reviews, and promoting the project everywhere makes you rather tired.

That's fine.

You can share the book quietly.

Give a copy to someone.

Send a personal message.

Or simply make it available to the people who've expressed interest.

There's no rule requiring you to transform a personal memory project into a marketing exercise.

Now, if you do choose public distribution, you may wish to prepare a description, author information, and an appropriate release plan.

That's a separate decision.

And it can be handled at whatever scale suits your goals.

But Project Crystal doesn't measure success by promotional activity.

The book was created for a reason.

Sharing should serve that reason.

Not replace it.

**You can finish something meaningful without turning it into a public performance.**

That's worth remembering.

# When You Do Choose Public Publication

Now let's consider the other possibility.

Perhaps you've decided to publish the book for a broader audience.

Maybe it contains material that could interest readers beyond the family.

Or perhaps you've written a memoir about your own experiences.

Now you'll need to think about discoverability.

How will readers understand what the book is?

What description will accompany it?

How will they know whether it's a memoir, family history, personal essay collection, or something else?

These are practical publishing questions.

You might prepare:

A short book description.

An author biography.

A cover suitable for its genre and audience.

Accurate publication metadata.

And any information required by the distribution service.

But the content should be truthful.

Don't invent professional credentials.

Don't exaggerate the book's historical significance.

And don't claim the memories have been independently verified if they haven't.

The same standards that guided the manuscript should guide its public description.

## The Book Description Should Make an Honest Promise

Imagine a collection of funny family memories.

An AI might suggest:

"An unforgettable journey through the extraordinary life of a visionary whose genius transformed everyone around him."

That sounds dramatic.

But perhaps the book doesn't make those claims.

And perhaps it shouldn't.

A more accurate description might be:

"A collection of family stories, childhood adventures, familiar places, and everyday memories that bring one person's humour and personality into focus."

That's less dramatic.

But it may be a better match.

Readers should encounter the kind of book the description promises.

And the author should feel comfortable standing behind the words.

**The public description is an invitation into the actual book, not an opportunity to invent a more impressive one.**

# Protecting the Digital Archive After Release

Now let's turn our attention to the source material.

You've shared the book.

Perhaps printed copies are on family shelves.

Perhaps a digital edition has been distributed.

Maybe an audiobook exists.

What happens to the original recordings?

The transcripts?

The photographs?

The editing notes?

The source records?

Those still matter.

In some ways, they may be more valuable than ever.

Because they preserve information that didn't fit into the finished book.

And they provide a way to check or expand the work later.

So don't delete the project archive simply because the book is finished.

Maintain your backups.

Preserve the original recordings.

Keep the approved manuscript versions.

And store important permission and release records.

If the archive contains sensitive information, protect it appropriately.

Consider who should have access.

And whether someone trusted should know how to locate the material if you want it preserved beyond your own use.

But don't automatically share every private file with every recipient of the book.

The archive and the finished edition are different things.

We've said that many times because it remains important.

# A Reusable AI Prompt for Sharing the Book

Let's create the final practical prompt for Part VII.

This one helps you prepare the distribution plan without automatically turning a private project into a public release.

### PROJECT CRYSTAL: BOOK SHARING ASSISTANT

I have completed a personal book based on genuine memories.

I want to plan how to share the finished edition responsibly.

I will provide the book's purpose, intended audience, available formats, and relevant release records.

Follow these instructions.

**1. Confirm the Purpose**

Help me identify why I'm sharing the book and which audience best serves that purpose.

Do not assume the book should be publicly published or commercially promoted.

**2. Identify the Exact Edition**

Distinguish between private family, limited distribution, public, digital, print, and audio editions.

Do not assume permissions transfer automatically between editions.

**3. Review Release Boundaries**

Use the supplied privacy, contributor-permission, photograph-rights, and audio-rights records.

Identify unresolved concerns.

Do not invent consent or release clearance.

**4. Plan the Recipient Experience**

Consider how recipients will receive and use the book.

Help me select practical formats and delivery methods appropriate to the audience.

**5. Prepare Optional Introductory Material**

If requested, help draft a short message, note, or book description.

Keep the language authentic and consistent with my intentions.

Do not manufacture emotional claims or exaggerated descriptions.

**6. Respect Recipient Choice**

Treat sharing as an invitation.

Do not frame reading or responding as an obligation.

**7. Protect Private Material**

Do not suggest publishing the complete source archive.

Distinguish the finished edition from recordings, transcripts, and other private project files.

**8. Support Corrections**

Help me record factual corrections and distinguish them from reader preferences.

Do not automatically overwrite the approved edition.

**9. Support New Memories**

When readers contribute additional stories, help organise them as new source material.

Preserve attribution, uncertainty, and permission status.

**10. Maintain Edition Records**

Help distinguish released editions from drafts and future revisions.

Do not claim files were distributed or publication completed unless those actions actually occurred.

**11. Avoid Unnecessary Promotion**

Do not turn a personal memory project into a marketing campaign unless I explicitly choose that direction.

**12. Preserve the Author's Control**

Present options and recommendations.

Let me make the decisions about audience, timing, formats, and future editions.

The goal is to share the book in a way that honours its purpose, protects personal material, and allows the memories to be encountered appropriately.

Sometimes the appropriate action is to ask whether someone would like a copy.

Then give it to them.

The process should fit the project.

Not the other way around.
