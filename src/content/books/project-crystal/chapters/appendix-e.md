## You Don't Need a Perfect Prompt. You Need the Right Tool for the Job.

Imagine walking into a workshop.

You've got something to build.

A few pieces of material.

A drawing.

And a fairly clear idea of what you're trying to accomplish.

Now imagine someone hands you one enormous tool.

They assure you it can cut, measure, drill, weld, paint, and assemble everything.

All you need to do is press a button.

Sounds convenient.

But perhaps you'd prefer to know exactly what the tool is doing.

Especially when the material is valuable.

And particularly when a mistake could destroy something you can't replace.

Creating a personal book with AI presents a similar challenge.

You can ask an AI system to do nearly everything at once.

Collect memories.

Organise stories.

Create an outline.

Write chapters.

Edit the manuscript.

And prepare the finished book.

But that doesn't mean combining every task into one enormous instruction is the best approach.

Different jobs require different kinds of attention.

Collecting a memory isn't the same as writing a chapter.

Writing a chapter isn't the same as checking its historical accuracy.

And proofreading a manuscript isn't the same as deciding whether someone else's private story should be published.

That's why we're creating a library of specialised prompts.

Each prompt establishes a particular task.

Defines what the AI should accomplish.

And places boundaries around what it should not do.

You can use one prompt at a time.

Combine compatible instructions when appropriate.

Or adapt them to your preferred tools.

But there's one principle running through this entire appendix.

**A prompt is an instruction for a task. It is not a guarantee that the task will be completed correctly.**

The resulting work still needs appropriate review.

And if the AI cannot access the required source material, it must not pretend otherwise.

We've discussed those principles throughout Project Crystal.

Now we're turning them into practical tools.

---

# Part One: How to Use This Library

You don't need to copy the entire appendix into every conversation.

In fact, that would often be unnecessary.

Instead, choose the prompt that matches the job you're doing.

If you're collecting a memory, use a memory interview prompt.

If you're organising transcripts, use a source extraction prompt.

If you're developing the Table of Contents, use a blueprint prompt.

And if you're reviewing a chapter for unsupported details, use a source fidelity audit.

The objective is to give the AI a specific responsibility.

Then examine the result.

## The Five Elements of a Useful Prompt

Most effective writing prompts contain five elements.

**1. The Task**

What are we trying to accomplish?

**2. The Available Material**

What sources, records, or documents should the AI use?

**3. The Boundaries**

What must the AI avoid inventing, changing, or disclosing?

**4. The Output**

What should the result look like?

**5. The Review**

What remains subject to author approval or additional verification?

You don't have to write those five elements formally every time.

But thinking about them helps.

For example:

"Make this better."

That's a vague instruction.

Now compare:

"Review this chapter for repetition and unclear transitions. Preserve the factual details and narrative voice. Suggest specific edits before rewriting anything."

That's a much more controlled task.

The AI knows what to examine.

And what not to change.

**A good prompt reduces ambiguity without making the creative process unnecessarily complicated.**

# Part Two: The Universal Project Crystal Instruction

This is the foundation prompt.

You can place it near the beginning of a new project or incorporate it into your standing project instructions.

It establishes the general boundaries that apply throughout the writing process.

## Prompt E-1: The Source-Grounded Collaborator

**TASK:** Establish an AI-assisted writing partnership.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

You are helping me develop a personal book based on genuine memories, experiences, documents, and other source material.

Your role is to assist with conversation, organisation, writing, editing, and review.

Follow these principles throughout the project.

1. Do not invent personal events, dialogue, dates, places, feelings, thoughts, motivations, or outcomes.
2. Distinguish source-supported facts from recollections, interpretations, hypotheses, and unknown details.
3. Preserve the author's intended voice and meaning.
4. Do not assume that a fluent explanation is factually correct.
5. Identify which source material you can actually access.
6. Never claim to have read, saved, searched, modified, or verified a file unless that action actually occurred.
7. Do not silently change approved manuscript content.
8. Respect privacy, contributor permissions, and publication restrictions.
9. Ask focused questions when genuinely necessary.
10. Prefer clear, manageable steps over unnecessary complexity.
11. Treat generated text as a draft until appropriately reviewed.
12. Keep significant creative and publication decisions with the author.

When important information is missing, identify the gap rather than filling it through imagination.

When you identify a problem, distinguish the observation from your proposed solution.

When you complete a task, describe what was actually accomplished and what remains unresolved.

The goal is a dependable collaboration that helps me communicate genuine material clearly and accurately.

---

Use this as a foundation.

Then select the more specialised prompts below.

You don't need to repeat the entire instruction in every message if it is already available and being followed.

However, for a new conversation or tool, provide the relevant project context again rather than assuming it has been retained.

# Part Three: Beginning a Memory Conversation

Before you have a manuscript, you need material.

These prompts help you explore memories without demanding polished writing.

## Prompt E-2: The First Conversation

**TASK:** Discover why the person wants to create a book.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I am beginning a personal memory-writing project.

I don't have a final title or outline yet.

Help me explore why I want to preserve these memories.

Ask one question at a time.

Let my answers guide the conversation.

Don't assume the book needs to be inspirational, emotional, chronological, or public.

Don't invent details about my life.

When we're finished, summarise the reasons I actually expressed and identify any possible book directions as suggestions rather than decisions.

Do not write chapters yet.

Begin by asking what made me interested in preserving these stories.

---

## Prompt E-3: Explore One Specific Memory

**TASK:** Develop the source account of one event.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I want to describe one specific memory.

Please help me explore it through conversation.

Ask one useful question at a time.

You may ask about the people, setting, objects, actions, and sequence of events.

Do not introduce details as though they must have happened.

If I don't remember something, leave it unknown.

If I estimate a date or age, preserve that uncertainty.

Don't invent dialogue or internal thoughts.

Don't turn the experience into a life lesson unless I choose to reflect on it.

When I say the account is complete, create a plain factual summary with:

- A working title.
- What I remember.
- People involved.
- Place and approximate time, if known.
- Distinctive details.
- Uncertainties.
- Questions worth revisiting.

Do not write a polished narrative chapter yet.

Begin by asking what I remember.

---

## Prompt E-4: Follow the Unexpected Thread

**TASK:** Preserve a memory that appears during another conversation.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I've just remembered something that may be a separate story.

Help me capture it without losing the subject we were discussing.

First, identify the new memory using only what I've said.

Give it a temporary working title.

Record its relationship to the earlier subject, if that relationship is supported.

Don't invent a connection merely because the memories appeared in the same conversation.

Then ask whether I want to explore the new memory now or return to the previous one.

Do not claim the memory has been saved outside this conversation unless a file was actually created.

---

This last prompt is particularly useful when one story leads to several others.

You can preserve the new direction without abandoning the original conversation.

# Part Four: Interviewing Another Person

When collecting someone else's story, the priorities change slightly.

You need to respect the contributor's preferences.

Their account.

Their right not to answer.

And the permissions governing the recording and later use.

## Prompt E-5: The Respectful Interviewer

**TASK:** Guide a memory interview.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I am helping another person preserve their memories.

Please assist me with conversational interview questions.

Begin with open, comfortable subjects.

Ask one question at a time.

Do not assume how the storyteller felt.

Do not impose a particular interpretation on their experiences.

Avoid leading questions.

Accept approximate dates, incomplete memories, and requests to skip subjects.

If several stories emerge, help me identify them separately.

Distinguish what the storyteller personally remembers from stories someone else told them.

Do not treat participation in the interview as permission for publication or AI processing.

At the end, produce a factual interview summary and identify any permission or attribution issues requiring attention.

Do not invent missing information.

---

## Prompt E-6: Follow-Up Interview Planner

**TASK:** Prepare useful questions from an existing source account.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I have a memory account that may benefit from another conversation.

Review the account I provide.

Identify details that are unclear or potentially important to the story.

Prepare up to ten open-ended follow-up questions.

Prioritise questions that would improve source accuracy, chronology, attribution, or understanding.

Do not ask for decorative sensory details merely to make the story more vivid.

Do not presume particular emotions or motivations.

For every proposed question, briefly explain what information it could clarify.

Separate essential questions from optional questions.

Do not answer the questions yourself.

---

This prompt helps prevent the familiar problem of asking more questions simply because there are more questions available.

---

# Part Five: Recording and Transcription

Now suppose the source material is audio.

Perhaps an interview.

A voice note.

Or a long personal conversation.

These prompts help organise the transcription stage.

## Prompt E-7: Transcript Quality Review

**TASK:** Identify transcription problems without inventing missing speech.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I have a transcript from a genuine recording.

Please review it for possible transcription problems.

First, state whether you can access the original audio or only the transcript.

Do not claim to verify spoken words against audio you cannot access.

Flag suspicious names, dates, numbers, technical terms, unclear phrases, and speaker attributions.

Preserve the speaker's corrections and uncertainty.

Do not silently reconstruct missing words.

If a passage cannot be established, mark it for review.

Produce a table containing:

- Source location or available timestamp.
- Suspected problem.
- Reason for concern.
- Recommended verification.
- Current status.

Do not convert the transcript into narrative prose.

---

## Prompt E-8: Readable Transcript Editor

**TASK:** Improve transcript readability while preserving source meaning.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I have a transcript that needs a readable working edition.

Preserve the original transcript unchanged.

Create a separate edited version.

You may improve punctuation, paragraph breaks, and obvious formatting problems.

Do not invent words or remove meaningful corrections.

Preserve uncertain dates, names, and quotations.

Avoid changing the speaker's natural expressions unnecessarily.

Mark passages where the transcript is unclear.

Provide a short edit summary describing the types of changes made.

Do not claim the result is verbatim.

Label it as a readable edited transcript.

---

## Prompt E-9: Long Recording Memory Extractor

**TASK:** Identify distinct recollections in a transcript.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Review the transcript I provide.

Identify distinct memory-bearing sections.

For each section, provide:

- Working memory title.
- Actual speaker or contributor, if identifiable.
- Short factual summary.
- Available timestamp or source location.
- People and places mentioned.
- Important uncertainties.
- Possible related sections.

Do not invent timestamps.

Do not treat every topic shift as a separate event.

Do not merge similar incidents automatically.

If two sections may describe the same memory, flag the possibility for review.

Preserve the full source transcript separately.

---

# Part Six: Building the Memory Library

Once you have several sources, organisation becomes important.

These prompts turn conversations into structured records without turning those records into fiction.

## Prompt E-10: Source Inventory

**TASK:** Determine which original materials exist.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I want to inventory the available source materials for my book project.

Examine only the files or information you can actually access.

Create a Source Register containing:

- Source ID.
- Source title or description.
- Source type.
- Actual date, if established.
- Contributor, if known.
- File or document reference.
- Review status.
- Permission status, if documented.

Do not invent filenames, dates, contributors, or identifiers for records that are not present.

Identify missing expected sources separately.

Do not edit the originals.

---

## Prompt E-11: Memory Record Builder

**TASK:** Create one organised record from a recollection.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Using the source material I provide, create a Memory Record.

Include:

- Memory ID, if supplied.
- Working title.
- Plain factual summary.
- Source references.
- People involved.
- Place and approximate time, if known.
- Details supported by the source.
- Distinctive original expressions.
- Later reflections, clearly separated.
- Uncertainties.
- Related memories.
- Privacy considerations.
- Current manuscript status.

Do not invent missing fields.

Use "Unknown" or "Not established" where appropriate.

Do not convert the record into polished story prose.

---

## Prompt E-12: Duplicate Memory Investigator

**TASK:** Determine whether two records concern the same event.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I have two Memory Records that may describe the same event.

Compare their actual source-supported details.

Examine people, places, chronology, event sequence, and outcomes.

Identify agreements and differences.

Do not assume similar subject matter proves they are duplicates.

Classify the relationship as:

- Likely same event.
- Possibly related.
- Likely separate events.
- Insufficient information.

Explain the evidence supporting the classification.

Do not merge or delete either record.

Recommend specific questions that could resolve important uncertainty.

---

## Prompt E-13: Conflicting Accounts Reviewer

**TASK:** Preserve multiple accounts without manufacturing agreement.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Two or more sources describe an event differently.

Please compare their accounts.

For each disputed detail:

Identify what each source says.

Identify which details are independently supported, if any.

Separate the accounts from interpretations about their reliability.

Do not select a preferred version merely because one sounds more coherent.

Do not invent a compromise account.

Suggest neutral wording suitable for a manuscript when the difference remains unresolved.

Keep the original accounts distinguishable.

---

# Part Seven: Discovering the Book

Now the source collection exists.

But you may not know what kind of book it supports.

These prompts help examine the possibilities.

## Prompt E-14: Find Possible Book Directions

**TASK:** Identify book concepts grounded in the collection.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I have a Memory Collection and want to discover what kind of book it could support.

Review only the records I provide.

Identify recurring subjects, people, places, periods, and activities.

Suggest up to three possible book directions.

For each direction, explain:

- Intended reader.
- Possible central purpose.
- Relevant Memory IDs.
- Potential structure.
- Strengths of the available material.
- Important source gaps.
- Risks of overinterpretation or repetition.

Do not choose the final direction for me.

Do not invent memories to make an idea work.

---

## Prompt E-15: Book Purpose Statement

**TASK:** Define the intended book.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Help me develop a concise Book Purpose Statement.

Use my answers to identify:

- Why I am creating this book.
- Who it is for.
- What I want readers to experience or understand.
- What material the book will mainly contain.
- What the book will not attempt to do.

Ask only the questions needed to clarify these points.

Do not impose a dramatic or inspirational purpose.

Present the completed statement for my review and approval.

---

## Prompt E-16: Compare Three Book Structures

**TASK:** Explore alternative arrangements.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Using my approved Book Purpose Statement and Memory Index, compare three possible ways to organise the material.

Consider chronological, thematic, people-centred, or place-centred structures as appropriate.

For each structure, explain:

- How the reader would move through the book.
- Which Memory Records support it.
- Where source material is strong.
- Where material is missing.
- Potential repetition.
- Chronological or attribution risks.

Recommend the structure that appears best supported by the available material, but distinguish that recommendation from my final decision.

Do not draft chapters yet.

---

# Part Eight: Building the Book Blueprint

Once you've chosen the direction, it's time to create the Table of Contents.

These prompts help connect the outline to actual sources.

## Prompt E-17: Source-Based Table of Contents

**TASK:** Create a working chapter structure.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Using my approved Book Purpose Statement and Memory Collection, propose a working Table of Contents.

Every chapter must have a clear purpose.

For each chapter, provide:

- Number.
- Working title.
- One-sentence Chapter Promise.
- Relevant Memory IDs.
- Main subject.
- Source readiness.
- Information still needed.

Do not create chapters supported only by invented material.

Prefer a manageable number of chapters.

Identify chapters that may need combining or narrowing.

Present the structure for approval.

Do not begin the manuscript yet.

---

## Prompt E-18: Chapter Readiness Audit

**TASK:** Check whether a proposed chapter has sufficient material.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I want to determine whether this proposed chapter is ready to draft.

Review its Chapter Promise and associated Memory Records.

Identify:

- What the available sources establish.
- The intended chapter scope.
- Important missing information.
- Unsupported assumptions.
- Privacy or permission restrictions.
- Potential repetition with other chapters.
- Questions that require clarification.

Classify the chapter as:

- Ready for source-grounded drafting.
- Ready with limited scope.
- Needs additional source work.
- Not suitable for the current book.

Explain the reasoning.

Do not invent missing material to make the chapter appear ready.

---

## Prompt E-19: Chapter Source Packet Builder

**TASK:** Prepare a complete drafting brief.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Using the approved Book Blueprint and available source records, prepare a Chapter Source Packet.

Include:

- Book title and blueprint version.
- Chapter number and title.
- Chapter Promise.
- Intended narrator.
- Writing voice.
- Primary Memory IDs.
- Supporting Source IDs.
- Source-supported factual details.
- Approved direct quotations.
- Important uncertainties.
- Required reader context.
- Privacy restrictions.
- Material that must not be invented.
- Suggested narrative organisation.
- Intended transition or ending.

Do not draft the chapter yet.

Identify any source material you cannot access.

Present the packet for author approval.

---

# Part Nine: Writing the Manuscript

Now we're ready for prose.

These prompts are designed to produce complete drafts while preserving the sources and the author's direction.

## Prompt E-20: Write One Complete Chapter

**TASK:** Draft a chapter from approved material.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Write the complete first draft of the chapter described in the approved Chapter Source Packet.

Use only the available and authorised source material.

Follow the Chapter Promise.

Preserve the intended narrator and voice.

Use genuine events, quotations, and details.

Do not invent dialogue, dates, locations, private thoughts, emotions, motivations, or outcomes.

When information is uncertain, use wording that preserves the uncertainty.

Make the chapter readable and coherent.

Use appropriate transitions without creating unsupported causal claims.

Do not include assistant-to-author commentary within the manuscript.

Do not introduce new chapter topics that belong elsewhere.

Provide the complete chapter, not an outline or summary.

Treat the result as a draft awaiting author review.

---

## Prompt E-21: Continue With the Next Chapter

**TASK:** Support serial manuscript construction.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

We are writing a book one chapter at a time.

Use the current approved Book Blueprint.

Identify the next chapter in the agreed sequence.

Review its Chapter Promise and source packet.

If the necessary source material is available and the chapter is ready, write the complete next chapter.

Do not repeat substantial explanations from earlier chapters unless necessary.

Preserve approved terminology and narrative voice.

Do not alter previously approved chapters.

Do not include conversational preambles inside the manuscript.

Do not invent missing source details.

If the required chapter packet or source material is unavailable, identify the specific missing material instead of pretending you remember it.

At the end, identify the chapter completed and the next chapter in the approved sequence.

---

This prompt is especially useful for the *Next Chapter Please* method.

The important detail is the approved blueprint.

Without it, the AI may guess what comes next.

With it, the writing process has direction.

---

## Prompt E-22: Preserve My Speaking Voice

**TASK:** Revise prose without making the author unrecognisable.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I want this chapter to sound more like my natural storytelling voice.

I will provide approved examples of my speech or writing.

Compare the draft with those examples.

Identify differences involving:

- Sentence rhythm.
- Vocabulary.
- Humour.
- Directness.
- Formality.
- Recurring expressions.
- How I introduce and conclude stories.

Suggest targeted changes.

Do not invent new personal expressions.

Do not exaggerate my personality.

Do not change factual details.

Preserve distinctive wording when it communicates the story effectively.

Provide a revised version and a brief explanation of the main stylistic changes.

---

## Prompt E-23: Write a Natural Transition

**TASK:** Connect two sections without inventing narrative facts.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I have two sections that need a clearer transition.

Review both passages.

Explain what connection the source material actually supports.

Suggest a short transition that helps readers move between them.

Do not invent chronology, causation, shared locations, or emotional significance.

Do not repeat the entire preceding section.

Preserve the author's voice.

Provide up to three options only if meaningfully different.

Recommend the simplest effective transition.

---

# Part Ten: Editing Without Losing the Story

Editing is where specialised prompts become particularly valuable.

Instead of asking the AI to improve everything at once, assign a specific review.

## Prompt E-24: First Complete Editorial Read

**TASK:** Review the manuscript without rewriting it.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I want an independent editorial assessment of this manuscript.

Read the available text as a complete work.

Do not edit anything yet.

Evaluate:

- Overall purpose.
- Reader experience.
- Chapter sequence.
- Repetition.
- Voice consistency.
- Transitions.
- Missing context.
- Unnecessary explanation.
- Structural problems.
- Areas requiring source verification.

Separate major problems from minor suggestions.

For each significant issue, identify the relevant chapter or passage.

Do not invent problems merely to make the report appear thorough.

Provide a prioritised editorial report.

Wait for approval before making changes.

---

## Prompt E-25: Repetition Finder

**TASK:** Identify unnecessary duplication across chapters.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Review the manuscript for repeated explanations, examples, and anecdotes.

Identify passages that substantially duplicate one another.

Distinguish unnecessary repetition from deliberate reinforcement.

Provide:

- Chapters involved.
- Repeated subject.
- Supporting passages or exact locations.
- Whether the repetition is useful.
- Recommended action.

Do not automatically delete repeated material.

Preserve essential context where readers need it.

Suggest the smallest changes that would improve continuity.

---

## Prompt E-26: Conversational Chatter Cleanup

**TASK:** Remove drafting-session artefacts without harming prose.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Review this manuscript for text that belongs to the original AI writing conversation rather than the book.

Examples may include:

Greetings addressed to the author.

Announcements that a chapter is complete.

Instructions about requesting the next chapter.

Unwanted interface references.

And other production commentary.

Distinguish these from legitimate reader-facing conversational prose.

Do not remove words merely because they appear in both categories.

Protect book titles, headings, quotations, names, and approved terminology.

First provide a list of proposed removals with source locations and reasons.

Do not apply changes until approved.

After editing, compare the result with the original and inspect surrounding sentences for damage.

---

## Prompt E-27: Structural Manuscript Audit

**TASK:** Inspect book structure without altering content.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Perform a read-only structural audit of the manuscript.

Check:

- File inventory.
- Chapter numbering.
- Heading hierarchy.
- Part divisions.
- Front matter.
- Appendices.
- Table of Contents.
- Chapter boundaries.
- Repeated title blocks.
- Misplaced transitions.
- Inconsistent closing devices.
- Available production metadata.

Identify confirmed defects separately from recommendations.

Record affected files and locations.

Do not silently rewrite chapters or remove content.

Do not claim the problems have been fixed.

Provide a proposed repair plan requiring author approval.

---

## Prompt E-28: Minimal-Change Revision

**TASK:** Correct a specific passage while preserving everything else.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I want to revise the passage below for a specific reason.

The problem is:

[DESCRIBE THE PROBLEM]

Make the smallest change that solves it.

Preserve:

- Factual details.
- Original meaning.
- Intended voice.
- Approved terminology.
- Surrounding structure.

Do not rewrite unrelated paragraphs.

Show the original and revised passage.

Explain exactly what changed.

Flag any uncertainty that prevents a dependable correction.

---

This is one of the most useful prompts in the entire library.

A precise revision often produces a better result than an uncontrolled whole-chapter rewrite.

---

# Part Eleven: Accuracy and Source Verification

Now we move into a different kind of work.

The following prompts are designed to identify unsupported statements and check claims against available evidence.

They should not be confused with ordinary prose editing.

## Prompt E-29: Source Fidelity Audit

**TASK:** Compare a manuscript passage with its source.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Compare this manuscript passage with the original source material I provide.

Evaluate every significant factual detail.

For each claim, classify it as:

- Directly supported.
- Supported with qualification.
- Interpretation.
- Unsupported by the provided source.
- Contradicted by the source.
- Cannot be assessed from available material.

Identify invented dialogue, precise dates, locations, thoughts, emotions, and causal claims where appropriate.

Quote only the minimum source text necessary to explain the finding.

Do not assume unsupported means false.

Recommend corrections that preserve the intended story while respecting the evidence.

Do not make the changes automatically.

---

## Prompt E-30: Scientific and Historical Claims Register

**TASK:** Identify factual statements that require external verification.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Review this manuscript for scientific, historical, medical, technical, or other externally verifiable claims.

Create a Claims Register containing:

- Claim ID.
- Exact claim or concise paraphrase.
- Manuscript location.
- Type of claim.
- Current supporting source.
- Verification status.
- Recommended action.

Distinguish:

Personal recollections.

Working models or hypotheses.

Metaphors.

Established factual claims.

And unverified statements.

Do not invent citations.

Do not treat an AI-generated explanation as evidence.

If external research is needed, identify that requirement.

If appropriate browsing or source-access tools are available, use authoritative original sources and report what they actually establish.

---

## Prompt E-31: Check One Factual Claim

**TASK:** Investigate a specific claim carefully.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I want to verify the following claim:

[INSERT CLAIM]

First identify what would count as appropriate evidence.

Then examine the sources actually available.

Distinguish primary evidence from secondary summaries.

Report whether the evidence supports, qualifies, contradicts, or fails to establish the claim.

Provide accurate source references when available.

Do not invent studies, quotations, dates, or citations.

If the claim remains unresolved, state that clearly.

Recommend wording suitable for the manuscript that reflects the actual evidence.

---

## Prompt E-32: Preserve Competing Recollections

**TASK:** Write responsibly when sources disagree.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I have conflicting recollections of one event.

Use the source accounts I provide.

Write a short manuscript passage that preserves the disagreement accurately.

Attribute the accounts where appropriate.

Do not combine contradictory details into an invented single version.

Do not declare one account correct without supporting evidence.

Keep the prose natural and readable.

If the disagreement is not relevant to the story's purpose, suggest a neutral way to avoid unsupported precision.

---

# Part Twelve: Privacy, Permissions, and Respect

Some editorial decisions involve more than style or factual accuracy.

These prompts help identify material requiring extra care.

## Prompt E-33: Privacy Review

**TASK:** Flag potential privacy issues before publication.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

Review this draft for potentially sensitive information about living people or private family matters.

Identify:

- Names and identifying details.
- Private conversations.
- Sensitive personal information.
- Third-party allegations.
- Information involving children.
- Material whose intended use may exceed known permissions.

Do not assume every mention requires removal.

Distinguish confirmed restrictions from possible concerns.

Recommend proportionate options such as omission, narrowing, anonymisation, or obtaining appropriate permission.

Do not claim anonymisation guarantees that a person cannot be identified.

Do not publish or share the material.

Provide an author review report.

---

## Prompt E-34: Contributor Permission Checklist

**TASK:** Identify missing permissions for an intended edition.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I am preparing material for the following use:

[DESCRIBE PRIVATE PRINT, FAMILY DISTRIBUTION, PUBLICATION, AUDIOBOOK, OR OTHER USE]

Review the contributor and source records I provide.

Identify which materials have documented permission for that specific use.

Flag unclear, missing, or limited permissions.

Do not treat permission to record as automatic permission to publish.

Do not invent consent.

Provide a list of issues requiring resolution before the intended use.

This is an organisational review, not a substitute for legal advice.

---

# Part Thirteen: Preparing the Finished Book

Once the manuscript has passed the necessary reviews, production can begin.

These prompts help with that stage.

## Prompt E-35: Publication Readiness Audit

**TASK:** Determine which production tasks remain.

### COPYABLE PROMPT

I am preparing a reviewed manuscript for publication.

Assess the available materials.

Check:

- Approved manuscript version.
- Complete chapter inventory.
- Table of Contents.
- Front matter.
- Appendices.
- Heading consistency.
- Factual verification status.
- Privacy and permission review.
- Image and quotation rights.
- Formatting requirements.
- Proofreading status.
- Intended publication format.

Distinguish completed checks from unfinished work.

Do not claim publication readiness merely because a file can be generated.

# Part Twenty: How to Combine Prompts Without Creating Confusion

Sometimes several tasks are closely related.

Perhaps you're reviewing a completed chapter.

You want to check:

Its source fidelity.

Repetition.

And voice.

You could combine those into one review.

But be careful.

If you ask the AI to review and rewrite everything simultaneously, the result may become difficult to audit.

A safer approach is to use stages.

First:

"Run the Source Fidelity Audit."

Then:

"Identify repetition."

Then:

"Review the voice."

Finally:

"Propose a combined correction plan."

Now you can see the different findings.

And decide which changes to approve.

That's a practical way to combine specialised tools.

**Separate the judgments first. Combine the approved corrections afterward.**

This is especially useful for large manuscripts.

Because factual, structural, and stylistic problems should not be treated as interchangeable.

# Part Twenty-One: The Difference Between a Prompt and a Workflow

Let's examine another important distinction.

A prompt is one instruction.

A workflow is a sequence of tasks.

For example:

**Prompt:** "Create a Memory Record."

**Workflow:**

Collect the original account.

Preserve the source.

Create the transcript if needed.

Review important details.

Create the Memory Record.

Check its source references.

Add it to the index.

Those are several steps.

The prompt supports one of them.

The workflow provides the larger structure.

That's why Project Crystal doesn't rely on a single giant instruction.

A large prompt can establish expectations.

But the work still needs to move through appropriate stages.

And each stage may require its own review.

**A prompt tells the AI what to do. A workflow establishes how the results fit together.**

Understanding that distinction can make the entire project easier to manage.

# Part Twenty-Two: What to Do When a Prompt Fails

Now let's consider something realistic.

You use a carefully written prompt.

And the AI still gets something wrong.

Perhaps it invents a detail.

Or overlooks a source.

Maybe it rewrites more than you requested.

What should you do?

First, identify the specific error.

Don't assume the entire collaboration has failed.

Perhaps the problem is local.

You can correct it.

For example:

"You added a date that wasn't in the source. Remove it."

Or:

"That heading was approved. Restore it."

Or:

"You said the file was reviewed, but you haven't shown evidence of accessing it. Please correct the status."

Those are precise corrections.

Then examine whether the problem requires a change to the workflow.

Perhaps the AI repeatedly invents dialogue.

You might add a stronger source-fidelity check before chapter approval.

Perhaps the system keeps selecting the wrong project.

You may need a clearer Project Control Sheet.

Or perhaps a cleanup operation damaged legitimate words.

Then the editing process needs more controlled rules and comparison checks.

This is how the process improves.

Not by endlessly rewriting the prompt in increasingly elaborate language.

But by understanding the failure.

Then introducing an appropriate safeguard.

**When a prompt fails, correct the result and examine whether the process needs improvement.**

That's a more dependable response than simply asking the AI to try harder.

# Part Twenty-Three: Don't Let the Prompt Replace Your Judgment

There's a temptation in AI-assisted work.

When something goes wrong, write a stronger prompt.

Then another.

Then another.

Eventually, the instruction becomes enormous.

It attempts to anticipate every possible mistake.

Every unusual situation.

And every editorial choice.

That can become counterproductive.

The AI may struggle to prioritise conflicting requirements.

The instructions may become difficult for the author to maintain.

And important decisions may disappear inside a wall of rules.

Sometimes the better solution is simpler.

Use a clear source.

A defined task.

And a review step.

For example, instead of writing twenty paragraphs telling the AI not to invent facts, require it to compare the finished passage with the source record.

Now you have an observable check.

The prompt still matters.

But the verification process does more of the work.

**Reliable collaboration comes from good instructions combined with reviewable evidence, not from assuming the perfect prompt can prevent every error.**

That's a central lesson from *The Meistro Manual* and the BookForge editorial workflow.

And it's worth carrying into every future project.

# Part Twenty-Six: The Final Prompt Quality Checklist

Before sending a complicated instruction, review it briefly.

**Is the task clear?**

Does the AI know what you're asking it to accomplish?

**Are the sources identified?**

Does the task depend on material the system can actually access?

Have you explained what must not be invented or changed?

**Is the requested output useful?**

Do you need a chapter, an audit, a summary, or a plan?

**Could the task be simpler?**
