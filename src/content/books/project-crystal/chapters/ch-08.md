## **The Day You Realise You've Collected Something Wonderful**

Imagine opening your computer one afternoon.

Over the past several weeks, you've been recording stories.

Some are only five minutes long.

Others are considerably longer.

You've talked about childhood adventures, family holidays, favourite places, and ordinary afternoons.

You've remembered conversations you hadn't thought about in years.

You've found photographs.

You've spoken with relatives.

You've even discovered a few stories you thought you'd completely forgotten.

Now you look at your collection.

Ten recordings.

Perhaps fifteen.

A few transcripts.

Some photographs.

Several notes.

And you realise something.

**You have the beginnings of a book.**

Not necessarily a manuscript.

Not even a table of contents.

But something equally important.

You have genuine source material.

The stories exist outside your memory now.

They've been captured.

And that means you can begin working with them in ways that weren't possible before.

But there's a small problem.

Your collection is growing.

And although you remember recording a particular story, you can't quite remember which file contains it.

Was it the recording about the family holiday?

Or the one about childhood adventures?

Maybe you mentioned it during a conversation about something completely unrelated.

You open one recording.

Not there.

Another.

Still not there.

Twenty minutes later, you're listening to a story about a birthday cake when you suddenly remember that the incident you're searching for happened during Christmas.

Well, that's helpful.

But surely there's an easier way.

There is.

And that's what we're going to build in this chapter.

**Your Memory Library.**

A simple, dependable system that allows you to preserve your stories, find them again, and eventually work with them as the foundation of your book.

No programming required.

No complicated database.

And no need to become a professional archivist.

We're going to take the material you've already collected and give it enough organisation that both you and your AI can use it effectively.

## **A Collection Is Not Quite the Same as a Library**

Let's begin with a distinction.

Imagine walking into a room containing five hundred books.

They're all there.

Nothing has been lost.

But they're stacked randomly from floor to ceiling.

You remember owning a particular book about gardening.

You know it's somewhere in the room.

Finding it, however, could take hours.

Now imagine those same books arranged on shelves.

Each has a title.

The shelves are organised.

There's a simple catalogue showing where everything belongs.

Suddenly, the collection becomes much more useful.

You haven't acquired any additional books.

You've simply made the existing ones easier to find.

That's the difference between a collection and a library.

A collection preserves material.

A library helps you retrieve and use it.

And retrieval becomes increasingly important as your writing project grows.

Because one day, you may wish to ask questions such as:

"Which memories involve his grandfather?"

"What stories have I told about our family holidays?"

"Where did I talk about his first bicycle?"

"Do I have any recordings about his childhood friends?"

"Have I already told the story about that ridiculous camping trip?"

You shouldn't have to listen through fifty recordings to answer those questions.

And you shouldn't have to rely on the AI remembering everything you've ever said.

Your memory library will provide a more dependable foundation.

## **The First Ten Recordings**

Let's imagine you've collected ten recordings.

They might look something like this:

| Recording | Subject |
| ----- | ----- |
| R001 | Childhood Adventures |
| R002 | Family Holidays |
| R003 | The First Bicycle |
| R004 | Stories About Grandpa |
| R005 | School Memories |
| R006 | The Family Dog |
| R007 | Camping Trips |
| R008 | Favourite Music |
| R009 | Friends and Neighbours |
| R010 | Funny Family Stories |

These are fictional examples, but they illustrate a realistic starting collection.

At first glance, everything looks fairly organised.

Each recording has a subject.

Each has a number.

You can probably find the files without too much trouble.

But here's where things become interesting.

Suppose Recording R002, Family Holidays, contains a story about the family dog.

Recording R004, Stories About Grandpa, includes a camping trip.

And Recording R010, Funny Family Stories, contains another memory involving Grandpa.

Now you have related stories scattered across different recordings.

That's perfectly normal.

It's what happens when people remember things naturally.

The challenge isn't preventing those connections.

It's making them easy to find.

And that's why we're going to introduce a second layer of organisation.

## **Two Different Things: Recordings and Memories**

This distinction is one of the most important concepts in your library.

**A recording is a source.**

**A memory is a story or recollection contained within that source.**

One recording might contain one memory.

Or five.

Or twenty.

A single memory might also be discussed in several different recordings.

We don't want to confuse the two.

Let's look at an example.

Imagine Recording R007 contains a thirty-minute conversation about camping trips.

During the conversation, you discuss three separate events.

First, you remember a family camping holiday.

Then you describe something funny that happened while setting up the tent.

Finally, you begin talking about your son's relationship with his grandfather.

That's three potentially distinct memories.

We might label them:

**MEM-001:** The Family Camping Trip

**MEM-002:** The Tent That Wouldn't Cooperate

**MEM-003:** Fishing With Grandpa

All three point back to Recording R007.

Now imagine that two weeks later, you record another story about fishing with Grandpa.

That second recording might become an additional source for MEM-003 rather than an entirely unrelated memory.

The library allows us to connect the material.

And when we're ready to write a chapter, we can gather everything related to that memory.

That's a powerful capability.

But it's built from something remarkably simple.

Numbers and references.

## **Giving Every Source an Identity**

Let's create a basic naming system.

Each recording receives a unique identifier.

We'll use the letter R followed by three digits.

R001.

R002.

R003.

And so on.

These identifiers refer to recordings, not necessarily to the individual stories inside them.

Your filenames can include both the identifier and a descriptive title.

For example:

**R001 \- 2026-11-10 \- Childhood Adventures.m4a**

**R002 \- 2026-11-12 \- Family Holidays.wav**

**R003 \- 2026-11-15 \- The First Bicycle.mp3**

The identifier stays the same even if you later refine the title.

That makes it easier to maintain references between files.

You can use other letters for different source types if you wish.

For example:

R for recordings.

P for photographs.

D for documents.

C for exported AI conversations.

This is optional.

If you find it unnecessarily complicated, keep things simpler.

The important principle is that each original source should be identifiable.

You want to be able to say:

"This story came from Recording R007."

Then actually find Recording R007.

That's the entire purpose.

## **Giving Individual Memories Their Own Identity**

Now let's create identifiers for the memories themselves.

We'll use MEM followed by three digits.

MEM-001.

MEM-002.

MEM-003.

These labels identify individual stories or recollections.

Let's imagine you have a memory about your son's first bicycle.

You create a record called:

**MEM-004 \- The First Bicycle**

Inside the record, you identify the original source.

Perhaps the story came from R003.

You might write:

**Source:** R003, beginning approximately 00:02:15.

That timestamp points to a location within the recording.

Now, whenever you want to revisit the story, you know exactly where to look.

And if the memory appears in another recording, you can add a second source.

For example:

**Additional Source:** R009, approximately 00:14:30.

The memory record now connects both accounts.

You can compare them.

Identify additional details.

Notice differences.

And decide how the material should eventually be presented.

You don't need to merge the recordings or delete repeated stories.

The original sources remain intact.

We're simply building connections between them.

## **Why Timestamps Can Be So Useful**

Let's discuss that little number we just introduced.

00:14:30.

That's a timestamp.

It means fourteen minutes and thirty seconds into the recording.

Imagine you have an hour-long conversation.

Somewhere in the middle, you told a wonderful story about a family holiday.

Three months later, you decide to revisit it.

Without a timestamp, you might need to search through the entire recording.

With a timestamp, you can go directly to the relevant section.

Not every memory needs a timestamp immediately.

If you have a short recording containing one story, the filename may be sufficient.

But for longer recordings, timestamps can save considerable time.

Some transcription tools produce timestamps automatically.

Others allow you to add them manually.

When possible, preserve them.

And if an AI supplies timestamps, verify that they actually correspond to the recording.

Don't assume they're accurate simply because they look precise.

A timestamp that points to the wrong section isn't particularly helpful.

It's just confusion with excellent formatting.

## **Building the Master Memory Index**

Now we're ready to introduce the most useful document in the entire library.

The Master Memory Index.

Don't let the name intimidate you.

It's simply a list of your memories.

You can create it in a spreadsheet.

You can use an ordinary text document.

You can even begin with a notebook.

The format isn't as important as the information it contains.

Let's imagine a simple index.

| Memory ID | Title | Main Source | Related Subject |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| MEM-001 | The Camping Trip | R007 | Family holidays |
| MEM-002 | The Tent Incident | R007 | Funny stories |
| MEM-003 | Fishing With Grandpa | R007 | Family relationships |
| MEM-004 | The First Bicycle | R003 | Childhood |
| MEM-005 | The Missing Shoe | R010 | Everyday life |

Now look at what we've accomplished.

We can find a memory by its title.

We can identify the recording it came from.

We can see a possible subject or theme.

And if we later decide to write a chapter about childhood adventures, we can look for relevant memories in the index.

Your index might eventually contain additional columns.

Approximate event date.

People involved.

Location.

Status.

Possible chapter.

Privacy restrictions.

But you don't need all those fields on the first day.

Start with the essentials.

Add complexity only when it solves a real problem.

**A useful index is one you'll actually maintain.**

A beautiful, elaborate index that becomes too frustrating to update won't help very much.

## **The Memory Record: Where a Story Gets Its Own Page**

The index tells us what memories exist.

The individual memory record contains the details.

Think of the index as a catalogue.

The memory record is the file you open when you want to examine a particular story.

Let's create one.

The following example is fictional.

---

### **Memory Record: MEM-004**

**Title:** The First Bicycle

**Date Recorded:** November 15, 2026

**Approximate Event Date:** Childhood, exact year unknown

**Original Source:** Recording R003

**Relevant Audio:** 00:01:20 to 00:08:45

**People Mentioned:** Son, mother, grandfather

**Location:** Family driveway

**What Happened:**

The storyteller remembers her son learning to ride a red bicycle. He was determined to try without assistance. His grandfather offered to help, but the boy insisted on doing it himself.

**Distinctive Details:**

* The bicycle was red.  
* His grandfather was present.  
* He repeatedly refused assistance.  
* The storyteller remembers his determination.

**Original Expression Worth Preserving:**

"Once that boy made up his mind, there was no changing it."

**Uncertain Details:**

* Exact age.  
* Exact year.  
* Whether the bicycle was a birthday present.

**Possible Connections:**

* Childhood adventures.  
* Learning new skills.  
* Relationship with Grandpa.  
* Determination.

**Privacy or Publication Notes:**

No restrictions recorded yet. Publication decisions remain open.

**Review Status:**

Reviewed by storyteller.

---

That's a memory record.

Notice that we're not trying to create beautiful prose.

We're preserving information.

And we're distinguishing what was actually remembered from what might become a useful interpretation later.

For example, the story may suggest determination.

But perhaps the storyteller eventually decides that stubbornness is a more accurate description.

That's her choice.

The memory record can be updated while the original source remains unchanged.

## **The Importance of Knowing Where Information Came From**

Let's pause here.

Because this is one of the greatest differences between a dependable AI-assisted book and one that slowly becomes filled with invented material.

**Every important story should be traceable to a source.**

Imagine you're reviewing a draft chapter.

The AI writes:

"His grandfather taught him to ride a bicycle on a warm summer afternoon."

It sounds lovely.

But you pause.

Did you actually say his grandfather taught him?

You remember saying Grandpa was present.

That's not necessarily the same thing.

And did you mention summer?

You don't think so.

Now imagine your manuscript includes a source reference.

MEM-004.

You open that memory record.

It points to Recording R003.

You listen to the relevant section.

And you discover what you actually said.

Grandpa offered to help.

Your son insisted on trying without assistance.

You never mentioned the weather.

Now you can correct the chapter.

This is why source tracking matters.

Not because we want to turn a personal memoir into an academic research paper.

But because memories deserve accurate representation.

And as a manuscript grows, it's surprisingly easy for unsupported details to slip into polished writing.

The source library gives you a way to catch them.

## **Three Categories That Should Never Be Confused**

As you collect information, you'll encounter three different kinds of material.

Let's keep them separate.

### **1\. What Was Remembered or Reported**

These are details someone actually shared.

For example:

"He had a red bicycle."

"Grandpa was there."

"He wanted to ride without help."

Those are statements from a source.

They may still require verification if historical certainty matters, but we know someone actually reported them.

### **2\. What Has Been Independently Supported**

Perhaps you find a photograph of the red bicycle.

Now you have additional evidence supporting that detail.

Or perhaps a dated letter confirms when the family moved to a particular house.

That provides documentary support for a date.

The memory record can note the additional source.

### **3\. What Has Been Suggested or Interpreted**

Perhaps the AI says:

"This story may illustrate his determination."

That's an interpretation.

It might be useful.

It might even be accurate.

But it isn't the same thing as an independently verified fact.

We can record interpretations in a separate section.

Then you can decide whether they're meaningful.

This distinction is particularly important when the AI begins identifying patterns across many stories.

We don't want possibilities silently becoming biographical facts.

And we don't want an attractive interpretation to replace the storyteller's actual understanding.

## **What If Two Memories Contradict Each Other?**

Let's imagine something that happens frequently in family storytelling.

You remember a camping trip taking place in 2004\.

Your brother insists it happened in 2005\.

Then someone finds an old photograph with a handwritten note suggesting 2003\.

Well.

That's helpful, isn't it?

Three dates.

One camping trip.

Now what?

First, don't panic.

You don't have to solve every discrepancy immediately.

Instead, record the different accounts.

For example:

**Mother's Recollection:** Summer 2004\.

**Brother's Recollection:** Summer 2005\.

**Photograph Annotation:** 2003, not independently confirmed.

**Current Status:** Event date uncertain.

Now you've preserved the available information.

Later, you might find another source.

Perhaps an old email.

A travel receipt.

A digital photograph with reliable metadata.

Or another family member who remembers the event.

If the evidence remains inconclusive, the manuscript can acknowledge the uncertainty.

You might simply write:

"One summer, during a family camping trip..."

The exact year may not be necessary to tell the story.

What's important is that the AI shouldn't arbitrarily choose a date because the paragraph seems to need one.

**When the evidence is uncertain, the writing should not pretend otherwise.**

## **Your Library Doesn't Need to Be Perfect**

Now I want to prevent something before it happens.

You've learned about recordings.

Memory numbers.

Indexes.

Source references.

Timestamps.

Individual memory records.

And perhaps you're beginning to think:

"This sounds like a lot of work."

It can become a lot of work if we make the mistake of trying to organise everything perfectly from the beginning.

But we're not going to do that.

Remember why Project Crystal exists.

To make storytelling more accessible.

Not to replace the challenge of writing a book with the challenge of managing a miniature national archive.

So let's introduce an important rule.

**Organise only as much as you need to make the next stage easier.**

If you have three recordings, descriptive filenames may be enough.

If you have ten recordings containing twenty stories, a memory index becomes useful.

If you have a hundred recordings, transcripts and source references become increasingly valuable.

The organisation should grow alongside the collection.

You don't need a complicated system before you've recorded your first memory.

And you don't need to spend three hours cataloguing a five-minute story.

Use the tools that help.

Ignore unnecessary complexity.

We can always improve the system later.

## **Letting AI Help With the Organisation**

Here's where your conversational writing partner becomes particularly useful.

Imagine you've recorded ten conversations.

You've transcribed them.

And now you have dozens of pages of text.

You could read every transcript and manually identify each memory.

That's a perfectly valid approach.

But an AI can help with the initial organisation.

For example, you can provide one corrected transcript and ask:

"Please identify the distinct memories or stories in this transcript. Give each one a short descriptive title, summarise the events using only the information provided, and identify any names, places, dates, or uncertainties. Do not invent details or turn the material into chapters."

The AI may produce a useful list.

You review it.

Perhaps it identifies four stories.

You decide that two belong together.

You correct a misunderstood name.

You remove an interpretation that wasn't supported.

Now you have a reliable working list.

The AI has reduced the mechanical effort.

You have maintained editorial control.

This is an important part of the Meistro Method.

Mark doesn't always perform every organisational task manually.

He uses AI to help manage complexity.

But the output still needs to be evaluated.

And for a personal memoir, accuracy remains especially important.

## **Don't Give the AI More Than It Can Reliably Handle**

Here's another useful lesson.

You may eventually accumulate hundreds of pages of transcripts.

It can be tempting to upload everything into one conversation and say:

"Here. Remember all of this forever."

Unfortunately, that isn't a reliable plan.

Different AI services have limits on how much information they can process at once.

Some can work with very large documents.

Others may struggle with lengthy inputs.

Even when a file is accepted, that doesn't guarantee every detail will remain available throughout a long conversation.

That's why we use the library.

Instead of depending on the AI to retain the entire collection, we bring relevant material into each working session.

Suppose you're developing a chapter about childhood adventures.

You don't necessarily need every recording in the archive.

You may only need the memory records and transcripts connected to that subject.

Those become your working sources.

The AI can analyse them.

Help identify connections.

Suggest a structure.

And eventually assist with drafting.

But the permanent originals remain in your library.

This is a much more dependable approach than treating one enormous AI conversation as the entire project.

**The library holds the material. The AI helps you work with selected portions of it.**

## **Creating a Working Source Packet**

Let's introduce another useful idea.

A source packet is simply a small collection of material gathered for a particular task.

Imagine you're beginning to explore a possible chapter about childhood adventures.

Your memory index identifies six related stories.

Perhaps they involve:

* The first bicycle.  
* Camping trips.  
* Adventures with the family dog.  
* Building things with Grandpa.  
* Exploring the woods.  
* A memorable school project.

You gather the six memory records.

You include the relevant transcript passages.

You make sure the source references are intact.

Now you have a small source packet.

You can give that packet to your AI and say:

"Please examine these six memories and suggest possible ways to organise them into a coherent chapter. Do not write the chapter yet. Identify recurring subjects, possible transitions, and any missing information."

The AI can work with the material.

You can review its suggestions.

And if you decide to write the chapter later, the relevant sources are already assembled.

This is a simplified version of how more sophisticated research and writing systems operate.

But you don't need sophisticated software to benefit from the principle.

A carefully prepared document containing six memory records can be enough.

## **How One Story Can Belong to Several Subjects**

Let's return to the first bicycle.

At first glance, it seems like a childhood memory.

So you might label it:

**Childhood.**

But perhaps the story also involves Grandpa.

Now it belongs under family relationships too.

And perhaps the storyteller associates the experience with her son's determination.

That's another possible theme.

Does this mean we need three copies of the memory?

No.

We keep one memory record.

Then we give it several descriptive tags.

For example:

**Tags:** Childhood, Grandpa, Bicycle, Determination.

Tags are simply keywords that help you find related material.

You can use them in your memory index.

Later, you can search for all memories tagged "Grandpa."

Or all memories involving "Childhood."

Or all memories involving "Cars."

This is surprisingly powerful.

Because a single memory can contribute to several possible chapters without being duplicated throughout the archive.

And when you eventually choose where it belongs in the manuscript, you can make that decision based on the overall structure.

For now, the tags simply keep your options open.

## **Be Careful With AI-Generated Tags**

There is one small complication.

An AI may be tempted to assign personality traits based on limited information.

For example, you tell a story about your son fixing a bicycle.

The AI tags it:

"Engineering Genius."

That's a rather enthusiastic interpretation.

Perhaps your son was mechanically talented.

Perhaps he was simply trying to get his bicycle working.

We don't know.

A more grounded tag might be:

"Bicycle Repair."

Or:

"Mechanical Interests."

And even those should reflect the actual memory.

Tags should help you retrieve material.

They shouldn't quietly turn ordinary experiences into exaggerated claims.

This is another place where reviewing the AI's suggestions matters.

You decide whether the labels accurately describe the stories.

## **Making the Library Searchable**

Once you've created transcripts and memory records, finding information becomes much easier.

Instead of listening through every recording, you can search the text.

Most computers allow you to search filenames.

Many document applications allow you to search within documents.

Spreadsheets can be filtered by subject, name, or date.

And AI can help examine collections of text that you deliberately provide.

Let's imagine you want to find every story involving the family dog.

You might search your memory index for:

"Dog."

Perhaps five records appear.

You open those records.

Each points to its original source.

Now you can gather the relevant stories without searching through the entire archive manually.

This may seem like a small improvement.

But as your collection grows, it becomes enormously valuable.

And it can lead to new discoveries.

Perhaps you hadn't realised how frequently the dog appeared in your stories.

Now you notice that several memories involve your son caring for animals.

You might explore that further.

Or perhaps the stories are simply funny and unrelated.

That's fine too.

The library helps you find possible connections.

It doesn't require every connection to become a profound interpretation.

## **A Simple Weekly Library Routine**

Let's imagine you've begun recording regularly.

Not every day.

Not according to a strict schedule.

Just whenever you feel comfortable.

How do you keep the collection organised without turning it into another demanding responsibility?

I suggest a simple review routine.

Perhaps once a week.

Or after several recording sessions.

Whatever works for you.

During that review, you might do five things.

**First:** Transfer any new recordings into your memory library.

**Second:** Give them descriptive filenames and unique source identifiers.

**Third:** Transcribe the recordings you wish to work with.

**Fourth:** Create or update memory records and your index.

**Fifth:** Back up the collection.

That's it.

You don't have to write chapters.

You don't have to analyse every pattern.

You don't have to make decisions about the finished book.

You're simply maintaining the collection.

And if you skip a week, nothing terrible happens.

You can return when you're ready.

The purpose is to prevent organisational work from accumulating into an overwhelming task.

A little maintenance can save a great deal of confusion later.

## **Keeping Backups Separate**

Let's revisit something important from Chapter 4\.

Your library should not exist in only one place.

Imagine you've collected six months of recordings.

You have transcripts, photographs, memory notes, and a growing manuscript.

Then your computer fails.

Or your phone is lost.

Or you accidentally delete an important folder.

Without a backup, that material may be difficult or impossible to recover.

That's why we maintain independent copies.

A useful principle is to have multiple copies stored in more than one location, with at least one copy protected from problems affecting your main device.

You might keep your main library on your computer.

An encrypted backup on an external drive.

And another protected copy using a cloud service, if you're comfortable with its privacy arrangements.

The exact arrangement depends on your equipment, budget, and preferences.

The important thing is making sure the backups are genuinely independent.

A folder copied to another location on the same failing hard drive isn't much protection.

And synchronised cloud storage may reproduce accidental deletions unless it provides suitable recovery or version-history features.

Every so often, test your backups.

Open a recording.

Read a transcript.

Confirm that the files are actually there.

A backup isn't useful merely because you remember creating it.

It needs to be recoverable.

## **Protecting Private and Sensitive Stories**

As your library grows, you may collect material that is deeply personal.

Private conversations.

Family disagreements.

Medical experiences.

Stories involving living relatives.

Memories that you aren't sure you wish to publish.

Perhaps you record something simply because you need to express it, not because it belongs in the finished book.

That's perfectly acceptable.

But it introduces an important distinction.

**Preserved does not mean approved for publication.**

You can keep a memory in your private archive without allowing it to appear in a manuscript.

One simple approach is to add a field to each memory record.

**Publication Status.**

For example:

Private.

Undecided.

Approved for Drafting.

Approved for Publication.

These statuses have different meanings.

A memory marked Private stays in your personal collection.

Undecided means you haven't made a decision.

Approved for Drafting means it may be used in working manuscript material.

Approved for Publication means you've reviewed the relevant content and are comfortable including it in the intended edition, subject to any other permissions or legal considerations.

You don't have to use exactly these labels.

The purpose is to create a clear distinction between collecting material and sharing it.

And if you collaborate with someone who helps organise or edit the book, they shouldn't automatically receive access to every private recording.

You can provide only the material relevant to their task.

Your library is yours.

You decide who sees it.

## **When Other People Contribute Memories**

Perhaps a friend shares a story about your son.

Or a sibling recalls something that happened during childhood.

Maybe a grandparent has an old photograph with a handwritten note.

These contributions can become valuable parts of the collection.

But they should have their own source information.

Imagine a memory record containing two accounts.

**Source One:** Mother's recollection, Recording R012.

**Source Two:** Childhood friend's recollection, Recording R018.

Now we know where each detail came from.

And if the accounts differ, we can preserve those differences.

You should also record any restrictions the contributor places on their material.

Perhaps they're happy for you to use the story privately but haven't agreed to public publication.

Perhaps they don't wish to be identified by name.

Perhaps they shared a photograph but asked that it not appear online.

Those preferences deserve attention.

You can preserve contributions without assuming unrestricted permission to use them.

The more carefully you manage these details now, the easier the final editorial process will become.

## **What If You Discover a Mistake?**

Let's imagine you've already created a memory record.

Everything seems accurate.

Then you find an old photograph that contradicts one of the details.

Perhaps the bicycle wasn't red after all.

It was blue.

Now what?

First, don't overwrite or destroy the original recollection.

That original record documents what you remembered at the time.

Instead, update the memory record.

For example:

**Original Recollection:** Red bicycle.

**New Evidence:** Photograph appears to show a blue bicycle.

**Status:** Colour requires confirmation.

Perhaps the photograph resolves the question.

Perhaps the lighting makes the colour uncertain.

Perhaps it was a different bicycle.

You can investigate as much as you wish.

The important thing is preserving the distinction between the original memory and later evidence.

This is useful for another reason.

As the book develops, you may refine your understanding of particular events.

Being able to see those changes helps prevent confusion.

And if the AI has already used an outdated detail in a draft, you can identify and correct it.

## **The Library Is Allowed to Change**

Let's make something clear.

The organisational system we're creating isn't sacred.

You can improve it.

You can add categories.

You can rename folders.

You can create new indexes.

You can move from simple documents to a spreadsheet.

You can eventually adopt specialised software if your collection becomes large enough to justify it.

Mark's own creative and technical systems evolved through experimentation.

He didn't begin every project knowing exactly what the final architecture would look like.

He built something.

Used it.

Discovered limitations.

And improved it.

We're borrowing that principle.

Your first memory library should be simple.

Then you use it.

If a problem appears, solve that problem.

Don't build complicated features just because they sound impressive.

And don't worry about creating a system that could manage a million memories when you've collected twelve.

The right amount of organisation is the amount that makes your actual work easier.

## **Your First Ten-Recording Library Exercise**

Now let's put everything together.

For this exercise, imagine you have ten recordings.

If you already have ten, use your real collection.

If you have fewer, that's perfectly fine.

You can practise with whatever material you've collected.

### **Step One: Gather the Originals**

Make sure your recordings are saved in the Original Recordings folder.

Don't delete the files from your recording device until you've verified the transferred copies and created an appropriate backup.

### **Step Two: Assign Recording Numbers**

Give each recording a unique identifier.

R001.

R002.

R003.

And so on.

Use descriptive filenames that include the recording date when known.

### **Step Three: Create or Review Transcripts**

For recordings you wish to organise, create transcripts.

Review important names, dates, and expressions.

Preserve the original audio and distinguish uncorrected transcripts from reviewed versions.

### **Step Four: Identify Individual Memories**

Take one transcript at a time.

Ask your AI to identify the distinct stories it contains.

Review the results.

Decide which stories deserve separate memory records.

### **Step Five: Assign Memory Numbers**

Create unique identifiers for the memories.

MEM-001.

MEM-002.

MEM-003.

Continue as your collection grows.

Remember that the memory numbers don't need to match the recording numbers.

One recording can contain several memories.

### **Step Six: Create the Master Index**

Start with four columns:

Memory ID.

Memory Title.

Original Source.

Main Subject.

Add other information only when it becomes useful.

### **Step Seven: Create Individual Memory Records**

For each memory, preserve the important details.

Include the original source.

Record uncertainties.

Keep interpretations separate from remembered facts.

### **Step Eight: Identify Possible Connections**

Look for recurring names, places, objects, and subjects.

You may begin noticing themes.

Record them as possibilities.

Don't force them into chapters yet.

### **Step Nine: Review Accuracy**

Check the AI's summaries against the transcripts.

Return to the audio when necessary.

Correct invented or misunderstood details.

### **Step Ten: Back Up the Library**

Create an independent backup.

Verify that important files can be opened.

And then stop.

You've completed the exercise.

You now have the beginnings of an organised memory library.

Not a finished book.

Not a perfect historical archive.

But a dependable collection that can grow with your project.
