## **A Place for Your Stories to Live**

Imagine something for a moment.

You've spent several months recording memories.

You've talked about childhood adventures, family holidays, unforgettable conversations, and countless little moments that made someone special.

You've laughed while remembering things you'd almost forgotten.

You've found photographs that brought entire afternoons back to life.

You've even started imagining what some of those memories might look like as chapters in a book.

Then one afternoon, you decide to revisit a particular story.

You remember telling it.

You remember approximately when.

You even remember something wonderful that came up during the conversation.

But you can't find it.

Was it in ChatGPT?

Did you record it on your phone?

Was it saved in a document somewhere?

Did you send it to someone in a text message?

Or was it in that folder you created three months ago and promptly forgot about?

Suddenly, you're hunting through devices, applications, and files.

That's not how we want this experience to feel.

We want you to spend your time remembering and telling stories, not conducting archaeological expeditions through your computer.

So before our collection grows too large, we're going to establish something simple.

A home for your memories.

Not an elaborate technical system.

Not a complicated database.

Just a comfortable, private place where your stories can be collected, organised, and found again.

Think of it as your own personal library.

And the most important thing about this library is that **it belongs to you.**

## **Your Workspace Doesn't Have to Look Like an Office**

When people hear the words "writing workspace," they often imagine a desk.

A computer.

A notebook.

A perfectly organised room.

Maybe a cup of coffee sitting beside an inspirational quotation.

There's nothing wrong with that arrangement.

But it's certainly not required.

Your writing workspace might be your favourite chair.

A quiet corner of your home.

Your kitchen table.

A place outside where you enjoy sitting.

Or anywhere you feel comfortable speaking.

Remember, Project Crystal begins with conversation.

And conversations don't require a formal environment.

You might find that certain places naturally bring back memories.

Sitting in the garden could remind you of summer afternoons.

Looking through a photo album might bring back stories you'd forgotten.

Hearing a familiar song might remind you of a particular drive.

Even ordinary household objects can trigger unexpected recollections.

You don't necessarily need to schedule a formal writing session every time something comes to mind.

You might simply reach for your phone and record a few sentences.

We'll eventually organise those recordings.

For now, the important thing is making the act of capturing a memory easy enough that you'll actually do it.

Because an elaborate system that you never use isn't nearly as valuable as a simple one that fits naturally into your life.

**Your workspace should accommodate your life, not require you to reorganise your life around it.**

## **Two Different Kinds of Workspace**

For this project, we're going to establish two separate spaces.

The first is where you explore memories.

The second is where you preserve them.

These might both exist on the same computer, but they serve different purposes.

### **Your Conversation Space**

This is wherever you feel comfortable talking or writing.

It might be a conversational AI application.

It might be a handheld recorder.

It might be the voice recording application on your phone.

Or it might be a notebook you keep beside your bed.

The conversation space is where the material originates.

It doesn't need to be organised perfectly.

You can speak freely.

You can jump between subjects.

You can change your mind.

You can stop in the middle of a story.

This is your creative space.

### **Your Memory Library**

This is where the material goes afterward.

Your memory library holds your recordings, transcripts, notes, photographs, and eventually your manuscript.

Unlike the conversation space, the library should follow a simple, consistent organisational system.

Not because creativity needs to be controlled.

But because you should be able to find your memories again.

Here's the distinction I want you to remember:

**Your conversation space is where stories are discovered. Your memory library is where they are preserved.**

Once you understand that difference, the entire process becomes easier to manage.

## **Why We Don't Rely on AI Memory Alone**

Let's return to something we discussed earlier.

An AI can often refer to information from earlier in a conversation.

Some applications also offer memory features that retain certain details across conversations.

That can be useful.

But it isn't the same as having a complete, reliable archive.

An AI may not retain every word you've said.

Older conversations may become difficult to access.

A long discussion may exceed the amount of information the AI can use at one time.

A saved memory may preserve only a short summary.

And sometimes an AI can confuse details or produce an answer that sounds like a recollection but is actually a guess.

This doesn't mean you shouldn't use AI.

It simply means we need to understand its role.

Imagine telling an AI a wonderful story about your son's childhood.

Months later, you ask it to recall that conversation.

It remembers part of the story but confuses the location.

You correct it.

Then you discover it has mixed details from two different memories.

Now imagine you still have the original recording.

You can listen to exactly what you said.

The details are there.

Your voice is there.

The original telling hasn't been altered by later interpretations.

That's why we preserve our source material separately.

The AI can help us find, understand, and organise information.

But the originals remain the foundation.

**We don't want the only surviving version of a precious memory to be an AI-generated summary of it.**

## **Let's Build Your Memory Library**

We're going to create a very simple folder system.

A folder is just a place on your computer or storage device where files are kept together.

If you've ever organised photographs into folders for different holidays or years, you already understand the basic idea.

Start by creating one main folder.

You can name it whatever you like.

For our demonstration, we'll call it:

**My Story Project**

Inside that folder, create six smaller folders.

\<textual-example\>

MY STORY PROJECT

01 \- Original Recordings

02 \- Transcripts

03 \- Memory Notes

04 \- Photos and Keepsakes

05 \- Book Manuscript

06 \- Backups and Exports

\</textual-example\>

That's our starting structure.

Nothing complicated.

Let's examine what belongs in each one.

### **Folder 01: Original Recordings**

This is where your audio files live.

If you record yourself telling a story, save a copy here.

If someone else shares a recording with you, and they've agreed to its inclusion in your collection, keep that here too.

These recordings are source material.

Treat them with care.

We won't edit or replace the originals.

If a recording contains background noise, we can create a cleaned-up copy later.

If a recording is difficult to understand, we can make a transcript and correct it.

But the original recording stays intact.

That way, you always have a record of what was actually said.

### **Folder 02: Transcripts**

A transcript is a written version of spoken words.

When you convert an audio recording into text, save the result here.

Transcription tools aren't perfect.

They can misunderstand names, punctuation, unusual expressions, and words spoken quietly.

That's why we'll review important transcripts before using them in the book.

You may eventually have two versions of a transcript.

An initial automatic transcription.

And a corrected version.

Keep both when practical, clearly labelled so you know which is which.

The corrected transcript becomes a useful working reference.

The original audio remains available whenever something needs to be checked.

### **Folder 03: Memory Notes**

This is where we'll keep organised records of the memories themselves.

For example, suppose you record a twenty-minute conversation about several childhood holidays.

The transcript contains everything you said.

But later, you may wish to identify the individual stories within it.

An AI can help create a memory note containing the names, places, events, distinctive details, and unanswered questions.

That note belongs here.

Over time, these notes make it easier to find and organise memories without repeatedly reading every transcript.

We'll learn how to create them in Chapter 9\.

### **Folder 04: Photos and Keepsakes**

This folder is for supporting material.

Scanned photographs.

Pictures of meaningful objects.

Copies of letters.

Old cards.

Programs from special events.

School projects.

Other documents that help preserve a memory.

You don't need to scan your entire family history before beginning.

Add things as they become relevant.

Whenever possible, include a note explaining who appears in a photograph, when it was taken, or why it's meaningful.

If you aren't sure, say so.

A photograph labelled "Possibly summer 2004" is better than one given an invented date.

And remember that photographs can include other people's private information.

We can preserve them without automatically deciding they'll appear in the published book.

### **Folder 05: Book Manuscript**

This folder will become important later.

For now, it might remain completely empty.

That's perfectly fine.

Eventually, it will contain chapter drafts, revisions, approved chapters, and the complete manuscript.

Keeping the writing separate from the original memories helps us maintain a very important distinction.

The transcript records what was shared.

The manuscript presents that material as a story.

They are related, but they are not identical.

If something in the manuscript seems questionable, we can return to the original source.

That's how we protect accuracy.

### **Folder 06: Backups and Exports**

This folder can hold dated exports, indexes, and backup records.

However, there's one important detail.

A copy stored inside the same main folder, on the same device, does not protect you if that device fails or is lost.

A reliable backup needs to exist somewhere separate.

We'll discuss that shortly.

For now, think of this folder as the place to organise backup-related files, not as a substitute for an independent backup.

## **A Simple Naming System**

Now that we've created a place to store our memories, let's make the files easy to recognise.

Imagine opening your recordings folder and finding these names:

Recording1

Recording2

New Recording

New Recording Final

New Recording Final 2

Untitled

Audio 17

You can probably see the problem.

A few recordings are manageable.

A hundred recordings with names like those would become frustrating.

Instead, we're going to use a simple naming pattern.

**Date \- Short Description**

For example:

2026-11-10 \- Childhood Camping Trips

2026-11-12 \- The Birthday Cake Story

2026-11-15 \- Memories of Grandma's House

Beginning with the year, month, and day keeps the recordings in chronological order when sorted by filename.

You don't have to use exactly this format.

The important thing is consistency.

And if you don't remember when an event occurred, don't confuse the date of the recording with the date of the memory.

For example:

2026-11-10 \- Childhood Camping Trips

means you recorded the story on November 10, 2026\.

It does not mean the camping trip happened that day.

The approximate date of the actual event can be recorded separately.

That simple distinction will prevent considerable confusion later.

## **Giving Each Memory Its Own Identity**

As your collection grows, we can introduce one additional organisational tool.

A memory number.

For example:

MEM-001

MEM-002

MEM-003

Each individual memory receives a unique number.

Let's imagine that a recording contains three separate stories.

The recording might be named:

2026-11-10 \- Childhood Memories

Inside that recording, we identify:

MEM-001: The First Bicycle

MEM-002: The Missing Fishing Pole

MEM-003: The Afternoon at Grandma's

Now we can refer to those memories individually without losing the connection to their original recording.

Later, when we begin constructing chapters, we can identify exactly which memories support each passage.

This is a simplified version of source tracking.

It may sound a little technical, but the idea is straightforward.

Every important memory has a label.

Every label points back to the source.

And every source can be found again.

You don't need to number everything immediately.

We'll introduce this as your collection grows.

For now, just understand why it can be useful.

**A book becomes easier to build when we can trace its stories back to where they came from.**

## **Your First Memory Record**

Let's look at what an organised memory might contain.

Remember, this is only an example. The details are fictional.

---

**Memory ID:** MEM-001

**Title:** The First Bicycle

**Date Recorded:** November 10, 2026

**Approximate Event Date:** Childhood, exact year unknown

**People Mentioned:** Son, mother, grandfather

**Location:** Family driveway

**Summary:**

The storyteller remembers her son receiving his first bicycle. He was excited to try riding it and insisted that he didn't need help. His grandfather stayed nearby while he practised.

**Distinctive Details:**

* The bicycle was red.  
* He insisted he could ride without assistance.  
* His grandfather was present.  
* The memory includes a funny incident involving the driveway.

**Uncertain Details:**

* Exact year.  
* His age at the time.  
* Whether the bicycle was a birthday gift.

**Possible Connections:**

* Childhood adventures.  
* His relationship with his grandfather.  
* Learning new skills.

**Original Source:**

2026-11-10 \- The First Bicycle.m4a

---

Notice what's happening here.

The memory hasn't been turned into a polished story.

We haven't invented dialogue.

We haven't described anyone's emotions beyond what the storyteller provided.

We've simply organised the available information.

Now imagine having fifty records like this.

You could search them by subject.

You could identify memories involving the grandfather.

You could find stories about childhood adventures.

You could begin recognising possible chapter themes.

That's what a good memory library makes possible.

And you don't have to create every record manually.

Later, we'll teach your AI how to help produce these records from your transcripts.

You simply review them for accuracy.

## **Keeping Your Original Words**

There's something especially important about preserving personal memories.

Sometimes the way a story is told is just as valuable as the information it contains.

Imagine saying:

"He was forever dragging home some ridiculous contraption. Half the time I didn't even know what the thing was supposed to do."

An AI might summarise that as:

"He frequently brought home unusual mechanical objects."

Technically, that captures part of the meaning.

But the original sentence has personality.

You can hear the storyteller.

You can almost imagine the expression on their face.

That original wording might be exactly what belongs in the eventual book.

If we only preserve summaries, those distinctive expressions can disappear.

That's another reason we keep the transcripts and recordings.

The memory notes help us find material.

The original words help us preserve the storyteller's voice.

And when we begin writing chapters, we can choose which phrases deserve to remain exactly as they were spoken.

This is particularly useful when preserving humour.

A funny story can lose its charm when everything is rewritten into formal language.

Sometimes the best editorial decision is to leave a perfectly good sentence alone.

## **What About Photographs?**

Photographs can become a wonderful part of this process.

A single image may bring back details that haven't crossed your mind in years.

Perhaps you find an old photograph of a family gathering.

You recognise the room.

You remember what everyone was wearing.

You notice something in the background.

Suddenly, an entire story returns.

You can record yourself describing the photograph.

Explain who was there.

What was happening.

What you remember about the occasion.

And why that particular picture matters to you.

The photograph becomes a memory prompt.

The recording becomes the story.

Later, the two can be connected in your library.

You might have:

Photo 014 \- Family Picnic

And:

MEM-027 \- The Picnic Where Everything Went Wrong

Now the image and the story belong together.

But remember something important.

A photograph can show what was visible at a particular moment.

It cannot necessarily tell us what everyone was thinking or feeling.

An AI may suggest an interpretation based on the image.

That doesn't make the interpretation a fact.

You remain the person who supplies the context.

And if you don't know something, it's perfectly acceptable to leave it unknown.

## **Privacy: Deciding What You're Comfortable Sharing**

Now let's talk about something particularly important.

When you're collecting stories about real people, especially family members, some of the information may be deeply personal.

You may discuss relationships.

Private conversations.

Medical experiences.

Family disagreements.

Sensitive events.

Or memories involving people who are still living.

Not every story needs to be shared with an online AI service.

And not every story you collect needs to appear in the finished book.

These are two separate decisions.

First, you decide what you're comfortable providing to the software.

Later, you decide what belongs in a manuscript.

You always have the option to keep certain material private.

### **Understanding Online AI Services**

Many conversational AI applications operate through online services.

When you submit information, it may be processed on the provider's systems.

Different services have different policies for storing conversations, using information to improve their products, retaining data, and managing privacy.

Some offer settings that limit certain uses of your conversations.

Others provide temporary chats or different data controls.

These options can change.

Before sharing sensitive material, review the current privacy information for the service you're using.

Look for answers to questions such as:

* Are conversations saved?  
* Can I export my conversations?  
* Can I delete conversations?  
* Are there settings controlling whether my data is used for model training?  
* What happens to recordings or uploaded documents?  
* Can anyone else access or share the information?

Don't assume that paying for a subscription automatically makes everything private.

And don't assume that deleting a conversation necessarily erases every copy immediately.

The provider's actual policies matter.

### **You Can Change Names**

If you wish to explore a sensitive memory without identifying everyone involved, you can use temporary names or labels.

For example:

"My cousin"

"A childhood friend"

"Family Member A"

You can maintain a private reference document that explains who those labels represent.

That information can remain outside the AI service.

This approach won't eliminate every privacy concern, because stories themselves can contain identifying details.

But it can reduce unnecessary exposure.

### **You Can Keep Some Material Offline**

You can also choose to work with certain memories without uploading them to an online service.

You might record them privately and store them locally.

You could write them in a notebook.

You could decide to include only a general summary in your AI conversations.

Some people may eventually explore locally operated transcription or AI software.

That can offer additional control, although it also introduces technical responsibilities.

You don't need to build a sophisticated offline system to begin.

The important point is that you have choices.

**Your memories don't become less valuable simply because you decide to keep some of them private.**

## **Recording Other People's Stories**

As your project develops, family members or friends may wish to contribute memories.

That can be wonderful.

Someone else may remember an event differently.

They may have stories you've never heard.

They may possess photographs, letters, or recordings that add another perspective.

But before recording another person, ask for their permission.

Recording laws differ by location, and consent requirements can vary.

Beyond the legal considerations, it's simply respectful to explain what you're doing.

Tell them that you're collecting stories for a possible book.

Explain whether you intend to record the conversation.

Let them know how the recording may be used.

And avoid promising that a story will appear in the finished book before you've discussed that decision.

You may also discover that some people are comfortable sharing memories privately but don't wish to be quoted publicly.

Respect that distinction.

A person can contribute to your understanding without giving permission for every detail to be published.

This is particularly important when dealing with sensitive family experiences.

Your book should not become a reason to disregard someone else's privacy.

We'll return to these questions during the editing and publishing stages.

For now, establish a simple habit.

**Ask before recording others, and clarify how their contributions may be used.**

## **Backups: Protecting the Collection**

Let's return to our memory library.

You've created your folders.

You've saved several recordings.

You've begun collecting transcripts.

Everything seems to be going well.

Now we need to protect that work.

Computers fail.

Phones get lost.

Storage devices stop working.

Files are accidentally deleted.

And sometimes a perfectly ordinary software mistake creates a surprisingly large problem.

Mark has spent years working with computer systems, and one lesson from that world is particularly relevant here.

If something is important, you don't rely on a single copy.

### **The Simple Backup Approach**

You don't need an elaborate technical system.

Start with two separate places to store your collection.

For example:

**Your main copy:** On your computer.

**Your backup copy:** On an external storage device that you keep somewhere safe.

Another option is a reputable cloud storage service, provided you're comfortable with its privacy and security arrangements.

For especially important collections, a third independent copy offers additional protection.

The essential idea is that the copies should not all depend on the same device.

If your computer fails, the backup should still exist.

If you accidentally delete a folder, you should have a way to recover it.

And if you use cloud storage, remember that synchronisation is not always the same as backup. Some services can synchronise accidental deletions or unwanted changes across devices.

Version history or a separate backup can help protect against that.

### **Make Backups Part of Your Routine**

You don't have to think about backups every five minutes.

Choose a simple routine.

Perhaps every week.

Perhaps after each substantial recording session.

Copy the new material to your backup location.

Occasionally, open a file from the backup and make sure it works.

A backup you have never tested is less reassuring than one you've actually checked.

And if a particular recording is especially meaningful, consider preserving an additional copy.

There's no prize for having the most complicated backup system.

The goal is simply to avoid losing material that matters to you.

## **Passwords and Access**

Because your collection may contain private family information, take a few basic precautions.

Use a strong, unique password for your AI account and any storage services you rely on.

Enable multifactor authentication where available.

Avoid sharing account passwords.

If several people will help with the book, consider how to give them access only to the material they need.

You might eventually work with an editor.

A family member.

A designer.

Or someone helping you prepare the book for printing.

That doesn't mean they need access to every original recording or private conversation.

You can create copies of approved material specifically for collaboration.

Keep your private archive separate.

This becomes increasingly useful as the project develops.

It allows you to share the manuscript without automatically sharing the entire history behind it.

## **The Difference Between an Archive and a Manuscript**

Let's pause here, because this distinction will become increasingly important.

Your archive and your book are not the same thing.

The archive contains the raw material.

The book contains the material you've selected, organised, and approved for readers.

Your archive may include stories that never appear in the book.

It may contain unfinished conversations.

Conflicting recollections.

Private thoughts.

Photographs you don't wish to publish.

Questions you haven't answered.

And memories that simply don't fit the structure of the manuscript.

That's perfectly normal.

A book is a curated presentation.

An archive is a collection.

The archive doesn't need to become public.

And the book doesn't need to contain everything.

Imagine collecting two hundred memories.

You may eventually decide that only eighty belong in the manuscript.

The remaining memories haven't become worthless.

They're still part of your collection.

They may be meaningful to you.

They may inspire another book.

They may become material for a private family edition.

Or they may simply remain preserved.

That's one of the benefits of keeping the source material separate.

**A finished book is one way to share a collection of memories. It doesn't have to be the only way.**

## **Giving Yourself Permission to Stop**

There's another kind of workspace we haven't discussed yet.

Your emotional workspace.

When you're writing about someone you love, especially someone you've lost, the experience may change from day to day.

One afternoon, you might enjoy spending an hour remembering funny stories.

Another day, even opening the folder may feel difficult.

You don't need to explain or justify that difference.

The process should be flexible enough to accommodate it.

There is no requirement to record a particular number of memories each week.

No word-count target you must satisfy.

No obligation to finish a chapter simply because you started it.

If a conversation becomes uncomfortable, you can stop.

If you're speaking with an AI, you can say:

"I'd like to leave this here for today."

Or:

"Please save a summary of what we've discussed, but don't ask me any more questions."

If you prefer not to create a summary, that's fine too.

You can simply close the conversation.

The material can remain unfinished.

And you can return to it whenever you choose.

This isn't about avoiding difficult subjects forever.

It's about retaining control over when and how you approach them.

An AI can help organise memories, but it cannot determine what you're emotionally ready to discuss.

You are the person who makes that decision.

## **Your First Workspace in Fifteen Minutes**

Let's bring everything together.

You don't need to implement every suggestion in this chapter immediately.

We'll start small.

Here is a practical exercise.

### **Step One: Choose Your Main Device**

Decide where you'd like to keep your primary memory collection.

This might be a desktop computer, laptop, or another device you regularly use.

Choose something familiar and accessible.

### **Step Two: Create Your Main Folder**

Create a folder named:

**My Story Project**

Or choose another name that feels right to you.

This is the home for your collection.

### **Step Three: Create Your Six Subfolders**

Add the folders we introduced earlier:

01 \- Original Recordings

02 \- Transcripts

03 \- Memory Notes

04 \- Photos and Keepsakes

05 \- Book Manuscript

06 \- Backups and Exports

Don't worry if some remain empty.

They'll become useful as the project grows.

### **Step Four: Add One Memory**

Choose a recording, note, or written memory you already have.

Copy it into the appropriate folder.

Give it a descriptive filename.

If you have no existing material, you can create a short recording about something ordinary.

This is simply a practice exercise.

### **Step Five: Choose a Backup Location**

Decide where you'll maintain a separate copy.

It might be an external drive or a storage service you trust.

Copy your new project folder there.

Make sure you can open the copied file.

### **Step Six: Make a Simple Project Note**

Create a document called:

**START HERE**

Inside it, write something like:

"This folder contains my personal story collection. Original recordings are stored in Folder 01\. Transcripts are stored in Folder 02\. Memory notes are stored in Folder 03\. The manuscript will eventually be stored in Folder 05."

You can also record where your independent backups are kept, without putting passwords in the document.

This small note will make the system easier to understand if you return after a long break or ask someone trusted to help you.

And that's it.

You now have the beginnings of a memory library.

It may contain only one recording.

That's perfectly fine.

You've established a structure that can grow with the project.

## **How Mark's Experience Helps Us Here**

Let's return to the origin of Project Crystal.

Mark's creative work eventually grew into a much larger collection of conversations, manuscripts, documents, and technical projects.

As that collection expanded, organisation became increasingly important.

He developed and worked with tools for preserving information, revisiting earlier conversations, and organising manuscript files.

Some of those systems became quite sophisticated.

But here's the important lesson.

The sophistication of the technology wasn't what made the original conversations valuable.

The material was already valuable.

The systems made it easier to preserve, retrieve, and work with that material.

That's the principle we're borrowing.

You don't need to recreate Mark's technical environment.

You don't need a database server.

You don't need specialised programming tools.

You don't need an automated publishing system.

Those things may be useful for particular projects, but they aren't prerequisites for writing a personal book.

What you need is a dependable process.

Capture the story.

Save the source.

Organise the material.

Keep a backup.

Return to it when you're ready.

Those five habits can carry you a surprisingly long way.

And when your collection becomes large enough to require additional tools, you'll have a foundation that makes those tools much easier to introduce.

## **The Workshop and the Library**

There's one analogy I'd like to leave you with.

Imagine an artist who works with clay.

Their workshop might be messy.

There are tools on the table.

Unfinished pieces sit on shelves.

There are sketches, experiments, and ideas scattered around.

That's where the creative work happens.

Now imagine the artist also maintains a carefully organised collection of completed pieces, reference photographs, and important records.

The two spaces serve different purposes.

One encourages experimentation.

The other preserves the results.

Project Crystal works the same way.

Your conversations are the workshop.

Your memory library is the archive.

You don't have to make the workshop perfectly tidy.

And you don't want the archive to become so disorganised that nothing can be found.

The beauty of this arrangement is that it allows you to explore freely without sacrificing the ability to preserve what you discover.

The creative process remains natural.

The organisational process remains dependable.

And the eventual manuscript has a foundation of authentic source material.

## **One Last Thought About Ownership**

I want to finish this chapter with something important.

When you begin using AI to develop a personal book, it can sometimes feel as though the technology is doing an extraordinary amount of work.

It may organise hundreds of pages.

It may identify connections.

It may help construct entire chapters.

It may improve grammar, transitions, and readability.

Those capabilities can be impressive.

But don't confuse the work of arranging information with the experience of living it.

The AI wasn't there for the birthday parties.

It didn't share the family holidays.

It didn't hear the laughter across the dinner table.

It didn't know the person whose story you're preserving.

You did.

And that understanding cannot be replaced by a polished paragraph.

The technology can help you communicate your memories.

It can help preserve your words.

It can help build a book.

But the memories themselves are yours.

That's why we've spent this chapter establishing a system that keeps you in control of the material.

You retain the recordings.

You keep the original transcripts.

You decide what becomes part of the manuscript.

You determine what remains private.

And you maintain copies that don't depend entirely on any single AI application.

This is more than a technical precaution.

It's part of respecting the story.
