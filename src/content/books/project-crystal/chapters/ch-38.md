## The Moment the Stories Begin Looking Like a Book

Imagine opening your Memory Collection.

You've finished your first seven conversations.

You've organised the memories.

Given them identifiers.

Linked them to their original sources.

And created a simple index.

Perhaps you have twelve memories.

Maybe twenty-five.

Some are funny.

Others describe ordinary afternoons.

A few involve people you haven't thought about in years.

And several contain details that still need checking.

You look at the collection.

There's a childhood bicycle.

Grandpa's workshop.

An old radio.

A family holiday.

A friendship.

And the time someone attempted to repair an appliance and made the situation considerably worse.

You recognise the stories.

You can remember telling them.

And now they're organised well enough that you can find each one.

That's a lovely achievement.

But there's a problem.

You still don't have a book.

You have source material.

Useful source material.

But it doesn't yet tell you which story the finished book should communicate.

Should you begin with childhood?

Should Grandpa's workshop have its own chapter?

Would the funny memories work better together?

Perhaps the book should follow a timeline.

Or perhaps it would be more interesting to organise it around people and places.

And how do you know whether you have enough material?

Those are the questions we're going to explore.

Because we've reached an important transition.

Until now, we've concentrated on remembering and preserving.

Now we're going to begin designing.

Not designing the cover.

Not choosing the font.

And certainly not worrying about the printing costs.

We're designing the reading experience.

We're going to decide what kind of book this material could become.

And we're going to build a plan for writing it.

That plan is your **Book Blueprint**.

It doesn't have to be perfect.

It doesn't need thirty chapters.

And it doesn't need to contain every memory you've collected.

It simply needs to help you understand what you're trying to create.

**The Memory Collection preserves what you remember. The Book Blueprint establishes how you intend to share selected parts of those memories with readers.**

That's the distinction.

And once you understand it, the idea of writing a book becomes considerably more manageable.

# First, Forget About the Title

I know.

A book needs a title.

Eventually.

But not necessarily today.

Here's what often happens when someone begins planning a book.

They come up with a wonderful title.

Something clever.

Memorable.

Perhaps a little dramatic.

Then they begin arranging every memory to fit it.

That's not always a problem.

A strong title can help clarify a book's purpose.

But there's a risk.

The title may start dictating what the stories are supposed to mean.

Imagine a collection of childhood memories involving someone who enjoyed repairing things.

You call the book:

**The Boy Who Never Gave Up.**

That sounds rather inspiring.

But what does it imply?

That the person was consistently determined.

That every setback became an opportunity.

And perhaps that the collected stories demonstrate a lifelong pattern of perseverance.

Do they?

Perhaps some do.

But maybe the person also abandoned plenty of projects.

Maybe they became frustrated.

Changed interests.

Or simply enjoyed experimenting without needing every attempt to succeed.

Now the title may be pushing the manuscript toward a conclusion the evidence doesn't support.

That's what we want to avoid.

For now, a working title is enough.

Something simple.

**Robert's Family Stories.**

Or:

**Memories From the Workshop.**

Or even:

**My First Book Project.**

Perfectly acceptable.

You can choose something more distinctive later.

When the manuscript's purpose is clearer.

**A good title should reflect the book you're creating, not pressure the memories into becoming a different story.**

That's our first principle.

# The Question That Determines the Blueprint

Before choosing chapters, we need to answer one question.

**What should this book give its reader?**

Not what every memory means.

Not what you've learned from your entire life.

Just what the reader should gain from this particular collection.

Perhaps you want younger family members to know what their grandfather was like.

Maybe you want to preserve funny stories that are repeatedly told at family gatherings.

Perhaps you're creating a personal memoir about a particular period.

Or maybe you're documenting how you learned a trade.

Those purposes are different.

And they lead to different books.

Let's use our fictional Robert.

He's a retired equipment repairer.

He has been collecting memories.

Some involve his father.

Others concern childhood projects.

Several are funny.

And a few describe his working life.

Now imagine three possible books.

## Book One: Robert's Life Story

This would be a chronological memoir.

It might begin with childhood.

Then follow education, work, family experiences, and later life.

The objective would be to communicate a broad account of Robert's life from his perspective.

## Book Two: Things We Tried to Fix

This would be a thematic story collection.

It might focus on mechanical experiments, improvised repairs, mistakes, and the people involved.

The book could move between different periods of Robert's life.

The central connection would be the activity.

## Book Three: Afternoons With Dad

This would focus on Robert's relationship with his father.

The old garage.

Shared projects.

Conversations.

And everyday experiences.

It might be a smaller, more personal book.

All three could draw from the same Memory Collection.

But they wouldn't contain exactly the same stories.

Or organise them in the same order.

And that's the important point.

**The source material can support several possible books. The author chooses which one to create.**

The AI can help identify possibilities.

But it shouldn't make the decision for you.

# Writing Your Book Purpose Statement

Now we're going to create one of the most useful documents in the entire project.

Your Book Purpose Statement.

It doesn't need to sound literary.

It doesn't need to impress anyone.

It's a working instruction.

Here's a simple format.

**I am creating this book for...**

**I want readers to experience or understand...**

**The book will mainly contain...**

**The book will not attempt to...**

Let's complete it for our fictional Robert.

### Robert's Book Purpose Statement

**Intended Readers**

My children and grandchildren.

**Purpose**

I want them to know some of the ordinary and funny experiences from my childhood and working life, especially the projects I remember doing with family and friends.

**Main Material**

Personal recollections about repairs, experiments, family members, and shared activities.

**Scope**

A collection of selected stories rather than a complete autobiography.

**Important Boundary**

I don't want the book to pretend every project was successful or that every incident taught me a profound life lesson.

Now we have direction.

And that final sentence is particularly useful.

Because it protects the character of the material.

Robert doesn't want a motivational book disguised as a family story collection.

He wants the stories.

The mishaps.

The humour.

And the people.

That's his purpose.

Your statement may be completely different.

And that's fine.

The important thing is writing it clearly enough that you can return to it when making editorial decisions.

# The Reader Is Part of the Design

Now let's examine something that can easily be overlooked.

Who will read the book?

Imagine creating a memoir for your grandchildren.

They may not know the names of your childhood friends.

They may never have visited the house where you grew up.

And they may not recognise the tools or equipment you mention.

That doesn't mean you need to explain every detail.

But some context may be helpful.

Perhaps Grandpa's workshop needs a brief introduction.

Who was Grandpa?

Where was the workshop?

And why did the storyteller spend time there?

Now imagine the same book is intended for siblings who shared those experiences.

They already know much of that information.

The writing might be more informal.

And certain references may need little explanation.

The audience changes what the reader needs.

Not what actually happened.

That's an important distinction.

**You can add accurate context for the reader without altering the underlying memory.**

For example:

"My grandfather kept a workshop behind the house."

That's useful if the source supports it.

But if the location is uncertain, don't invent that detail for convenience.

Perhaps:

"I spent many afternoons in Grandpa's workshop."

That's sufficient.

The reader doesn't need a complete architectural survey to enjoy the story.

The objective is clarity.

Not unnecessary precision.

# Four Common Ways to Organise a Personal Book

Now let's examine some possible structures.

You don't have to use any of these exactly.

They're simply common ways to arrange genuine material.

## 1. Chronological Structure

This follows time.

Childhood.

Teenage years.

Early adulthood.

Later experiences.

And perhaps the present.

It's useful when the book's main purpose is to describe change over time.

A broad life memoir may benefit from this approach.

But it creates a challenge.

Dates matter.

If you don't know when particular events occurred, you'll need to avoid unsupported chronology.

You can use approximate periods.

Or group memories by life stage.

But don't invent a timeline simply to make the book appear orderly.

## 2. Thematic Structure

This groups memories by subject.

Family.

Friendships.

Work.

Hobbies.

Travel.

Humour.

And other recurring interests.

A thematic collection can move between different periods of life.

For example, a chapter about building things may contain childhood projects and adult experiments.

But the transitions should make the time periods clear.

You don't want readers assuming events occurred together when they didn't.

## 3. People-Centred Structure

This organises memories around important individuals or relationships.

A chapter about Grandpa.

Another about a childhood friend.

Another about family gatherings.

This can be useful when the purpose is to preserve shared experiences.

But avoid turning each person into a fixed character description.

A chapter about Grandpa should communicate genuine memories.

Not invent an idealised personality.

## 4. Place-Centred Structure

This uses meaningful locations.

The family home.

The garage.

The workshop.

The lake.

The neighbourhood.

Or another familiar place.

Places can connect several otherwise separate memories.

For example, the kitchen table might appear in stories about meals, homework, games, and childhood projects.

A place-centred structure could bring those experiences together.

But don't assume every story connected to a place occurred at the same time.

The chapter can make those differences clear.

---

# Choosing a Structure

Let's make the decision practical.

Ask yourself:

**Does the collection mainly show how something changed over time?**

Chronological organisation may help.

**Does it contain several recurring subjects?**

A thematic structure may be appropriate.

**Are particular people central to the stories?**

A people-centred approach might work.

**Do familiar locations connect the memories?**

Consider a place-centred structure.

You can also combine approaches.

Perhaps the book is broadly chronological.

But contains thematic chapters within each period.

Or perhaps it is organised around people.

With individual memories arranged chronologically inside each chapter.

That's perfectly reasonable.

The objective isn't to obey one system.

It's to choose an arrangement that helps the reader.

**The best structure is the one that communicates the selected memories clearly without creating relationships the sources don't support.**

---

# The Five-Minute Blueprint Test

Here's a useful way to test a possible book direction.

Imagine explaining the book to someone in a few sentences.

For example:

"It's a collection of funny and ordinary stories about growing up around my father's garage. It includes the repairs we attempted, the people who helped, and the projects that didn't always work."

That's clear.

Now imagine saying:

"It's about my childhood, my family, several different jobs, all the things I've learned, how my mind works, the history of machinery, and perhaps some philosophy."

That might contain interesting material.

But it doesn't yet describe one coherent book.

Perhaps there are several books hiding inside that explanation.

Or perhaps the purpose needs narrowing.

You don't have to discard the extra material.

Keep it in the Memory Library.

The current book can focus on one direction.

Other ideas can remain available.

That's one of the reasons we separated the archive from the manuscript.

**Choosing one book doesn't mean abandoning every other possible story.**

It means giving the current project a manageable purpose.

# Your First Book Blueprint

Now we're ready to create the actual document.

Let's begin with a simple template.

### PROJECT CRYSTAL: BOOK BLUEPRINT

**Working Title**

A temporary name for the book.

**Book Purpose**

Why the book exists.

**Intended Readers**

Who the book is for.

**Primary Structure**

Chronological, thematic, people-centred, place-centred, or a combination.

**Narrator**

Who is telling the stories.

**Voice**

How the writing should sound.

**Source Collection**

Which Memory Library or records support the book.

**Important Boundaries**

Privacy, factual uncertainty, attribution, and material outside the current scope.

**Proposed Parts or Chapters**

The working Table of Contents.

**Source Assignments**

Which memories may support each chapter.

**Outstanding Questions**

Information that needs checking or further collection.

**Drafting Approach**

How chapters will be written and reviewed.

**Current Status**

Planning, drafting, editing, or another clearly defined stage.

That's your blueprint.

It isn't a contract you can never change.

It's a working plan.

And it should remain editable as you learn more about the material.

But changes should be deliberate.

Not something an AI quietly makes because it prefers a different arrangement.

Chapter titles.

At last, we're beginning to see the shape of the book.

But we're going to approach it carefully.

We aren't going to ask AI for thirty dramatic chapter titles.

We're going to start with the Memory Index.

Look at the records.

Identify possible groups.

Then consider how those groups might become chapters.

# The Chapter Evidence Check

Now we're going to examine whether the proposed chapters can actually be written from the available memories.

This is one of the most important parts of the blueprint.

Because a chapter title can sound wonderful while having very little material behind it.

Imagine Robert approves:

**Chapter 6: The Repair That Worked**

But MEM-009 contains only one short sentence:

"I remember fixing something that actually stayed fixed."

That's not enough to support a detailed narrative chapter.

We don't know what was repaired.

When it happened.

Who was involved.

Or what the outcome meant to Robert.

The AI could produce a plausible chapter.

But it would have to invent most of the content.

We don't want that.

So the chapter needs more source material.

Or a narrower purpose.

Or perhaps it should be combined with another chapter.

That's what the evidence check helps us decide.

## The Chapter Evidence Matrix

Let's create a practical table.

| Chapter | Candidate Sources | Readiness |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The Bicycle That Wouldn't Stay Fixed | MEM-001 | Ready for source review |
| 2. Grandpa's Toolbox | MEM-002, MEM-007 | Needs more detail |
| 3. The Old Workshop | MEM-005, MEM-008 | Ready for source review |
| 4. Things That Didn't Quite Work | MEM-003, MEM-011 | Needs source comparison |
| 5. Friends Who Helped | MEM-006 | Insufficient material |
| 6. The Repair That Worked | MEM-009 | Insufficient material |
| 7. The Stories We Still Tell | MEM-004, MEM-010 | Needs scope review |

These are illustrative readiness assessments.

In a real project, the statuses would be based on examining the full records.

Now we have a clearer picture.

Some chapters may be ready to develop.

Others need additional conversations.

And a few may need structural changes.

That's normal.

A blueprint is supposed to reveal those issues before drafting begins.

**It's much easier to revise a proposed chapter than to repair ten pages of invented prose.**

That's why we're doing this work now.

# What Makes a Chapter Ready?

Let's answer that question.

Does a chapter need five memories?

Ten?

A thousand words of source material?

Not necessarily.

There's no universal number.

A chapter containing one richly documented incident may work beautifully.

Another chapter may require several short memories.

It depends on the book's purpose.

But there are useful questions.

Do we know what the chapter is supposed to communicate?

Is there enough source material to support that purpose?

Are the essential details available?

Is the intended narrator clear?

Can the chapter be written without inventing important events?

And if the material is uncertain, can that uncertainty be communicated honestly?

If the answer is yes, the chapter may be ready for drafting.

If not, we need to decide what happens next.

Perhaps collect more information.

Narrow the chapter.

Combine related memories.

Or leave the subject out of the current edition.

Those are all legitimate options.

**A chapter is ready when its purpose and available evidence are sufficiently aligned to support responsible writing.**

Not when the title sounds impressive.

# The Chapter Promise

Now let's introduce another practical tool.

For every chapter, write one sentence explaining what it should give the reader.

We'll call that the **Chapter Promise**.

For example:

**Chapter 1: The Bicycle That Wouldn't Stay Fixed**

"This chapter introduces Robert through a specific childhood repair attempt and the people involved."

That's clear.

It doesn't promise that the incident changed his life.

Or proved a lifelong trait.

It simply identifies what the chapter will communicate.

Now another.

**Chapter 3: The Old Workshop**

"This chapter introduces the workshop as a recurring setting and presents selected memories connected to it."

Again, straightforward.

Now consider:

**Chapter 5: Friends Who Helped**

"This chapter preserves memories of friends who participated in Robert's projects and activities."

That may be appropriate.

But the source collection currently has only one relevant memory.

Perhaps the chapter needs more material.

The promise helps reveal that.

## Why Chapter Promises Matter

Imagine you're drafting a chapter.

The AI begins wandering into unrelated subjects.

Perhaps it starts discussing the history of bicycle manufacturing.

That could be interesting.

But does it serve the Chapter Promise?

Probably not.

Unless a specific historical detail is needed to understand Robert's bicycle.

The promise helps you recognise when the writing has drifted.

It also helps prevent repetition.

If Chapter Two introduces Grandpa's tools, Chapter Three doesn't necessarily need to repeat the entire explanation.

The chapters should complement one another.

Not compete to tell the same story.

**A chapter title tells readers the subject. A Chapter Promise tells the writer what that section is supposed to accomplish.**

That's a useful distinction.

---

# The Beginning, Middle, and End of the Book

Now let's examine the overall reading experience.

You've created several chapters.

But does their sequence make sense?

A book doesn't necessarily need a dramatic plot.

Especially when it's a collection of memories.

But the reader still needs a coherent journey.

Imagine beginning with a funny childhood incident.

Then introducing the people and places that recur throughout the book.

Then exploring more involved experiences.

And finally ending with stories or reflections that provide a satisfying sense of completion.

That can work.

But don't force an emotional climax.

A personal memoir doesn't have to imitate a novel.

And a family story collection doesn't need every chapter to build toward one grand revelation.

Sometimes the ending can simply acknowledge that the book preserves selected experiences rather than an entire life.

## A Useful Three-Part Test

Look at your proposed Table of Contents.

Ask:

**Beginning:** Does the reader understand what kind of book this is and who is telling the stories?

**Middle:** Do the chapters develop the material without unnecessary repetition or confusion?

**End:** Does the book conclude in a way that suits its purpose?

That's enough for a first review.

You don't need a complex literary formula.

The reading experience should feel intentional.

And the chapter order should help rather than hinder.

---

# Don't Confuse Emotional Order With Historical Order

Here's something particularly important.

You may arrange stories so that the book becomes more engaging.

That's legitimate.

But you must not distort the actual chronology when it matters.

Imagine two memories.

One happened when Robert was twelve.

Another occurred when he was thirty.

You can place the adult memory first if that serves the book.

But you shouldn't imply the events occurred in the reverse order.

Likewise, placing two stories beside each other doesn't establish that one caused the other.

Perhaps Robert enjoyed repairing bicycles as a child.

And later became a professional equipment repairer.

Those facts may be related.

But the relationship should be presented appropriately.

It shouldn't automatically become:

"That childhood repair was the event that determined his entire career."

Unless Robert actually makes that interpretation, and the manuscript presents it as his reflection rather than verified causation.

The blueprint should organise the available stories.

Not manufacture a destiny.

**Editorial sequence can change. Historical claims must remain accountable to the sources.**

That's the boundary.

# How Many Chapters Should Your First Book Have?

Now let's address a practical concern.

Perhaps you've seen that *The Meistro Manual* contained thirty-six numbered chapters.

And you've noticed that Project Crystal itself is a substantial guide.

Does your book need to be that large?

No.

Absolutely not.

A first memory book might contain five chapters.

Or eight.

Or twelve.

Perhaps it's a collection of fifteen short stories.

The appropriate length depends on the material and the purpose.

Imagine you have ten well-documented memories.

You could create a concise book.

Perhaps fifty pages.

Maybe more or less.

There's no need to stretch it into a three-hundred-page manuscript.

And if you have fifty rich memories, you may want a longer collection.

The goal isn't to hit a particular word count.

It's to communicate the stories.

## Start With a Minimum Viable Book

Here's a useful concept.

A **Minimum Viable Book**.

Not a low-quality book.

Not an unfinished draft.

A small, coherent book that fulfils its intended purpose.

Perhaps:

An introduction.

Five chapters.

A short closing.

And a selection of photographs.

That's enough to create a meaningful family keepsake.

You can always develop an expanded edition later.

But beginning with a manageable scope makes the project easier to complete.

**A short book containing genuine, carefully preserved stories is better than a long book padded with invented material.**

That principle is worth keeping close.

# What Belongs in a Chapter and What Stays in the Archive?

Now let's imagine your Memory Collection contains thirty records.

You have a proposed book containing eight chapters.

Perhaps twenty memories seem directly relevant.

What happens to the other ten?

Nothing terrible.

They stay in the archive.

Perhaps they don't fit the current purpose.

Maybe they need more information.

Or perhaps they're simply too private for the intended audience.

That doesn't make them worthless.

They're still preserved.

And they may support another project later.

This is one of the most important reasons to maintain a separate Memory Library.

The book is selective.

The archive can remain broad.

You don't need to force every source into the current manuscript.

## The Parking Lot

Here's a practical idea.

Create a section in your blueprint called:

**Stories for Later Consideration.**

Place memories there when they don't currently fit.

For example:

**MEM-014:** Family holiday unrelated to the workshop theme.

**MEM-018:** Interesting personal experience requiring additional details.

**MEM-021:** Private recollection excluded from the intended edition.

The records remain available.

But the current manuscript has a clearer scope.

Now you can concentrate on the selected material.

Without worrying that everything else has been forgotten.

**Exclusion from one book is not deletion from the source collection.**

That distinction makes editorial decisions much easier.

# The Blueprint Doesn't Have to Be Perfect

Now let's address something that often stops people from progressing.

The fear of choosing the wrong structure.

Perhaps you create a Table of Contents.

Then think of a better one.

Perhaps you realise two chapters overlap.

Or a memory you expected to be important turns out to be quite short.

That's normal.

A blueprint is a working plan.

And a good one can evolve.

But the changes should remain visible.

For example:

**Blueprint v1**

Seven chapters.

Then:

**Blueprint v2**

Chapters Five and Six combined because the available source material was insufficient to support separate chapters.

Now you have six chapters.

That's perfectly reasonable.

The book may become clearer.

The important thing is not allowing the AI to silently reorganise the manuscript whenever it generates a new idea.

You should know which version is current.

And why it changed.

That's one of the lessons we learned from *The Meistro Manual* and BookForge.

**A structure can evolve without becoming unreliable, provided its changes remain deliberate and traceable.**

# The Problem With a Beautiful Outline That Has No Sources

Here's a caution worth repeating.

AI can create impressive Tables of Contents.

It can generate poetic titles.

Elegant part names.

And apparently sophisticated chapter sequences.

But an attractive outline isn't proof that the source material supports it.

Imagine you ask:

"Create a deeply emotional memoir about my grandfather."

The AI may propose:

**Chapter 1: The Man Who Taught Me Everything**

**Chapter 2: The Day Everything Changed**

**Chapter 3: The Lessons That Lasted Forever**

Those titles sound dramatic.

But what do the available memories establish?

Perhaps Grandpa taught you how to use a particular tool.

Maybe you have several funny stories about fishing trips.

And perhaps you remember spending ordinary afternoons together.

That material may be wonderful.

But it doesn't automatically support claims that he taught you everything.

Or that one event changed your life.

A more grounded outline might contain:

**Grandpa's Workshop**

**The Fishing Trips**

**The Things He Used to Say**

Those titles are less dramatic.

But perhaps more faithful to the material.

And more useful for the reader.

**The purpose of an outline isn't to make the life sound extraordinary. It's to organise the experiences you're actually able to describe.**

That's the standard.

# Using AI to Explore Different Blueprints

Now let's make use of AI's strengths.

It can compare possible structures quickly.

Suppose your Memory Collection contains twenty records.

You might ask for three alternatives.

A chronological structure.

A thematic structure.

And a people-centred structure.

Then compare them.

Which makes the best use of the available material?

Which requires fewer unsupported assumptions?

Which would be easiest for the intended readers to follow?

And which best serves your purpose?

The AI can help explain the trade-offs.

But it shouldn't silently choose one.

Let's imagine Robert considers two possibilities.

### Option A: Chronological Memoir

This would require a dependable sequence of childhood, work, and family experiences.

But several dates are uncertain.

And the current collection contains significant gaps.

That doesn't make the approach impossible.

But it may require more source work.

### Option B: Thematic Story Collection

This would organise the available memories around tools, projects, people, and humour.

Exact dates may matter less.

The current material may support this approach more naturally.

Perhaps Robert prefers Option B.

That's an editorial decision.

Not a scientific result.

And certainly not proof that a chronological memoir would be inferior.

It's simply the choice that best fits his current purpose and material.

Now the blueprint can proceed.

# The Project Crystal Blueprint Builder Prompt

Let's create the reusable instruction for this chapter.

Use it after you've established your initial Memory Collection.

The AI should review the material you actually provide.

And develop proposed structures grounded in those records.

### PROJECT CRYSTAL: MY FIRST BOOK BLUEPRINT

I have created a Memory Collection containing genuine recollections and source references.

I want to discover what kind of book this material could support.

Please help me develop a practical Book Blueprint.

Follow these instructions.

**1. Confirm the Available Material**

Identify the Memory Index, source records, and other documents you can actually access.

Do not claim to have reviewed missing material.

**2. Understand My Purpose**

Help me clarify why I want to create the book and who I intend to read it.

Do not choose my purpose without my approval.

**3. Identify Possible Book Directions**

Suggest up to three grounded approaches based on the available material.

These might be chronological, thematic, people-centred, place-centred, or instructional.

Explain the benefits and limitations of each.

**4. Preserve Source Boundaries**

Do not invent events, dates, quotations, emotional reactions, personality claims, or causal relationships.

Distinguish recollection from interpretation.

**5. Recommend a Manageable Scope**

Prefer a book the available material can genuinely support.

Do not inflate the chapter count merely to make the project appear substantial.

**6. Propose a Working Table of Contents**

Create chapter titles appropriate to the selected purpose.

Keep the titles accurate and understandable.

**7. Write a Chapter Promise**

For every proposed chapter, identify what it should communicate to the reader.

**8. Map Sources to Chapters**

Use the actual Memory IDs.

Do not invent or reuse source identifiers incorrectly.

**9. Evaluate Chapter Readiness**

Identify which chapters have sufficient source material.

Flag weak chapters and explain what additional information would be needed.

**10. Identify Overlap**

Look for chapters that repeat the same story or purpose.

Suggest combining or narrowing sections where appropriate.

**11. Identify Missing Context**

Determine whether readers may need explanations of people, places, terminology, or chronology.

Do not manufacture those explanations.

**12. Review Privacy and Permissions**

Identify material that may be inappropriate for the intended audience or requires additional authorisation.

**13. Preserve Excluded Material**

Keep stories outside the current scope in the Memory Library.

Do not delete them.

**14. Create the Blueprint Document**

Include:

- Working title.
- Purpose.
- Intended audience.
- Narrator.
- Structure.
- Voice.
- Proposed Table of Contents.
- Chapter promises.
- Source assignments.
- Readiness status.
- Outstanding questions.
- Drafting plan.

**15. Present the Blueprint for Approval**

Do not begin writing full chapters until I approve the direction.

The goal is a manageable, source-based plan for creating a genuine personal book.

---

That's the prompt.

It gives AI a meaningful planning role.

But it doesn't authorise the system to invent the book's source material.

And it keeps the final structural decisions with the author.

1

Introduce Robert through a childhood repair attempt involving a friend.
