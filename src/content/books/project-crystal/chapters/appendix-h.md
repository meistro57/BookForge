## When the Writing Machine Develops a Mind of Its Own

Imagine you've been working on a book for several weeks.

You've collected your memories.

Organised the sources.

Created a Table of Contents.

And written several chapters.

Everything seems to be moving along nicely.

Then something peculiar happens.

You ask the AI to continue Chapter Six.

It produces Chapter Eight.

You correct it.

It apologises and produces Chapter Seven.

Then begins discussing a memory you never told it about.

You explain that the memory isn't yours.

The AI agrees.

Then produces a beautiful paragraph containing three more details that didn't happen.

Well.

That's not quite the collaboration we had in mind.

Perhaps you try again.

This time, the AI remembers the chapter number.

But it changes the writing style.

Your straightforward, humorous story suddenly sounds like a speech delivered at a prestigious literary awards ceremony.

You ask for a simpler version.

Now it removes the humour.

You ask it to restore the original voice.

And it rewrites the entire chapter.

Including the sections you'd already approved.

At this point, you might reasonably wonder whether writing the book yourself would have been easier.

Sometimes it might be.

But before abandoning the process, consider something.

These problems don't necessarily mean AI-assisted writing is useless.

They often indicate that the task, source material, project state, or review process needs better control.

And those are things we can examine.

Throughout Project Crystal, we've explored the creative possibilities of conversational writing.

Now we're going to examine what happens when the process doesn't behave as intended.

Because reliable collaboration isn't the absence of mistakes.

It's the ability to recognise a problem, understand its consequences, and correct it without causing unnecessary damage.

That's the purpose of this appendix.

Not to promise that every error can be prevented.

Not to pretend that a sufficiently elaborate prompt can solve everything.

But to give you a practical troubleshooting method.

**When something goes wrong, identify the actual problem before asking the AI to change anything.**

That one habit can save an extraordinary amount of unnecessary work.

---

# Part One: The Project Crystal Troubleshooting Method

Let's begin with a simple procedure.

You can use it for almost any problem in the writing process.

We'll call it the **Five-Step Repair Loop**.

## 1. Observe

What actually happened?

Not what you assume happened.

What can you see?

Perhaps the AI produced the wrong chapter.

Maybe a sentence was deleted.

Or the transcript contains a suspicious name.

Describe the symptom.

## 2. Identify the Expected Result

What should have happened?

Perhaps Chapter Six should have been written from an approved source packet.

Or a particular paragraph should have remained unchanged.

You need a reference point.

Otherwise, it's difficult to determine whether the result is incorrect.

## 3. Compare

Examine the relevant sources.

The original transcript.

The approved chapter.

The current Book Blueprint.

Or the earlier manuscript version.

Find the actual difference.

## 4. Correct

Choose the smallest appropriate correction.

Don't rewrite the entire manuscript to fix one sentence.

Don't rebuild the Memory Library because one source link is wrong.

And don't replace the whole workflow when a specific instruction is sufficient.

## 5. Verify

Check that the correction worked.

And that it didn't create another problem.

For a manuscript edit, compare the revised passage against the source.

For a structural change, inspect the affected headings.

For a publishing problem, examine the actual output.

**A correction isn't complete merely because the AI reports that it made one.**

The result needs to be checked.

# Part Two: First, Identify Which Kind of Problem You Have

Most Project Crystal problems fall into a few broad categories.

| Problem Category | Typical Symptom |
|---|---|
| Source problem | Missing, unclear, or contradictory information |
| Memory problem | AI claims to remember something it cannot access |
| Project-state problem | Wrong book, version, or chapter |
| Writing problem | Invented details, repetition, or voice drift |
| Editing problem | Approved content changed or deleted |
| Structural problem | Broken headings, chapter order, or front matter |
| Verification problem | Unsupported claims or unreliable citations |
| Privacy problem | Sensitive material handled beyond its authorised use |
| Production problem | Output exists but contains formatting or content defects |

You don't need to memorise the table.

It's simply a reminder that different problems require different responses.

A source problem shouldn't automatically be treated as a prose problem.

And a production problem shouldn't automatically trigger a complete manuscript rewrite.

First identify the category.

Then choose the relevant repair.

# Part Three: The AI Invents Details

## Problem H-1: A Memory Becomes Fiction

**Symptoms**

You describe a childhood incident.

The AI adds a precise date.

Invents dialogue.

Describes the weather.

Or supplies another person's feelings.

The chapter sounds natural.

But some details weren't provided.

### Why It Happens

AI writing systems are designed to generate coherent language.

When information is missing, the system may produce a plausible continuation.

In fiction, that can be useful.

In source-based nonfiction, it's a serious problem.

Plausibility isn't evidence.

### The Repair

Return to the original source.

Identify the unsupported details.

Remove them.

Or explicitly mark them as creative reconstruction if that form is appropriate to the work and clearly disclosed.

For Project Crystal's genuine-memory workflow, the default is not to invent.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**SOURCE FIDELITY CORRECTION**

Compare this draft with the source material I provide.

Identify every significant detail not supported by the source.

Pay particular attention to dates, dialogue, scenery, emotions, motivations, and outcomes.

Do not assume an unsupported detail is false.

Classify it as unsupported by the available material.

Recommend the smallest corrections that preserve the story.

Do not rewrite unrelated passages.

### Verification

Read the revised section against the original memory.

Confirm that the unsupported additions are gone.

**Remember:** Fluent writing is not proof of factual accuracy.

# Part Four: The AI Pretends to Remember Earlier Conversations

## Problem H-2: The Phantom Memory

**Symptoms**

You say:

"Remember the story I told you last month?"

The AI confidently replies with a description.

But the details are wrong.

Perhaps it mixes two projects.

Or generates an event that sounds familiar but never occurred.

### Why It Happens

AI systems may have different memory and retrieval capabilities.

A particular conversation may not be accessible.

Some information may be summarised rather than preserved in full.

And a model may produce an answer based on incomplete context.

That answer can sound more certain than the available evidence justifies.

### The Repair

Require source identification.

Ask which conversation, file, or record supports the claim.

If the source isn't available, provide the relevant material again.

Don't allow a confident reconstruction to replace the original.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**MEMORY ACCESS CHECK**

Before continuing, identify which previous conversations, files, and project records you can actually access.

For each relevant claim about my earlier work, identify its available source.

If the source isn't accessible, say so.

Do not invent or reconstruct missing personal memories.

Use the current Project Control Sheet and source records as the authoritative working context.

### Verification

Check the referenced source yourself when necessary.

If no source exists, treat the claim as unverified.

**Remember:** An AI saying it remembers isn't the same as demonstrating the source.

# Part Five: The AI Starts Working on the Wrong Book

## Problem H-3: Project Identity Confusion

This problem appeared in the documented *Meistro Manual* editorial history.

Several manuscripts and creative projects existed in the wider working environment.

During one archived exchange, the relevant book had to be identified through the available project records rather than assumed from the conversation alone.

That's an important lesson.

### Symptoms

You ask for an edit to one manuscript.

The AI references chapters from another.

It uses the wrong title.

Or combines terminology from two different books.

### The Repair

Provide a clear Project Identity Block.

### Project Identity Block

**Project:** [Exact book title]

**Current manuscript:** [Filename or version]

**Current blueprint:** [Version]

**Current chapter:** [Number and title]

**Approved source packet:** [Reference]

**Task:** [Specific requested action]

**Other projects:** Do not use unless explicitly requested.

### Verification

Before major work, check that the AI identifies the correct project and version.

**Remember:** Familiarity with several projects can be useful, but it is not permission to merge them.

# Part Six: The AI Writes the Wrong Chapter

## Problem H-4: Chapter Sequence Drift

**Symptoms**

You request the next chapter.

The AI skips one.

Repeats an earlier chapter.

Or invents a title that isn't in the approved Table of Contents.

### Why It Happens

The current structure may not be available in the conversation.

The AI may be following an older outline.

Or it may infer what should come next.

### The Repair

Use the approved Book Blueprint as the authoritative sequence.

Don't rely on conversational memory alone.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**CHAPTER SEQUENCE CHECK**

Use the approved Book Blueprint I provide.

Identify the last completed chapter and the next chapter in the actual sequence.

Do not invent chapter titles or numbers.

Do not change the order.

If the current blueprint isn't available, identify that limitation rather than guessing.

Write only the next approved chapter.

### Verification

Compare the output heading with the blueprint.

Check that the previous chapter remains unchanged.

**Remember:** The phrase "next chapter" only works reliably when the correct sequence is established.

# Part Seven: The AI Changes Your Voice

## Problem H-5: The Story No Longer Sounds Like You

**Symptoms**

Your natural humour disappears.

Simple expressions become elaborate prose.

Or the manuscript develops a style you wouldn't recognise.

Perhaps every sentence sounds inspiring.

Even when the original story was about a failed repair.

### Why It Happens

The AI may interpret "professional writing" as more formal or dramatic writing.

It may also rely on generic stylistic patterns rather than your actual language.

### The Repair

Provide genuine examples of your preferred voice.

Compare the draft with those examples.

Then request targeted changes.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**VOICE RESTORATION**

The current draft doesn't reflect my intended storytelling voice.

I will provide examples of my actual language.

Identify where the draft becomes unnecessarily formal, dramatic, sentimental, or generic.

Preserve the facts and chapter structure.

Make targeted revisions that restore my directness, humour, and natural expressions.

Do not invent new personal mannerisms.

### Verification

Read the revised passage aloud.

Does it sound like something the intended narrator might genuinely say?

If not, revise further or return to the original wording.

**Remember:** A polished sentence isn't automatically a better sentence.

# Part Eight: Every Story Becomes an Inspirational Lesson

## Problem H-6: Forced Meaning

**Symptoms**

A funny incident ends with a paragraph about resilience.

A childhood hobby becomes proof of destiny.

Or an ordinary afternoon is presented as a life-changing revelation.

### Why It Happens

AI-generated nonfiction frequently gravitates toward thematic closure.

A neat conclusion can sound satisfying.

But it may introduce an interpretation the storyteller never expressed.

### The Repair

Separate the event from the reflection.

If the author hasn't provided a larger meaning, don't invent one.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**REMOVE FORCED INTERPRETATION**

Review this passage.

Identify conclusions about personal growth, destiny, character, or life lessons that aren't supported by the storyteller's own account.

Remove or qualify unsupported interpretations.

Preserve the actual events and natural humour.

Allow the story to end without a moral if that better reflects the source.

### Verification

Check whether the revised ending still communicates the event clearly.

Sometimes the simplest ending is the strongest.

**Remember:** A story doesn't require a moral to deserve a place in a book.

# Part Nine: The AI Repeats Itself

## Problem H-7: The Same Explanation Appears Everywhere

**Symptoms**

Every chapter restates the book's purpose.

The same example appears repeatedly.

Or the reader encounters nearly identical conclusions at the end of several chapters.

### Why It Happens

Chapters drafted separately may each include enough context to stand alone.

That can be helpful during writing.

But repetition becomes more noticeable when the chapters are assembled.

### The Repair

Review the complete manuscript.

Identify recurring material.

Then decide where each explanation belongs.

Don't simply delete every repeated phrase.

Some repetition provides useful reinforcement.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**REPETITION AUDIT**

Review the chapters I provide for substantial repetition.

Identify duplicated explanations, examples, and conclusions.

Distinguish useful reinforcement from unnecessary duplication.

For each finding, identify the affected passages and recommend which version should remain.

Do not edit the manuscript automatically.

### Verification

Read the revised chapters in sequence.

Check that removing repetition hasn't created missing context.

**Remember:** A repeated idea isn't automatically a defect. Unnecessary repetition is the problem.

# Part Ten: The AI Produces Too Much Material

## Problem H-8: The Endless Explanation

**Symptoms**

You ask a simple question.

The response becomes a long essay.

You request a short introduction.

The AI writes five pages.

Or every chapter contains extended explanations of points already established.

### Why It Happens

The task's scope may not be clear.

The system may also treat thoroughness as a reason to include every possible angle.

### The Repair

Define the intended purpose and level of detail.

For example:

"Explain this concept in two paragraphs for readers who already understand the previous chapter."

Or:

"Keep the example, remove the repeated background."

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**FOCUSED EDIT**

This section is longer than its purpose requires.

Identify what the reader actually needs to understand here.

Preserve the essential explanation and distinctive examples.

Remove unnecessary repetition and digressions.

Do not remove source-supported details simply to reach an arbitrary word count.

### Verification

Check that the shortened passage remains understandable.

**Remember:** Concision is useful when it improves clarity, not when it removes necessary information.

---

# Part Eleven: The AI Produces Too Little

## Problem H-9: A Full Chapter Request Becomes an Outline

**Symptoms**

You ask for a complete chapter.

The AI produces a summary.

Or several headings with short paragraphs beneath them.

### Why It Happens

The system may misinterpret the requested deliverable.

The source material may also be insufficient to support a substantial chapter.

Those are different problems.

### The Repair

First determine whether enough source material exists.

If it does, request the complete chapter.

If it doesn't, gather additional source material or narrow the chapter's purpose.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**COMPLETE CHAPTER REQUEST**

Using the approved Chapter Source Packet, write the full chapter as continuous, developed nonfiction prose.

Do not substitute an outline, summary, or chapter preview.

Follow the Chapter Promise and intended voice.

Use only supported material.

If the sources cannot support the intended chapter, identify the limitation rather than padding it with invented content.

### Verification

Check the actual chapter against its promise.

**Remember:** A longer chapter is not necessarily a better one, and unsupported padding isn't a solution.

# Part Twelve: The Transcript Contains Mistakes

## Problem H-10: The Recording and the Transcript Disagree

**Symptoms**

Names are misspelled.

Technical terms are wrong.

Numbers change.

Or a sentence loses an important word such as *not*.

### Why It Happens

Speech recognition can struggle with background noise, overlapping voices, accents, uncommon names, and technical vocabulary.

### The Repair

Return to the original audio.

Check the important passages.

Correct the working transcript.

Preserve the original recording.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**TRANSCRIPT ERROR REVIEW**

Review this transcript for likely speech-recognition errors.

Flag suspicious names, dates, numbers, technical terms, and changes in meaning.

Do not invent missing speech.

Identify the passages requiring comparison with the original recording.

Do not claim verification against audio unless that comparison has actually occurred.

### Verification

Listen to the relevant audio.

Record unresolved speech as unclear.

**Remember:** A transcript is a representation of speech, not automatically an exact record.

# Part Thirteen: Two Memories Get Mixed Together

## Problem H-11: The Hybrid Story

**Symptoms**

A person from one incident appears in another.

Two different projects become one.

Or details from separate time periods are combined.

### Why It Happens

Similar accounts may be treated as duplicates.

Or the AI may incorrectly infer that related memories describe the same event.

### The Repair

Separate the source records.

Compare people, locations, dates, events, and outcomes.

Don't merge unless the evidence supports doing so.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**SEPARATE POSSIBLY CONFLATED MEMORIES**

The draft may combine two different events.

Compare the relevant Memory Records and sources.

Identify details belonging to each event.

Flag uncertain relationships.

Do not merge the memories automatically.

Recommend corrections that preserve the distinction.

### Verification

Confirm that each event can be traced to its own source.

**Remember:** Two similar memories aren't necessarily one memory.

# Part Fourteen: Two People Remember the Same Event Differently

## Problem H-12: Conflicting Recollections

**Symptoms**

One person remembers an event happening in a garage.

Another remembers a backyard.

Or family members disagree about a date or sequence.

### Why It Happens

People may recall the same event differently.

Some memories may also be based on stories heard from others rather than firsthand experience.

### The Repair

Preserve both accounts.

Identify their sources.

Determine whether additional evidence exists.

And avoid inventing agreement.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**CONFLICTING MEMORY REVIEW**

Compare these accounts of the same event.

Identify what each source says.

Distinguish agreement, disagreement, and unknown details.

Do not choose a version solely because it sounds more coherent.

Recommend manuscript wording that preserves the uncertainty appropriately.

### Verification

Check that the final passage doesn't quietly convert one person's account into established fact.

**Remember:** Disagreement can remain unresolved.

# Part Fifteen: The AI Invents Citations

## Problem H-13: The Reference That Doesn't Exist

**Symptoms**

The AI provides a research-paper title that can't be found.

Quotes a source incorrectly.

Or supplies a confident claim with an irrelevant citation.

### Why It Happens

AI may generate plausible bibliographic details or rely on incomplete source information.

A citation-shaped sentence isn't evidence that the cited work exists or supports the claim.

### The Repair

Examine the original source.

Verify the author, title, publication, and relevant passage.

If the source can't be established, remove the unsupported citation or mark the claim for further research.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**REFERENCE VERIFICATION**

Review the citations in this section.

For each reference, determine whether the actual source is available and whether it supports the associated claim.

Do not invent bibliographic information.

Separate verified references from unresolved or unsupported citations.

Recommend corrections reflecting the evidence.

### Verification

Open the relevant sources when available.

Check the cited material directly.

**Remember:** An impressive-looking citation is not a substitute for evidence.

# Part Sixteen: The AI Treats a Hypothesis as Fact

## Problem H-14: Interpretation Becomes Certainty

**Symptoms**

The author offers a possible explanation.

Later, the AI refers to it as an established mechanism.

Or a metaphor gradually becomes a literal claim.

### Why It Happens

Repeated discussion can blur the distinction between a working idea and verified knowledge.

The AI may summarise uncertainty away.

### The Repair

Create a Claims Register.

Label statements according to their evidentiary status.

For example:

Personal observation.

Hypothesis.

Metaphor.

Interpretation.

Established claim.

Unverified claim.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**CLAIM STATUS REVIEW**

Examine this passage.

Identify statements that began as possibilities, interpretations, or metaphors but are now presented as established facts.

Restore the appropriate qualification.

Do not dismiss an idea merely because it remains hypothetical.

Do not present it as proven without sufficient evidence.

### Verification

Compare important claims with their actual supporting sources.

**Remember:** Interesting ideas can be explored without pretending they're established facts.

# Part Seventeen: The AI Changes Approved Text

## Problem H-15: A Small Edit Becomes a Large Rewrite

**Symptoms**

You request a spelling correction.

The AI rewrites three paragraphs.

A heading changes.

Or a distinctive phrase disappears.

### Why It Happens

Generative editing often recreates text rather than making strictly constrained replacements.

Even when the instruction requests a small change, the output may differ elsewhere.

### The Repair

Use minimal-change editing.

Provide the original passage.

Define the exact problem.

Request a comparison.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**MINIMAL-CHANGE EDIT**

Correct only the following issue:

[DESCRIBE ISSUE]

Preserve every unrelated sentence, heading, quotation, and factual detail.

Show the original passage and the proposed revision.

Identify every change.

Do not rewrite additional material.

### Verification

Compare the before-and-after versions.

**Remember:** The smaller the intended edit, the easier it should be to verify.

# Part Eighteen: Automated Cleanup Damages the Manuscript

## Problem H-16: The Search-and-Replace Disaster

This problem has a direct connection to the *Meistro Manual* case study.

The manuscript contained conversational expressions that needed removal.

But some short text patterns also appeared inside legitimate words.

A careless automated replacement could damage unrelated prose.

### Symptoms

Ordinary words contain missing letters.

Chapter titles change unexpectedly.

Protected terminology is altered.

Or sentences become grammatically broken.

### Why It Happens

A broad replacement operation may match character sequences without understanding their context.

### The Repair

Stop applying further cleanup to the affected working copy.

Preserve the original.

Compare the damaged text with the source.

Restore unintended changes.

Then replace the broad rule with a more controlled method.

### A Safer Editing Procedure

1. Inventory the source files.
2. Preserve untouched originals.
3. Identify the exact unwanted passages.
4. Review proposed changes.
5. Apply corrections to working copies.
6. Compare the results with the originals.
7. Test protected words and headings.
8. Inspect the surrounding prose.
9. Record the approved differences.

### Verification

Don't rely only on a script reporting success.

Inspect actual changed passages.

**Remember:** A text replacement can be mechanically successful and editorially disastrous.

# Part Nineteen: The Book Contains Chat Messages

## Problem H-17: Drafting Chatter Leaks Into Publication

**Symptoms**

A chapter begins with an AI greeting.

The narrator tells the author that a chapter is finished.

Or the manuscript contains instructions to request another chapter.

### Why It Happens

Serial chapters may be saved directly from conversational responses.

Those responses can contain material intended for the author rather than the eventual reader.

### The Repair

Conduct a contextual chatter audit.

Distinguish:

Reader-facing prose.

Quoted dialogue that legitimately belongs in the book.

And production commentary.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**CONVERSATIONAL ARTEFACT AUDIT**

Review the manuscript for messages addressed to the author during drafting.

Identify greetings, production updates, chapter-completion announcements, interface instructions, and other material inappropriate for the publication edition.

Preserve legitimate reader-facing conversational language.

Do not remove all occurrences of informal words or first-person plural phrasing.

List the proposed changes before applying them.

### Verification

Read the cleaned chapter opening and ending.

Check that the prose still flows naturally.

**Remember:** The manuscript should speak to its intended reader, not accidentally expose the drafting conversation.

# Part Twenty: Chapter Headings Break the Book

## Problem H-18: Structural Heading Problems

**Symptoms**

The Table of Contents contains repeated book titles.

Part headings appear in the wrong chapters.

Or automated navigation becomes confusing.

### Why It Happens

Individual chapter files may contain standalone title information that isn't suitable for the combined manuscript.

This was another documented *Meistro Manual* issue.

### The Repair

Establish a consistent heading hierarchy.

Separate publication metadata from chapter content.

Move formal part divisions to their intended positions.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**STRUCTURAL HEADING AUDIT**

Review the manuscript heading structure.

Identify repeated book-level headings, missing part headings, misplaced transitions, inconsistent chapter titles, and inappropriate heading levels.

Provide exact affected locations.

Recommend a consistent publication hierarchy.

Do not alter the original files automatically.

### Verification

Regenerate the Table of Contents or navigation.

Inspect the actual result.

**Remember:** A document can look correct while its underlying structure is wrong.

# Part Twenty-One: The Book Has Too Many Introductions

## Problem H-19: Front-Matter Overlap

**Symptoms**

The foreword explains the book.

Then the preface explains it again.

Then the introduction repeats the same material.

### Why It Happens

Opening sections may be drafted independently.

Each attempts to orient the reader.

When assembled, their purposes overlap.

### The Repair

Identify what each section contributes.

Keep material that serves a distinct purpose.

Combine or remove repetition.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**FRONT-MATTER REVIEW**

Compare the foreword, preface, introduction, collaboration note, and other opening sections.

Identify overlapping content.

Explain the distinct purpose of each section.

Recommend the smallest structural changes that produce a clear opening sequence.

Preserve important authorial material.

Do not automatically delete or merge sections.

### Verification

Read the front matter in its actual publication order.

**Remember:** Every introductory section should earn its place.

# Part Twenty-Two: The Manuscript Passes Tests but Still Has Problems

## Problem H-20: False Confidence From Automated Validation

**Symptoms**

A test suite reports success.

But the book still contains an awkward sentence.

An unsupported claim.

Or a confusing transition.

### Why It Happens

Automated checks examine specific conditions.

A successful test result establishes only that the tested conditions passed.

It doesn't establish overall editorial quality.

### The Repair

Separate mechanical validation from editorial review.

For example:

**Mechanical Review**

File count.

Chapter numbering.

Heading consistency.

Missing files.

**Editorial Review**

Clarity.

Voice.

Repetition.

Structure.

**Source Review**

Factual accuracy.

Attribution.

Uncertainty.

**Production Review**

Rendered layout.

Navigation.

Accessibility.

### Verification

Require evidence for each completed review stage.

**Remember:** Passing tests is useful evidence, not universal approval.

# Part Twenty-Three: The AI Says a File Was Saved, but It Wasn't

## Problem H-21: The Imaginary Deliverable

**Symptoms**

The AI announces that a PDF was created.

Or that a file was updated.

But the document doesn't exist where expected.

### Why It Happens

The system may describe intended work instead of a completed tool action.

Or the operation may have failed.

### The Repair

Require confirmation from the actual file or application.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**DELIVERABLE VERIFICATION**

Identify the exact file or document you claim to have created or modified.

Confirm whether the relevant operation actually completed.

Provide the verified location or identifier when available.

If the operation was not performed or failed, state that clearly.

Do not describe an intended action as completed work.

### Verification

Open the actual file.

Check its contents.

**Remember:** A status message isn't a substitute for a deliverable.

# Part Twenty-Four: The Manuscript Version Is Unclear

## Problem H-22: Which File Is the Real Book?

**Symptoms**

You have:

`book_final.md`

`book_final_v2.md`

`book_final_fixed.md`

And:

`book_final_REALLY_FINAL.md`

Nobody knows which one is authoritative.

### The Repair

Create a version register.

Record:

The current approved text.

The working version.

The last released version.

And any pending changes.

Use descriptive version names.

### Example

**Approved Text:** Manuscript v1.4

**Current Working Copy:** Manuscript v1.5-draft

**Last Released Edition:** Edition 1.0

**Open Changes:** Three documented corrections

Now the project has a clear state.

### Verification

Check the file contents and associated change record.

**Remember:** A filename containing "final" doesn't establish approval.

# Part Twenty-Five: The Printed Book Looks Wrong

## Problem H-23: Successful Export, Bad Layout

**Symptoms**

Text is too close to the spine.

A photograph is blurry.

The page numbers are wrong.

Or chapter headings appear at awkward positions.

### Why It Happens

The manuscript and production layout are separate artefacts.

A successful export doesn't guarantee a readable physical book.

### The Repair

Review the actual production file against the printer's specifications.

Then inspect a proof.

### Print Review Questions

Are margins appropriate?

Is the text readable?

Are chapter starts consistent?

Does the cover fit the selected binding?

Are photographs correctly sized?

Is anything clipped?

### Verification

Inspect the rendered pages.

And, when practical, a physical proof copy.

**Remember:** The book the reader receives is the real production test.

# Part Twenty-Six: The Ebook Doesn't Navigate Properly

## Problem H-24: Broken Digital Structure

**Symptoms**

The Table of Contents points to the wrong location.

Headings appear twice.

Or images behave poorly when text is enlarged.

### The Repair

Inspect the ebook's document hierarchy.

Check chapter navigation.

Use appropriate validation tools.

Then open the ebook in actual reading software.

### Verification

Test important navigation paths and text resizing.

**Remember:** An EPUB file existing is not proof that the reading experience works.

# Part Twenty-Seven: The Audiobook Gets Things Wrong

## Problem H-25: Audio Production Errors

**Symptoms**

Names are mispronounced.

Sentences are repeated.

A chapter is missing.

Or the narrator reads visual-only material that makes little sense aloud.

### The Repair

Prepare a narration script from the approved manuscript.

Create a pronunciation guide.

Adapt visual material appropriately.

Then review the actual audio.

### Verification

Listen to the output.

Check completeness, pronunciation, chapter order, and rights.

**Remember:** Audio production is a separate edition, not simply another export button.

# Part Twenty-Eight: Privacy Becomes a Problem

## Problem H-26: Material Is Used Beyond Its Permission

**Symptoms**

A private interview appears in a public draft.

A contributor's full name is included unexpectedly.

Or an audio recording is uploaded to a service without the required authorisation.

### The Repair

Stop the affected use where possible.

Identify the source.

Review the relevant permissions and applicable requirements.

Determine whether the material must be removed, restricted, or handled differently.

Don't assume changing a name solves every privacy problem.

### Copyable Repair Prompt

**PRIVACY AND USE REVIEW**

Review the intended use of this source material.

Compare it with the documented permissions and restrictions I provide.

Identify any use that is not clearly authorised.

Do not invent consent.

Recommend proportionate corrective actions.

Do not share, publish, or upload additional material.

### Verification

Confirm that the affected working and distribution copies have been handled appropriately.

**Remember:** Permission to participate in a conversation isn't automatic permission for every future use.

# Part Twenty-Nine: The Project Becomes Too Complicated

## Problem H-27: You're Managing the System Instead of Writing

**Symptoms**

You have more dashboards than chapters.

More prompts than memories.

And several applications whose purpose you can no longer explain.

### Why It Happens

New tools can be exciting.

Each promises to solve a problem.

But the accumulated system may become harder to maintain than the original task.

### The Repair

Return to the current goal.

Ask:

What must happen next to advance the book?

Perhaps you need to finish one memory.

Or review one chapter.

You may not need another tool.

### The Simplification Test

For each component, ask:

What problem does it solve?

What happens if I remove it?

Is there a simpler way?

And is it actually used?

If a component has no clear value, consider removing it from the active workflow while preserving anything important.

**Remember:** The writing system should serve the book.

Not become the main obstacle to creating it.

# Part Thirty: You're Stuck Choosing Between Endless Options

The AI offers twenty titles.

And endless opening paragraphs.

### The Repair

Define acceptance criteria.

For example:

"The title must be accurate, easy to remember, and appropriate for a family audience."

Now evaluate the available options.

Record the decision.

And continue.

# Part Thirty-Three: When to Fix and When to Rebuild

Now let's examine a particularly important decision.

Suppose a chapter contains a problem.

Should you repair the passage?

Rewrite the chapter?

Or return to the source collection and start again?

The answer depends on the defect.

## Use a Small Correction When:

A name is misspelled.

One sentence is unclear.

A transition is awkward.

Or a single unsupported detail needs removal.

## Use a Section Revision When:

Several paragraphs repeat one another.

The argument becomes confusing.

Or the narrative loses its intended direction.

## Consider a Chapter Rewrite When:

The chapter's structure fundamentally fails its purpose.

Important source material was omitted.

Or extensive invention makes the draft unreliable.

## Return to Source Collection When:

The essential memory hasn't been established.

Different events have been confused.

Or the information required to support the chapter is unavailable.

That's the important distinction.

You shouldn't solve a missing-source problem by generating more prose.

And you shouldn't discard a strong chapter because one heading is wrong.

**Choose the repair that matches the actual defect.**

That's how you avoid unnecessary work.

# Part Thirty-Four: Why Verification Must Be Independent

Now let's return to a recurring lesson from *The Meistro Manual*.

Imagine an AI performs an edit.

Then you ask the same AI:

"Did you fix it correctly?"

It responds:

"Yes. Everything is perfect."

Perhaps the correction was successful.

But the answer alone doesn't establish that.

The system may be relying on its own description of the change.

Rather than independently comparing the output with the original.

That's why a separate verification pass can be valuable.

Especially for large or automated edits.

## A Better Procedure

**Editing Pass**

Make the approved correction.

**Comparison Pass**

Identify exactly what changed.

**Regression Pass**

Check important content that should have remained unchanged.

**Editorial Review**

Read the affected passages for meaning and flow.

Those stages can involve the same tool or different tools.

What matters is that the verification examines the actual result.

Not merely the earlier claim of success.

## The Verification Question

Instead of asking:

"Is everything good now?"

Ask:

"Show me the specific before-and-after differences, identify anything changed outside the intended scope, and report what checks were actually performed."

That's much more useful.

**Verification should produce evidence, not reassurance.**

## The Difference Between Severity and Priority

A problem may be serious but not immediately blocking.

For example, an uncertain reference in an appendix may require attention before publication.

But you can continue drafting unrelated chapters.

Meanwhile, a missing current manuscript file may block all further editing.

So it's useful to distinguish:

**Severity:** How much the problem affects quality, accuracy, or safety.

**Priority:** How urgently it needs to be addressed for the current task.

That helps prevent every issue from becoming an emergency.

# Part Thirty-Six: The Emergency Recovery Prompt

Now let's create a single prompt for situations where a project has become confused.

Perhaps files were edited incorrectly.

Versions are inconsistent.

Or an AI agent has made changes you don't understand.

The first goal is to establish the actual state.

Not immediately repair everything.

## PROJECT CRYSTAL: EMERGENCY MANUSCRIPT RECOVERY

I have a writing project with possible errors, missing context, or unintended changes.

Do not edit anything yet.

First establish the current state from the materials you can actually access.

Follow these instructions.

**1. Identify the Project**

Confirm the exact book title and intended manuscript.

**2. Inventory Available Files**

List the actual source, working, and production files.

**3. Identify the Last Known Good Version**

Use documented evidence.

Do not guess.

**4. Identify Reported Problems**

Separate confirmed defects from suspected problems.

**5. Compare Versions**

Identify changes that can be demonstrated.

**6. Protect Originals**

Do not overwrite raw sources or approved backups.

**7. Classify the Damage**

Determine whether the problem concerns sources, prose, structure, metadata, permissions, or production.

**8. Propose Minimal Corrections**

Recommend the smallest safe repair for each confirmed defect.

**9. Identify Unrecoverable or Missing Information**

Do not invent lost content.

**10. Create a Recovery Plan**

Prioritise actions according to their consequences and dependencies.

**11. Require Approval Before Broad Changes**

Do not perform bulk rewrites, deletion, or publication actions without appropriate authorisation.

**12. Verify Every Repair**

Compare the repaired material against the relevant source and acceptance criteria.

The objective is to recover a dependable working state without causing additional damage.

---

That's the recovery prompt.

It is deliberately cautious.

Because when the state of a manuscript is uncertain, uncontrolled additional editing can make the situation worse.

**First establish what exists. Then decide what to repair.**

# Part Thirty-Seven: A Realistic Troubleshooting Session

Let's walk through a fictional example.

Robert has been writing *Things We Tried to Fix*.

He's reached Chapter Four.

The chapter concerns several unsuccessful mechanical projects.

The AI produces a draft.

Robert reads it.

Something feels wrong.

The chapter includes a scene in which his father laughs after a machine fails.

Robert doesn't remember that happening.

Let's follow the repair loop.

## Observe

The manuscript includes a reaction not present in Robert's recollection.

## Identify the Expected Result

The chapter should describe the actual project and preserve what Robert remembers.

## Compare

Robert opens the relevant Memory Record.

The source describes the machine failing.

It doesn't describe his father's reaction.

## Correct

Robert instructs the AI to remove the unsupported reaction.

He asks it to preserve the rest of the passage.

## Verify

The revised chapter no longer attributes the reaction to his father.

The surrounding prose remains intact.

Problem solved.

Now imagine another issue.

That's a different defect.

Robert doesn't need to reopen the memory interview.

He needs a repetition review.

He identifies the repeated passage.

Decides which chapter should contain the full explanation.

Then revises the other.

Two problems.

Two different repairs.

That's why classification matters.

**A dependable writing process doesn't treat every problem as a reason to start over.**

# Part Thirty-Eight: What If You Cannot Recover the Original?

Now let's consider a harder situation.

A recording is missing.

The original conversation wasn't exported.

And only an AI summary remains.

Can you still use the material?

Possibly.

But be honest about its status.

The summary is now a derivative record.

You may be able to remember the original account.

Or reconstruct parts of it through another conversation.

But you shouldn't pretend the missing source has been preserved.

## The Appropriate Record

**Original Source:** Unavailable.

**Available Material:** AI-generated summary from an earlier conversation.

**Verification Status:** Not independently checked against original transcript.

**Possible Next Action:** Ask the storyteller to review and correct the summary.

Now the limitation is visible.

Perhaps the resulting material is sufficient for a personal recollection.

Perhaps it isn't.

That depends on the intended use.

But the decision can be made honestly.

**When a source is lost, preserve the distinction between what remains and what can no longer be verified.**

That's better than inventing the missing evidence.

# Part Thirty-Nine: When to Stop Troubleshooting

Here's another useful question.

When is the problem actually resolved?

AI can always suggest another improvement.

Another check.

Another possible edit.

But not every manuscript needs endless revision.

A problem is resolved when its agreed acceptance criteria are satisfied.

For example:

**Issue:** Chapter title duplicated.

**Acceptance Criteria:** One correct chapter heading appears in the final navigation.

**Verification:** Generated Table of Contents inspected.

**Status:** Closed.

Or:

**Issue:** Unsupported year.

**Acceptance Criteria:** Manuscript no longer presents the year as established fact.

**Verification:** Source and revised passage compared.

**Status:** Closed.

That's enough.

You don't need a philosophical discussion about whether the entire book is now perfect.

You addressed a specific defect.

Checked the result.

And moved on.

**A good troubleshooting process includes a clear definition of finished.**

That prevents small problems from consuming the whole project.

Publishing software introduces problems.

# Final Quick Reference: The Five-Step Repair Loop

**1. Observe**

Describe the actual problem.

**2. Establish the Expected Result**

Identify what should have happened.

**3. Compare**

Examine the relevant sources, versions, and records.

**4. Correct**

Choose the smallest appropriate repair.

**5. Verify**

Check the actual result and any unintended changes.

## The Rule to Remember

**Don't solve a missing-source problem with invented writing, a structural problem with a chapter rewrite, or a verification problem with another confident promise. Match the correction to the evidence.**
