# Hidden Knowledge and Lost Civilizations

One prominent theme in Cannon’s work is the existence of hidden sacred knowledge from ancient civilizations. In books like The Convoluted Universe series and The Search for Hidden, Sacred Knowledge, Cannon’s hypnotic subjects described advanced ancient societies (such as Atlantis and Lemuria) and mystery schools where profound wisdom was taught 3 . She wrote that many people alive today may have had past lives as “keepers of sacred knowledge” in those lost civilizations or esoteric orders 3 . For example, Cannon’s sessions depict Atlantis as a highly advanced society with great technology and spiritual power that was lost to cataclysm 4 . Similarly, she explored ancient cultures like Lemuria and even pre- historic monuments (e.g. an “original” Stonehenge) in her fourth Convoluted Universe book 5 . This hidden history, in Cannon’s view, has been largely lost due to disasters and deliberate destruction over millennia, surviving only in fragmentary myths and secret archives.

Comparisons with historical and current knowledge: Mainstream archaeology has indeed been uncovering evidence that pushes civilization’s timeline further back, aligning in part with the idea that sophisticated culture existed earlier than long believed. A striking example is Göbekli Tepe in Turkey – a massive megalithic complex built ~11,000 years ago (6,000 years before Stonehenge) by organized hunter- gatherer communities 6 . Its discovery in the 1990s surprised researchers, revealing that prehistoric people could erect monumental temples before the advent of agriculture 7 8 . This reshapes previous ideas of civilization and suggests ancient humans had knowledge (technical, architectural, possibly astronomical) beyond what was traditionally assumed for that era 9 6 . While Göbekli Tepe is not proof of Atlantis, it supports the notion that advanced societal skills (stone carving, complex organization) existed in remote prehistory – echoing Cannon’s assertions of lost advanced cultures.

She believed, for instance, that secret information about human history and metaphysics might be kept in places like the Vatican archives 10 . It is true that the Vatican’s “Secret” Archives (now officially the Apostolic Archives) contain a vast collection of historical documents spanning over a millennium 11 . This repository includes correspondence and books that only qualified scholars can access after a waiting period 12 . The archives have spurred conspiracy theories – for example, speculation that the Church conceals proof of extraterrestrial life or apocryphal texts in those vaults 10 . In reality, the contents known to historians include papal records, political letters, and artifacts (e.g. a letter from Mary Queen of Scots) 13 , and the Church denies any sensational secrets (the term “secret” really means “private” in Latin) 14 . Still, Cannon’s suggestion of hidden archives parallels the enduring public belief that not all knowledge of our past or the cosmos has been released – a belief bolstered whenever new discoveries (like ancient manuscripts or unexplained archaeological finds) emerge.

Looking to the present and future, there is growing interest in revisiting humanity’s past for forgotten wisdom. Independent researchers and authors (e.g. Graham Hancock) have popularized theories of a “lost civilization” wiped out around the last Ice Age, pointing to sites like Göbekli Tepe as clues. While such ideas remain debated, mainstream science is increasingly open to the complexity of prehistoric societies. Ongoing archaeological projects may uncover older sites or records (for example, undersea ruins or new Dead Sea Scrolls) that could validate some of the “hidden knowledge” claims. Cannon’s work urges that we pay attention to ancient clues; indeed, ignoring them might mean missing lessons from a prior epoch of humanity. As evidence, note that even conventional historians now acknowledge that civilization’s story is not as linear as once thought – humans of 10–12,000 years ago achieved feats we struggle to explain, suggesting lost chapters in our collective memory 6 .

In summary, Cannon’s portrayals of advanced ancient civilizations and guarded wisdom find partial support in real-world trends: archaeological finds are rewriting history’s timeline, and institutions like the Vatican do hold vast historical records (if not necessarily mystical secrets). The pattern that emerges is one of history being richer and older than textbook accounts – a point where Cannon’s metaphysical narratives and empirical research converge. Her insight that key knowledge from our distant past could be waiting to be rediscovered should not be dismissed. If anything, recent discoveries encourage us to remain open-minded, as there may indeed be “hidden sacred knowledge” that, once revealed, can transform our understanding of human origins and destiny.
