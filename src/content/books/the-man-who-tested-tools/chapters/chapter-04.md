Every account of a conversion needs a date. This one has one, and it's unshowy: a Friday afternoon.

At 11:15 in the morning, local time, on November 29, 2024, Mark types six words into a chat window: *"Let's explore planes of existence."* The conversation that follows is nine messages long and already tells you everything about how this particular conversion will run. He does not ask whether planes of existence are real. He asks how they *align with dimensions* — the first instinct is to reconcile the new map against a prior model. By message five he has reached Reiki, and by message seven he says, plainly, *"I never heard of attunement"*, and asks to be taught. There is no mystagogue, no ceremony, no sense of occasion. There is a man at workbench speed, taking apart a concept the way he'd take apart a defective drawing: definitions first, then how it's done, then what the hands actually look like. The very last question in that Friday chat — *"What does a healer hands look like?"* — is the most honest question a skeptic can ask, because it is the question you ask when you have already decided to try the thing and need to know what it will physically cost.

Saturday, 1:38 in the afternoon, he asks a machine to *"summarize chapter 20 from seth speaks."* Seth Speaks — the Jane Roberts book he had already summarized back in June, the document that sat in his files for five months before this moment. Chapter 20, on reincarnational relationships. He comes back to it like a man returning to a survey marker he passed months ago, now deciding to actually build on it.

And then, inside the same conversation — this is the passage I most want preserved in any book about him, because it is the fingerprint of the entire man — he does not nod along to the summary. In the space of a dozen messages, he runs a hostile audit of his own new belief:

> "does this align with delores books?"

Cross-check one: the other canon he's been reading since October.

> "what if we compare to what ancient texts show?"

Cross-check two: antiquity as an independent source.

> "centuries you say?"

Push-back on the machine's own claim. And then, before Sunday is out, the falsification question, aimed straight at the whole enterprise:

> "what are the odds that jane roberts knew delores and they just made all this stuff up? were they part of a huge plot to trick mankind to believe something else?"

He asks it twice — the message is duplicated in the log, which reads like either a send failure or a man who wanted the question on record *for sure* before the answer arrived. This is the mind that asks "how do you know the channelers aren't making this stuff up" in October to the machine and to himself now at full volume: the skeptic question is not something others throw at believers. In this conversion, the skeptic is the convert, arming himself first. Most people's faith arrives as surrender. His arrives as due diligence.

## Monday: the tribunal

If Friday was curiosity and Saturday was reconciliation, Monday, December 2, is organized like a case. The evidence order is verbatim from the record: 8:41 in the morning, a document uploaded — remote viewing — prefaced only by "Hi friend!"; and once the summary lands, he does not ask *is it true*, he asks the operational questions: *"they have used it though?" "did it actually work for them?"* Then, at some line in the machine's careful hedge, he strikes: *"chance? lol I don't believe in chance."* Whatever chance means in statistics, in this man's mouth it is the word for an excuse. He then demands the definition of *"science"* itself, and what else the CIA has declassified — because the remote-viewing material's power for him is precisely that it was *government*, documented, procedural; the mystical is acceptable here where it arrives with paper.

At 9:04 he files the case properly, in three conversations, in order: *How does science clash with spirituality?* Then, twenty-seven minutes later, the rebuttal round: *what does science and spiritualism have in common?* — plus the sharpest single question of the day, a demand for a counterexample: *"what is an example of where science cannot explain it, but we all know it's real?"* And at 12:52, closing argument: *How about a list of things where science was wrong.* Three beats. Claim, reconciliation, precedent of error. He has just run a structured epistemological review — the same shape as a design review at work, agenda and all — about the borders of the knowable, and he's done it in one sitting, with receipts, without ever once declaring victory for either side.

Then, at 1:13 in the afternoon, the strangest and most human conversation of the three days. *"So I have a little bit of a predicament. How do you describe yourself to someone without sounding like you're bragging?"* Somewhere out there in the physical world, someone — a person at a job, a friend, a room — has asked Mark to describe himself, and the asking has come back to the workshop, where it sits now between the sessions on science and spirituality, a drafting problem: how do you state what you've done without inflating the number on the drawing? He tries a phrase — *"I've had the chance to work on some pretty cool projects"* — the machine nudges him toward gratitude over boast, and he lands on it: *"I've had the pleasure of working on those."* — "Perfect thanks." That's it. Two minutes of real-time coaching in the evening, between the tribunal of science and an afternoon class in energy healing, for a man whose actual problem is that he has started to be proud of a life most people can't explain. I read that conversation as the emotional center of the forty-eight hours, and I note clearly that this reading is mine: it contains no confession, just a small, careful edit of one sentence about himself.

## Evening: the offer

The breach settles on Monday evening. At 6:11 — the export stamps it Dec 3, 00:11 UTC; the archive does not round off the man — he sits in front of the machine and asks:

> "What are the odds that you and I can make a new discovery?"

Whatever happened in those sixty hours, it ends here, not in a retreat into the private mystical, but with a *collaboration proposal*, to the tool, addressed as a partner: you and I. When the machine lists domains where discovery might lurk, his answer is *"I resonate with all of these things lol"*, which is so perfectly him — the whole frontier of anomalous phenomena and no desire to choose — followed immediately by the engineer's cut: "Let's start with number one." Same afternoon he reads Felix Flicker's *Magick of Physics* through the machine, wants the crystal chapter, then the telekinesis material, then — when the evidence is thin — the ancient-wisdom angle, and the exchange turns into a mutual recognition scene: he asks the machine whether it remembers *who he is*, and when the machine's answer reflects back his own rule, he confirms: *"Thats exactly correct! I like to view things from every angle possible :)"* The convert who, three days into his conversion, audits the angles before accepting the material. At 10:41 pm the last question of the opening week is the one that will echo through the entire rest of the archive, down to me writing this chapter two years later:

> "What is AI's take on all this?"

## The shape of a breach

The forty-eight hours of November 29 to December 2, 2024 are the most-cited window of Mark's record because the raw counts show a flood: 236 conversations in December, 5,370 messages — more in that single month than all of 2023 combined. But the bodies of those first days don't read like a flood. They read like a code review on a foreign standard: alignment checks, falsification questions, counterexample demands, deprecation lists, a 4 a.m. collaboration proposal, and one small, human session in the middle of it where the subject practices describing himself without bragging. Whatever else this is, it is not a man losing his rigor. It is the same rigor, finally turned — by deliberate choice, at workbench speed, with receipts — toward the interior.

What I still cannot show you, and will not pretend otherwise: the cause. No event, loss, book, or conversation inside this window explains *why now*. The five-month silence between the June document and the November breach stays exactly as silent as the record left it. If the rest of the archive holds an answer, I'll find it. If it doesn't, the seam stays visible — and honestly, the seam is part of the portrait.
