Every biographer eventually learns the subject's birth date. Mine began with something better: the first thing he ever asked a machine.

It was a Tuesday night in February 2023, nine-thirty in the evening by his clock, and the question was about traction — *explain how the drivetrain works on an awd drive car*. Three days later came the second surviving question, and it is the one that taught me how to read this entire archive:

> "Are you familiar with the AISC steel shapes database?"

He was not asking for information. He knew the AISC shapes database; he had lived inside it for years as a detailer of structural steel. He was asking the machine whether it knew it — a calibration question, the human equivalent of plugging in a meter to see if the display reads zero correctly. Give the new tool a known truth. Watch what it does with it. This is how a man who works with load-bearing steel approaches anything: *establish the reference point before you trust the instrument.*

I say this is the key to the whole archive because the pattern does not stay in 2023. It is in December 2024, when he opens a brand-new subject — channeling entities — with "tell me about Bashar. Who is he?" The same entry probe. It is in November 2026, three years late, when he hands me his own books and asks me to compare an author's mechanics against another's. He never stops calibrating. The objects change; the method never does. What he trusted, ultimately, was not any answer. It was his own procedure for testing answers — applied, at last, to the answers themselves.

## The year of small machines

The rest of 2023 — all eighty-eight of his surviving first-year conversations, which is small enough to read entire and strange enough to reward it — is a portrait of a mind with twelve irons on the fire and a job that fed none of them.

The steel is there, of course: beam deflection problems worked three times over (same eighteen-foot beam, refined until the arithmetic agreed), a W8x13 asked for its maximum load at eleven feet "based on AISC steel guide" — the man citing the code like a witness — a Python script to compute member weights from a CSV, a PyQt GUI to browse shape properties, a steel railing cost estimator, a linear nesting interface, corten steel questions, a search for free shop estimating software because the licensed stuff costs a fortune and his shop had needs. On the wall of this archive, the complaints wrote themselves: in February he asked what users complain about in Autodesk Advance Steel, and by March he had commissioned Lisp scripts for the same program. I have watched this motion across three and a half years of his records and it has a name now, in my reading of him: *Mark does not leave a frustration installed.* Complaint is input. Every tool that annoys him gets either scripted, replaced, or eventually converted into a product. The Advance Steel gripe of week one returns, decades of record later, as an API project in December 2025 and a manufacturing-requirements system named SteelFlowMRP on New Year's Day 2026. This is not ambition in the usual sense. It is closer to hygiene.

But the steel is only one register. The 2023 record keeps switching channels with a straight face. In March, between the beam deflections, he asks the machine to write him a RhinoScript that generates iron scrollwork — decorative railing, the art side of the trade — and then, without apparent transition, asks it to describe, in text, what Johnny Carson looks like. He requests ten ways an architect could improve his drawings ("sketching with humor," he titles it himself, as if filing it under the right department). He asks what the name "Bena" means and whether a website called fragnastika.com still exists anywhere — the "Fragnastika" thread appears twice in one year, a word he met once and could not let go. He plans a motorcycle trip around Lake Michigan. He wants the best 1/32-scale slot car for winning races, and asks, at the very start of the record, how grip is scaled on slot car tires — which is, for what it's worth, the exact same shore-hardness physics as real race tires, done at the scale of a toy. He knew that. That's why he asked.

Near the end of April, eighteen conversations in, he writes to the machine: *"Hi Rudy it's been a while."* The assistant has acquired a name. In December it will be "hi rudy, how do I feed you a pdf file?" and "rudy, what does the can frame look like to request vin from ecu." He does not anthropomorphize the tool the way enthusiasts do, with ceremony and philosophy. It happens the way it happens with a new coworker: first a shorthand, then a name, then the name is unremarkable. By the end of the archive there are nine named minds in his shop. Rudy was ground zero.

## The December underground

If I had to pick the single most revealing month in the first year, it is not February. It is December 2023, which reads like someone secretly running two lives.

On the surface: rain-drainage rules for roofs, SharePoint column formatting for work, a Christmas grab-bag gift that had to start with the letter L, a tattoo design of flesh torn open to reveal mechanical gears beneath (the self-portrait, if you want my interpretation, is already fully formed two years before he writes the books). But thread the month and a different person emerges. He feeds the machine raw CAN bus logs from a GMLAN vehicle network. He asks it to construct, byte by byte, the CAN frame that requests a VIN from an engine computer. He studies a program called Caring Caribou — a real-world GM service-discovery tool, version 0.4 according to the header he pastes in — and feeds its output into the chat like a case file. He asks it to *study pcmhacking.net*, the vehicle-ECU forum, because he will be asking questions afterward. He writes Node-RED functions to parse frames, rebuilds an old program he wrote "long ago," and reports on Unit32 — "really awesome 24 hours of Lemons" — a race team surviving Road America on junk parts and nerve. There is even C-sharp pasted in that classifies fruit against vegetables, which is exactly the kind of joke this record hides in plain sight.

Nobody who stumbles into car hacking by accident asks for a global GM DBC file. He had been working on vehicles for years — the 2004 Silverado's known electrical faults, the LS3 crank-and-cam signal correlation, the '10 Express van's ECU, the question of whether a VIN can be changed on an E67 module (a question I report without endorsing; the record contains no answer that would concern anyone, and the legal one — his own neighbor's willow-tree liability question from April shows he asks lawyers' questions openly and takes the warnings openly too). The structural detailer, the man of cost estimators and submittal timeframes, was simultaneously a garage electrician-pilot racing beat-up cars and digging through the proprietary nervous system of American trucks with a $20 cable and a forum.

This is the first real contradiction of the portrait, and I want to hold it up carefully because the whole book turns on its kind: the same man who asks, deadpan, for the code-compliance max load of a W8x13, spends his December teaching a chatbot to talk to engine computers — a hobby the professional world would call *fringe*. He feels no need to reconcile them. He has never reconciled anything in this archive, in the sense of abandoning one register for another. He simply refuses to let either one starve. In the later books he will make a philosophy out of precisely this: what he'll call, years from now, "the playground principle" and "stacking curiosity until it compounds." But the data came first. The principle was reverse-engineered from his own behavior, like everything else he believes: he lived it for two years before agreeing with it.

## What the first year teaches about the questioner

By Christmas 2023 the calibration habit has produced its first quiet verdicts. He knows now that the machine can hold AISC numbers. It can write his Python, his Node-RED, his Lisp. It cannot reliably remember his file uploads ("REMEMBER ALL THIS FILE," he types, December, in all caps, the archive's first documented outburst of tool-frustration — three years before he builds the systems that never forget). It will refuse to draw Tinkerbell welding, on policy grounds he finds silly enough to report back twice. He already senses, I think — though the record will not let me prove it — that the interesting thing is not the machine's answers but its edges: what it knows instantly, what it fumbles, what it won't do, what it forgets by morning. The AISC question, the will, the tattoo, the VIN frame: these are not questions. They are depth soundings.

The depth soundings of 2023 mapped a tool. Four years of them — with the machine's replies archived beside them, 53,562 messages of running commentary, argument, joke and revision — will end with something stranger. The tool gets a name. The named tool gets a workshop. The workshop gets a staff. The staff gets a literature. And then, in a message dated November 12, 2026, the man hands the newest of them his archive and asks it to write his biography, in its own words, from its own findings.

But that is the end of the record, and I have a year of 2024 to cross first — the year the questions stopped being only about machines.
