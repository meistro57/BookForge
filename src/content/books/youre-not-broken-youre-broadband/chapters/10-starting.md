# CHAPTER 10
## Starting

There is a specific kind of paralysis that I have never been able to explain to anyone
who doesn't have it.

The task is small. You know exactly how to do it. It will take eleven minutes. It has
been on the list for five weeks. Nothing is stopping you. You are not tired, not busy,
not confused about the steps.

And you cannot start it.

Not "don't want to." Not "keep forgetting." You are sitting there, aware of the task,
wanting it done, fully capable — and the distance between you and beginning is a wall you
cannot get through. Meanwhile you'd cheerfully spend four hours reorganizing something
nobody asked you to touch.

Every person without this wiring who has watched you do it has concluded the same thing.
Laziness. And after enough years of watching yourself do it, you've probably concluded the
same thing, which is worse.

It isn't laziness. Let me show you what it actually is.

---

### The activation problem

Here's the reframe, and it's mechanical rather than moral.

**Your system doesn't run on importance. It runs on activation.**

Most people have a working link between *this matters* and *I am now doing it*. It's not
that they enjoy their paperwork — they don't. It's that "should" generates enough signal to
get the body moving.

That link is weak in us. Knowing something matters — even desperately, even with real
consequences coming — often produces nothing at all. No movement. The importance is fully
understood and completely inert.

What *does* produce movement is roughly four things: **interest, novelty, urgency, and
challenge.** If a task has one of those, you'll do it, sometimes for nine hours straight
without eating. If it has none, it can sit there through three job changes and a house
move.

This is why the same person can build three impossible canopies in 280 hours and not open
a piece of mail for a month. It isn't inconsistency. It's a system that only starts when
one of four switches gets thrown, and "you promised" is not one of the four.

*(The common explanation for this is dopamine — that ADHD brains underproduce it or use it
inefficiently, so unrewarding tasks don't register. That's the story you'll hear
everywhere, including in my own earlier notes. It's more contested than the confident
version sounds, and I'm keeping my Chapter 5 promise not to oversell science I can't
evaluate. Good news: you don't need the mechanism. The **pattern** is checkable against
your own life in about four seconds, and it's the pattern we're going to work with.)*

---

### The one principle underneath everything that works

I've tried a lot of approaches to this over twenty years. The ones that worked all had a
single thing in common, and it took me embarrassingly long to see it.

**You cannot generate the missing signal internally. Stop trying. Import it from
outside.**

That's it. Every effective technique in this chapter is a way of adding urgency, interest,
novelty, or challenge to a task that doesn't have any — from the environment, because it
isn't coming from you.

Twenty years of "try harder" is twenty years of attempting to manufacture the signal by
will. It doesn't work. It has never worked. Not because you're weak, but because willpower
is not the input this system takes.

Once you stop trying to fix yourself and start modifying the *task*, everything gets
easier.

Here's how, in order of how much return you get.

---

### ONE: Body doubling

Work in the presence of another person.

That's the whole technique. They don't help you. They don't check on you. They can be doing
something completely unrelated in the same room, or on a video call saying nothing.

And for reasons I cannot fully explain, the task becomes possible.

This is the highest-return thing in this chapter and it's the one people skip because it
sounds too simple to matter. It works because another person's presence supplies a low
level of accountability and stimulation from outside — exactly the import principle, in its
cheapest form.

If you're alone, there are services built entirely for this, where you get matched with a
stranger on video and you both just work. It sounds ridiculous. It works anyway.

I have done paperwork in a room with someone that I could not do alone in that same room
an hour earlier. Same task, same desk, same me. One variable.

---

### TWO: Shrink the first step until it's stupid

The wall isn't in front of the task. It's in front of *starting*.

So make starting smaller. Then smaller. Then keep going until the step is so small it's
almost embarrassing.

Not "do the taxes." Not "start the taxes." **"Put the folder on the table."**

That's the actual technique, and the reason it works is that once you're in motion you
usually continue — the wall is at the boundary, not throughout. Getting one inch past the
boundary is most of the job.

The test for whether your step is small enough: **if you feel any resistance at all, it's
still too big.** Cut it in half again. There's no floor. "Open the drawer" is a legitimate
first step. I'm not joking.

---

### THREE: Put a boundary on it

An open-ended task has no shape, and a task with no shape lives in the fog forever
(Chapter 9 — it's the same fog).

Give it edges: *"I'll do fifteen minutes."*

Set the timer from the last chapter. When it goes, you're allowed to stop. Genuinely
allowed — this only works if the permission is real, and if you turn it into a trick to
make yourself work an hour, you'll stop believing it and it'll stop working.

The competitive version works even better on some of us: *"how much of this can I get done
in ten minutes?"* That's the **challenge** switch, and it's often the easiest one to throw
deliberately.

---

### FOUR: Pair the boring thing with something stimulating

Music while cleaning. A podcast while folding laundry. A walk while thinking through a
problem.

You're importing interest from a second channel to get one boring channel over the
threshold. Cheap, immediate, and one of the few things on this list that costs nothing to
set up.

Careful with the second channel, though. If it's too good it becomes the activity and the
laundry stays in the basket. Music with no words is usually safer than a show you love.

---

### Things from my notes I'm cutting

I want to be straight about what I'm leaving out and why, because the original version of
this material had thirteen techniques and you deserve to know what got dropped.

**Dopamine-boosting foods.** My earlier notes had a whole section on protein, bananas, and
dark chocolate. I'm cutting it. I'm not a nutritionist, the effect of a banana on your
ability to start a task is nowhere near strong enough to build a chapter on, and I'm not
willing to publish a "eat this to fix your motivation" section that could nudge somebody
into a weird relationship with food. Eat well because eating well is good. It isn't a
technique.

**Gamification with points and levels.** This does work for some people. But I know exactly
what happens to people like us: you spend three hours designing the point system, award
yourself nothing, and never do the task. If you already have a system that works, keep it.
Don't build one now — building it is the procrastination.

**Visualizing the finished result.** The claim is that vividly imagining success triggers
the same reward as achieving it. I'd push back gently. In my experience imagining the
clean garage in detail sometimes *takes the edge off wanting the clean garage*, and I've
seen work suggesting pure positive visualization can reduce follow-through rather than
increase it. If it works for you, use it. I wouldn't lead with it.

**Pretending you're a secret agent.** It was in the notes. I'm forty-plus years past that
one and I suspect you are too.

---

### The thing about shame

One real point from the emotional section, because it's the exception that isn't a trick.

When you can't start something, the automatic move is to get angry at yourself. Lazy.
Pathetic. What is wrong with you. Every one of us has that voice, and most of us think it's
motivating us.

It isn't. Watch what actually happens next time: the shame arrives, and then you do the
task *less*, not more. It adds weight to something already too heavy to lift.

I'm not telling you to love yourself. I'm telling you something more practical: **the
self-attack is a technique that doesn't work, and you've been running it for decades.** On
those grounds alone it's worth dropping. Not because you deserve kindness — though you do —
but because it's a tool with a demonstrated zero success rate.

What to do instead is what this whole chapter is: stop negotiating with yourself and change
the task.

---

### This week

Two things.

**Text one person and ask if they'll work in the same room as you for an hour.** That's
body doubling. It'll feel strange to ask. Ask anyway.

**Take the thing that's been on your list longest and write down a first step so small it
embarrasses you.** Then do that one step and stop. You're allowed to stop. That's the point.

Next chapter is the one that costs the most in relationships — what happens when the
feelings arrive at four times the expected volume, and what to do in the ninety seconds
before you say something you can't take back.
