# CHAPTER 15
## Work, and the Job You Don't Hate

Most books like this one arrive here and tell you to start a business.

The logic goes: traditional workplaces are rigid, entrepreneurship is dynamic, ADHD people
are natural risk-takers and visionaries, so go build something. Then a list of famous
founders who have it.

I want to be careful with this, because it isn't entirely wrong and it has ruined people.

Here's what that advice leaves out. Running your own business is roughly thirty percent
the exciting part — the vision, the pitch, the building — and seventy percent the part that
will destroy you: invoicing, bookkeeping, tax filings, compliance, chasing payment,
following up when you said you would. Long stretches with no urgency until suddenly
catastrophic urgency. Nobody supplying structure from outside. Full responsibility for a
sequence you can't feel the shape of.

That is a nearly perfect list of the things this book has spent thirteen chapters
explaining that we're bad at.

Some people with this wiring thrive at it. Most of the ones you've heard of had a partner
or a hire doing the seventy percent from early on, and that detail rarely makes the
chapter.

**And here's the thing I'd point at hardest: I never started a business.**

Thirty years in steel, all of it working for somebody else. I'm an employee. I found work
that fits inside an ordinary job, in an ordinary trade, with a paycheck and a boss. That's
available to almost everyone reading this. Founding a company is not.

So let's talk about fit instead.

---

### What makes a job fit

Forget job titles. Titles tell you almost nothing. What matters is the **shape of the day**,
and it comes down to about six things.

**Does the work repeat, or does it vary?** Same thing every day is the single most
reliable predictor of failure for us. Not because we're too good for it — because
repetition removes novelty, and novelty is one of the four switches.

**How fast is the feedback?** Can you tell within minutes whether what you did was right,
or do you find out in a quarterly review? Fast feedback keeps the system engaged. Slow
feedback is a fog you'll drift out of.

**Is your body involved?** Standing, moving, handling things, walking somewhere. A job
where you sit still for eight hours is fighting your hardware all day. A job where you're
moving is quietly doing half the regulation for you.

**Do you control the sequence?** Being told *what* to accomplish is fine. Being told the
exact order and pace to accomplish it in is where we die. Autonomy over sequence matters
more than autonomy over hours.

**Is there real urgency, at least sometimes?** Not constant crisis — that's a different
kind of damage. But work with genuine stakes and deadlines that are actually felt supplies
the switch you can't throw yourself.

**Is it problems, or is it procedure?** Work where something has to be figured out will
catch you. Work where the answer is known and you just execute it will not.

Score any job you've had against those six. I'd bet the ones you were good at score high
and the ones that broke you score low — and I'd bet it had nothing to do with how much you
cared.

---

### My own two jobs

I've had two careers in the same trade and they fit for different reasons, which is useful
because it shows there isn't one right shape.

**Fabrication** scored on the physical and the immediate. Hands on metal, standing all day,
constant variation, and you know within minutes whether the cut is right. Loud, fast, and
the consequences of being wrong are visible immediately. That's about as friendly an
environment as this wiring gets, and I was good at it from nearly the first week — not
because I'd learned discipline but because nothing in that job required the thing I don't
have.

**Detailing** — what I do now — is desk work, which should be a disaster. It isn't, and the
reason is instructive.

The work is *problems*. Every job is a new building with new connections that have to be
figured out. Nothing repeats. There's constant sequencing pressure and real consequences —
get it wrong and steel arrives on site that doesn't fit, with my name on the drawing. And
crucially, I control the order I work in.

It's a desk job that happens to score four out of six.

That's the point I want you to take: **it isn't about finding a "creative" job or a
"flexible" job.** Those are marketing words. It's about the mechanics of the day.

---

### The ladder

Most advice in this territory assumes you can quit. Most people can't. So here's the range
of moves in order of cost, and I'd genuinely start at the top.

**Level 1: Change how you do the job, not the job.**
Move the recurring work to your best hours. Ask for the meeting-heavy day to be one day
instead of scattered. Take calls walking. Get the standing desk. Batch the boring things
into one dreaded block instead of letting them leak across the week. None of this needs
permission from anyone.

**Level 2: Change what you do inside the same job.**
Almost every role has parts that fit you and parts that don't, and almost every team has
somebody whose preferences are the mirror image of yours. Trade. I have swapped work with
colleagues for years — I take the gnarly puzzle nobody wants, they take the routine
tracking that would kill me. No manager was ever involved. Everyone was happier.

**Level 3: Change roles within your field.**
This is what I did. Same industry, thirty years of accumulated knowledge intact, completely
different daily shape. An engineer told me I'd make a good detailer and he was right — and
I kept everything I'd learned in the shop.

Look for the version of your field that's closer to problems and further from procedure.
Nursing has floor work and it has emergency work. Teaching has lectures and it has shop
class. Law has document review and it has the courtroom. Same credentials, different
hardware requirements.

**Level 4: Change fields.** Expensive, slow, sometimes necessary. Not first.

**Level 5: Work for yourself.** Highest ceiling, highest floor, and only if you've got a
plan for the seventy percent.

### If you're stuck where you are

Some of you can't move. Mortgage, family, health insurance, a town with three employers.
I'm not going to pretend otherwise, and any book that assumes you can just leave isn't
talking to you.

Three things that help, honestly.

**Protect one part of the day.** One hour where the work matches you — the problem-solving
part, the part you're good at. Defend it like it's a meeting. It's what keeps you from
concluding you're bad at everything.

**Get the fit somewhere else.** If the job can't supply novelty and problems, get them from
something outside it. This is not a consolation prize. A person who's engaged somewhere in
their life is a different person at work.

**Name the trade honestly.** If you're staying in a job that doesn't fit because it pays
for a life you want, that's a legitimate choice made by an adult. What corrodes you isn't
the job — it's staying while believing that struggling in it proves something is wrong with
you. It doesn't. It proves the six things above score low.

---

### On telling them

Should you disclose at work?

I can't answer that for you — it depends entirely on your workplace, your field, and your
manager, and anyone giving a universal answer isn't being straight with you.

What I'll say is this: **you usually don't have to.** Most of what you need is available by
asking for the thing rather than explaining the reason. "I do my best detail work in the
morning, can we move the check-in to afternoons" is a normal professional request. "I have
ADHD, so..." invites a conversation about you rather than about the work.

Ask for the accommodation, not the understanding. You'll get it more often and it costs you
nothing.

### The reframe that took me thirty years

For most of my working life I thought the goal was to become the kind of person who could
do any job properly.

That was never available. Nobody's the kind of person who can do any job — everyone else
just happened to be born matching the default, so nobody ever made them notice.

**The goal isn't becoming employable everywhere. It's finding the room where what you
already are turns out to be exactly what's needed.**

I found mine twice, in the same trade, by accident both times.

You can do it on purpose. That's the only advantage I can offer you over how I did it.
