# Chapter Twenty-Nine

## Recognising Patterns Without Inventing Them

Why the human mind finds connections everywhere, how meaningful discoveries can become tangled with coincidence, and why the ability to question a beautiful pattern may be just as important as the ability to recognise one.

### The Pattern in the Steel

Imagine a steel detailer examining a complicated connection.

The assembly contains plates, bolts, welds, stiffeners, and several intersecting members.

At first glance, the geometry looks complicated.

But something catches his attention.

Three components appear to share an alignment.

He rotates the model.

Examines the dimensions.

And notices that the alignment continues through another part of the assembly.

An idea begins forming.

Perhaps the geometry can be simplified.

Perhaps several pieces can be fabricated using a common reference.

Perhaps an entire sequence of operations can be reduced.

He becomes excited.

The pattern is beautiful.

It suggests an elegant solution.

Now imagine that he begins modifying the design around that apparent relationship.

He adjusts a plate.

Changes a dimension.

Repositions a stiffener.

Everything seems to fit.

Until he examines the connection from another direction.

One component doesn't actually share the alignment.

Its apparent position was created by the viewing angle.

The pattern was partly an illusion.

Now the detailer faces a choice.

He can preserve the elegant explanation and attempt to force the geometry to fit.

Or he can acknowledge that the original interpretation was incomplete.

The second choice may be frustrating.

But it creates the possibility of a better design.

This is a familiar problem in engineering.

A relationship appears.

We construct an explanation.

Then new information reveals that the relationship isn't what we thought.

But the same process occurs when we investigate ourselves.

We notice recurring experiences.

Repeated behaviours.

Similar emotional responses.

Unexpected connections between events.

And we begin constructing patterns.

Some are meaningful.

Some are incomplete.

Some are coincidences.

And some exist primarily because we've selected observations that make them appear convincing.

The challenge is not to stop recognising patterns.

That would be impossible, and undesirable.

Pattern recognition is essential to learning.

The challenge is to recognise when a pattern deserves confidence.

A pattern is a proposal about relationships in the evidence. It becomes useful when it survives attempts to discover where it fails.

That's our investigation.

### 1. The Mind Is a Relationship-Finding Machine

Imagine walking through a forest.

You notice a familiar shape among the branches.

A face.

You look again.

It's only a collection of shadows and leaves.

Or perhaps you hear a sound that resembles your name.

You turn.

Nobody called you.

These experiences illustrate how perception can organise ambiguous information into familiar patterns.

The same tendency appears in more abstract thinking.

We notice similarities between events.

Connect experiences across time.

Recognise recurring structures.

And use those relationships to make predictions.

These abilities are valuable.

Without them, learning would be extraordinarily difficult.

We couldn't easily recognise familiar objects.

Anticipate consequences.

Or transfer knowledge from one situation to another.

But pattern recognition has limitations.

The mind can detect relationships that aren't actually present.

Or exaggerate weak relationships.

Or overlook differences that matter.

The Meistro Method encourages us to treat pattern recognition as a source of hypotheses.

Not automatic proof.

### 2. The Difference Between Seeing and Establishing

Imagine noticing that several of your best ideas appeared during walks.

You recognise a pattern.

Now consider what you've established.

You've remembered several occasions when walking and useful ideas occurred together.

That's meaningful.

But what about the walks when no interesting idea appeared?

What about ideas developed while sitting at a desk?

What about the influence of conversation, mood, or accumulated knowledge?

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish pattern detection from pattern evaluation.

Detection asks:

"Do these events seem related?"

Evaluation asks:

"How strong is the relationship, and what else could explain it?"

Both are necessary.

Without detection, we may overlook important possibilities.

Without evaluation, we may become convinced by coincidences.

The goal is to allow the first process to generate possibilities and the second to examine them.

### 3. The Meistro Case Study: The Repository Constellation

Consider a collection of approximately ninety-nine GitHub repositories.

Each repository represents some combination of interests, tools, experiments, and development work.

Now imagine arranging them chronologically.

At first, the history may look chaotic.

Different languages.

Different frameworks.

Different technical domains.

Some projects are small.

Others are substantial.

Some were completed.

Others remain experimental.

But as we examine the history, relationships may begin to appear.

Perhaps certain programming languages become more common.

Perhaps automation grows more important.

Perhaps early scripts evolve into larger systems.

Perhaps AI-related projects begin connecting previously separate areas of work.

These would be interesting patterns.

But we must be careful.

A repository's creation date doesn't necessarily reveal when the underlying skill was learned.

A language appearing in a project doesn't establish mastery.

A large repository isn't automatically more sophisticated than a small one.

And an abandoned project doesn't necessarily represent failure.

The Meistro Method encourages us to examine what the available evidence actually supports.

A repository history can reveal technological activity.

It may provide clues about learning.

But reconstructing a person's development requires more than counting projects.

We need context.

Commit histories.

Project purposes.

Dependencies.

And, where available, the person's own account of what they were attempting.

The constellation may reveal a trajectory.

But we shouldn't draw connecting lines merely because the stars are there.

### 4. The Problem of the Missing Events

Now imagine remembering five occasions when you had an excellent idea during a conversation.

You conclude that conversation reliably produces your best thinking.

But how many conversations produced no important idea?

How many useful ideas emerged alone?

How many conversations generated ideas that initially seemed exciting but later proved unhelpful?

Without those observations, the pattern may be distorted.

This is a form of selection bias.

We preserve memorable successes.

We overlook ordinary outcomes.

And the resulting collection may make a relationship appear stronger than it is.

The Meistro Method encourages us to examine the denominator.

Not merely how many successes occurred.

But how many opportunities existed for success or failure.

If five useful ideas emerged from six conversations, that's a different pattern from five useful ideas emerging from two hundred conversations.

The number of successes matters.

But so does the number of attempts.

### 5. The Pattern That Appears Because You Started Looking

Imagine buying a particular model of car.

Suddenly, you notice that model everywhere.

At intersections.

In parking lots.

On highways.

Did the number of those cars increase?

Probably not because you bought one.

Your attention changed.

The car became personally relevant.

And you began noticing examples that previously blended into the background.

A similar effect can occur during self-investigation.

Suppose you begin studying visual thinking.

You may notice visual imagery more frequently.

You may remember examples more readily.

And you may interpret ambiguous experiences through the new framework.

The Meistro Method encourages us to ask:

Did the pattern become more common?

Or did my attention become more sensitive to it?

The distinction can be difficult to establish.

But recognising the possibility prevents us from treating increased noticing as automatic evidence of increased occurrence.

### 6. The Meistro Case Study: Discovering a Cognitive Pattern

Mark has described experiences involving visual imagery, associative thinking, and rapid connections between ideas.

These observations may reveal meaningful characteristics of his cognitive experience.

But the Meistro Method encourages us to examine them carefully.

If we begin asking questions about visual thought, we may collect many examples.

If we begin asking about verbal reasoning, we may discover examples of that too.

If we investigate emotional influences, still other patterns may emerge.

The resulting picture depends partly on what we choose to examine.

This doesn't mean the observations are false.

It means the investigation must account for selective attention.

A useful research question might be:

"Across a variety of tasks, what forms of conscious thought does Mark report, and how do those reports vary with the task?"

That question allows multiple patterns to emerge.

Rather than assuming one description must explain everything.

### 7. Coincidence Is Not a Defect in Reality

Now consider something that often feels mysterious.

Coincidence.

Two people mention the same unusual subject on the same day.

You think about an old acquaintance.

They contact you.

You discover that two apparently unrelated projects use a similar design principle.

The experience can feel remarkable.

Sometimes it is.

But coincidence doesn't automatically establish a hidden causal relationship.

When many events occur, some surprising combinations are inevitable.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish surprise from evidence.

How many opportunities existed for the coincidence?

How specific was the match?

Was the prediction made before the event?

Or did the relationship become apparent afterwards?

And what ordinary explanations might account for it?

The goal isn't to dismiss meaningful experiences.

It's to understand what the evidence actually establishes.

### 8. The Birthday Problem

Here's a classic example from probability.

Imagine a room containing twenty-three people.

What is the probability that at least two share a birthday?

Assuming birthdays are distributed approximately evenly across 365 days, and ignoring leap-day complications, the probability is slightly greater than fifty percent.

That surprises many people.

Why?

Because we tend to imagine comparing one person's birthday with everyone else's.

But the actual question concerns any pair in the room.

Twenty-three people create 253 possible pairs.

That's a lot of opportunities for a match.

The Meistro Method encourages us to remember this when evaluating coincidences.

The number of possible comparisons matters.

A surprising match may be less surprising when we account for all the opportunities for it to occur.

And when we're examining a lifetime of experiences, the number of possible relationships is enormous.

### 9. The Story We Construct Afterwards

Imagine experiencing an unexpected event.

Later, you remember an earlier conversation that seems related.

You connect the two.

The relationship feels meaningful.

But did you predict the event?

Or did you recognise the similarity afterwards?

This distinction matters.

A prediction made before an event can be tested.

A relationship identified afterwards may still be interesting.

But it has more opportunities for selective interpretation.

The Meistro Method encourages us to preserve the timing.

What did I believe before the event?

What did I notice afterwards?

And how did the interpretation change?

This is one reason written predictions are valuable.

They help distinguish genuine anticipation from retrospective pattern construction.

### 10. The Meistro Case Study: Ideas That Arrive Together

Imagine two collaborators independently mentioning similar ideas within a short period.

The coincidence may feel exciting.

Perhaps they share interests.

Perhaps they've been exposed to similar information.

Perhaps earlier conversations created a common conceptual foundation.

Or perhaps the timing is coincidental.

The Meistro Method encourages us to investigate the relationship without assuming one explanation.

What exactly was similar?

How specific was the idea?

What prior information did each person possess?

How many other ideas were discussed during the same period?

And were the similarities recorded before the participants compared them?

These questions can help distinguish shared context, convergent reasoning, and coincidence.

The emotional significance of the experience may be genuine.

But its causal explanation remains a separate question.

### 11. The Pattern That Explains Too Much

Now consider a theory that appears capable of explaining everything.

Suppose someone proposes:

"All my behaviour is driven by curiosity."

They begin finding examples.

They explore technology because they're curious.

They ask questions because they're curious.

They change projects because they're curious.

They even avoid certain tasks because they're curious about something else.

The theory seems powerful.

But perhaps it's becoming too flexible.

If every possible behaviour can be explained through curiosity, what would count as evidence against the theory?

The Meistro Method encourages us to examine whether an explanation makes discriminating predictions.

What would we expect if the theory were true?

What would we expect if it were false?

And what observations could distinguish the possibilities?

An explanation that fits everything may tell us very little.

### 12. The Difference Between a Useful Metaphor and a Mechanism

Earlier in this book, we introduced the cognitive mesh.

The metaphor suggests that ideas may be connected through networks of knowledge, experience, and association.

It's useful because it helps us imagine relationships.

But a metaphor isn't automatically a scientific mechanism.

The brain does contain networks of neurons.

Cognition involves distributed activity.

And learning changes relationships within neural systems.

But those facts don't establish that every feature of our cognitive-mesh metaphor corresponds directly to a particular neural process.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish conceptual usefulness from biological explanation.

A metaphor can help generate questions.

It can guide observations.

And it can suggest experiments.

But it must not be treated as proof of an underlying mechanism.

The same applies to metaphors involving engines, computers, electrical circuits, or structural connections.

They may illuminate relationships.

But they also have limits.

### 13. The Danger of Pattern Completion

Imagine looking at a partially completed drawing.

Several lines suggest a familiar shape.

Your mind anticipates the missing geometry.

You can almost see the completed object.

This ability is useful.

It supports recognition.

Prediction.

And efficient interpretation.

But it can also lead to error.

You may assume the missing details match the familiar pattern.

When the actual geometry differs, your expectation may interfere with noticing the discrepancy.

Something similar happens in personal narratives.

We know part of a person's history.

We recognise a familiar pattern.

And we begin filling in the missing details.

Perhaps we assume motives.

Emotions.

Or intentions.

The Meistro Method encourages us to identify where the evidence ends and inference begins.

What do we actually know?

What are we assuming?

And what information would be needed to complete the picture accurately?

### 14. The Meistro Case Study: Reverse-Engineering Without Inventing

The Reverse-Engineering Meistro project contains a long history of experiences, technical work, and self-observations.

It's tempting to connect everything into one elegant explanation.

Perhaps childhood mechanical curiosity led directly to programming.

Perhaps visual thinking explains every technical achievement.

Perhaps a single motivational pattern accounts for the entire career.

These stories may contain meaningful relationships.

But development is complicated.

Opportunities matter.

Other people matter.

Economic circumstances matter.

Accidents matter.

And choices occur within changing environments.

The Meistro Method encourages us to resist reducing a life to one explanatory principle.

We can investigate recurring themes.

But we should preserve complexity.

A useful personal model explains patterns while acknowledging exceptions.

It doesn't require every event to fit one story.

### 15. The Counterexample Hunt

Now let's introduce one of the most useful tools in this chapter.

The counterexample hunt.

Suppose you believe:

"I do my best thinking while walking."

Instead of collecting more examples that support the belief, deliberately search for cases that challenge it.

When did you develop a useful idea while sitting?

When did walking produce no meaningful insight?

When did a conversation help more than movement?

And when did deliberate concentration produce a breakthrough?

The Meistro Method encourages us to treat counterexamples as valuable evidence.

They may reveal that the original pattern is weaker than expected.

Or they may help identify its boundaries.

Perhaps walking supports certain kinds of thinking.

But not others.

That's a more useful conclusion than a universal claim.

### 16. The Difference Between Exceptions and Disproof

Now consider an important complication.

A counterexample doesn't always invalidate a pattern.

Suppose you notice that interruptions usually make complex work harder.

Then one day, an interruption helps you solve a problem.

Does that disprove the pattern?

Not necessarily.

Perhaps the interruption provided useful information.

Perhaps the task was different.

Perhaps the person offered a relevant insight.

The Meistro Method encourages us to examine exceptions rather than automatically treating them as decisive.

What made this case different?

Does the exception reveal a missing condition?

Or does it challenge the broader relationship?

A useful pattern often includes boundaries.

It may describe what tends to happen under particular conditions.

Not what must happen every time.

### 17. The Problem of Small Samples

Imagine conducting three experiments.

In all three, a new method works.

You conclude that the method is reliable.

But three observations may not capture ordinary variation.

Perhaps the tasks were unusually similar.

Perhaps the conditions were favourable.

Perhaps the result occurred by chance.

The Meistro Method encourages us to match confidence to the amount and quality of evidence.

Small samples can generate useful hypotheses.

But they often support limited conclusions.

The exact amount of evidence required depends on the question.

A large, consistent effect may become apparent quickly.

A small, variable effect may require many observations.

And some questions cannot be answered reliably through informal self-experimentation.

The goal isn't to demand enormous datasets for every personal decision.

It's to recognise the limits of small samples.

### 18. The Pattern That Changes Over Time

Now imagine discovering that a particular routine improves concentration.

You use it successfully for several months.

Then its effectiveness declines.

Was the original pattern false?

Not necessarily.

Perhaps your circumstances changed.

Perhaps the tasks became different.

Perhaps the routine became less engaging.

Or perhaps other factors began influencing the outcome.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise that patterns can change.

A relationship observed during one period may not remain stable indefinitely.

This is especially important when studying human behaviour.

People learn.

Adapt.

Age.

And respond to changing environments.

A personal operating manual should therefore remain revisable.

What worked last year may not be the best approach today.

### 19. The Meistro Case Study: The Technology Curve

Now return to the repository history.

Suppose we discover that Mark's projects gradually incorporate more automation and AI-related tools.

That would suggest a technological trend.

But what caused it?

Perhaps growing expertise.

Perhaps the availability of new technologies.

Perhaps changes in work requirements.

Perhaps collaboration.

Perhaps curiosity.

Or perhaps several factors interacted.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish the observed trend from its explanation.

The repositories may reveal what changed.

Interviews and historical context may help explain why.

And comparisons with broader technological developments may reveal which changes were personal and which reflected industry trends.

A meaningful technology curve requires more than plotting repository counts.

It requires understanding what those repositories represent.

### 20. The Pattern Confidence Ladder

Let's create a practical framework.

## The Pattern Confidence Ladder

1. Interesting coincidence

One or two events appear related. Worth noticing, but little is established.
2. Recurring observation

The relationship appears repeatedly, although the full range of cases may be unknown.
3. Documented association

Records show that the events occur together more often under certain conditions.
4. Tested relationship

Deliberate comparisons support the association, while alternative explanations have been considered.
5. Well-supported working model

The relationship has survived repeated testing across relevant conditions and makes useful predictions.

This is a practical reasoning aid, not a formal scientific grading system. Evidence quality matters at every level, and confidence may move downward as well as upward.

The Meistro Method encourages us to identify where a pattern currently stands.

Not every observation needs to reach the highest level.

Some are useful as possibilities.

Others guide practical decisions.

But the language we use should reflect the strength of the evidence.

### 21. The Emotional Appeal of Patterns

Now let's examine why patterns can be so compelling.

A pattern creates order.

It connects events.

Reduces uncertainty.

And offers an explanation.

Sometimes it also creates a sense of meaning.

Imagine discovering that several difficult experiences share a common theme.

The relationship may help you understand your history.

It may reveal a recurring challenge.

Or suggest a practical change.

That can be emotionally powerful.

But emotional significance doesn't establish causal accuracy.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish the meaning a pattern holds for us from the evidence supporting its explanation.

A relationship can be personally meaningful even when its cause remains uncertain.

And an explanation can be factually accurate without carrying much emotional significance.

These dimensions should not be confused.

### 22. The Difference Between Meaning and Mechanism

Imagine a person who associates a particular song with an important period of life.

Hearing the song produces vivid memories.

The association is meaningful.

But it doesn't require a mysterious mechanism.

Memory, emotion, and repeated experience can create powerful associations.

Now imagine two events that seem symbolically connected.

The person may find meaning in the relationship.

That meaning can influence reflection.

Art.

Or personal narrative.

But symbolic meaning doesn't automatically establish a physical causal connection.

The Meistro Method encourages us to respect personal meaning while investigating causal claims separately.

This distinction allows curiosity to remain open without abandoning standards of evidence.

### 23. The Pattern That Becomes an Identity

Now consider a recurring observation.

You often begin projects enthusiastically.

Some remain unfinished.

You notice the pattern.

Then you conclude:

"I'm someone who never finishes anything."

The pattern has become an identity.

But perhaps the evidence supports a more specific description.

You complete projects with external deadlines.

You finish tasks that have clear requirements.

You leave some exploratory prototypes unfinished after learning what you wanted to know.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish behavioural patterns from global identity judgements.

What exactly recurs?

Under what conditions?

What exceptions exist?

And what practical consequences follow?

The goal isn't to eliminate self-descriptions.

It's to prevent a limited pattern from becoming an unnecessarily broad definition of the person.

### 24. The Meistro Case Study: The Danger of a Perfect Theory

Imagine completing the Reverse-Engineering Meistro project.

You have hundreds of observations.

A detailed timeline.

Cognitive models.

Emotional patterns.

Learning histories.

And a personal operating manual.

Now imagine that everything fits perfectly.

Every event supports the theory.

Every contradiction is explained away.

Every new observation confirms the existing model.

That would be suspicious.

Not because a coherent theory is automatically wrong.

But because a complex human life contains variation.

Unexpected influences.

Changing circumstances.

And incomplete information.

The Meistro Method encourages us to preserve unresolved questions.

A useful model should identify where it works.

Where it fails.

And what remains unknown.

The purpose isn't to construct a theory that cannot be challenged.

It's to develop one that improves through challenge.

### 26. The Question of Prediction

Now let's introduce one of the strongest tests of a pattern.

Can it predict something that hasn't happened yet?

Imagine believing that uninterrupted work periods improve your ability to complete complicated modelling tasks.

You examine previous experiences.

The pattern seems plausible.

Now make a prediction.

"During the next two weeks, tasks completed during scheduled uninterrupted periods will require less recovery time than comparable tasks performed during ordinary periods."

Then collect the observations.

The result may support the prediction.

Or challenge it.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish retrospective explanation from prospective prediction.

A pattern that helps anticipate future outcomes may be more useful than one that merely organises past events.

But prediction isn't the only measure of value.

Some personal insights concern meaning or history rather than repeatable behaviour.

The appropriate standard depends on the claim.

### 27. The Pattern That Survives Being Wrong

Here's an interesting possibility.

Suppose your theory predicts that a particular condition will improve performance.

You test it.

The prediction fails.

Now what?

You could abandon the theory.

Or revise it.

Perhaps the effect occurs only during certain tasks.

Perhaps the intervention requires practice.

Perhaps the original mechanism was wrong.

The Meistro Method encourages us to examine failed predictions carefully.

A theory may become more useful when its boundaries are clarified.

But we must avoid endlessly adding exceptions merely to preserve it.

At some point, a simpler alternative explanation may fit the evidence better.

The goal isn't to save the theory.

It's to improve understanding.

### 28. The Twenty-Ninth Meistro Principle

We're now ready to establish the twenty-ninth principle.

Principle Twenty-Nine: Recognise patterns freely, but earn confidence by examining counterexamples, missing information, and alternative explanations.

Pattern recognition is essential to learning.

It allows us to connect experiences.

Develop expectations.

And discover relationships.

But the same ability can produce illusions.

Coincidences.

Selective memories.

And explanations that fit only because we've chosen favourable evidence.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish noticing from establishing.

What pattern appears?

How often does it occur?

What evidence is missing?

What exceptions exist?

What alternative explanations remain?

And what future observation could challenge the conclusion?

The purpose isn't to become suspicious of every insight.

It's to give our insights the opportunity to become more accurate.

### 29. The Steel Connection, One More Time

Let's return to the steel detailer.

He noticed an alignment.

It suggested an elegant solution.

But when he rotated the model, the apparent relationship disappeared.

One component wasn't where he thought it was.

The original pattern was incomplete.

Now imagine what happens next.

He doesn't abandon the entire project.

He doesn't conclude that pattern recognition is useless.

He examines the geometry more carefully.

Identifies the actual relationships.

And develops a different solution.

Perhaps the revised design is less elegant.

Perhaps it's more complicated.

But it fits the evidence.

And that's what matters.

The Meistro Method encourages us to approach our own minds in the same spirit.

We will notice patterns.

Some will be beautiful.

Some will feel deeply meaningful.

Some will explain experiences that have puzzled us for years.

But we must remain willing to rotate the model.

Examine it from another direction.

Look for the component that doesn't fit.

And revise the explanation when necessary.

Because the purpose of the investigation isn't to preserve the first pattern we discover.

It's to understand what is actually there.

## Chapter Twenty-Nine: Field Assignment

### The Seven-Day Pattern Challenge

Choose one recurring pattern you believe exists in your life.

Make it specific.

For example:

"I concentrate better when interruptions are limited."

Now write down your current evidence.

Then spend seven days looking for both supporting observations and counterexamples.

Record when the pattern appears.

When it doesn't.

And what conditions differ.

Include ordinary outcomes.

Don't record only memorable successes.

At the end of the week, examine the evidence.

Does the pattern remain plausible?

Is it more specific than you originally believed?

What alternative explanations remain?

And what prediction could you test next?

Finally, write a conclusion that reflects the evidence.

Perhaps:

"During this week, I noticed that interruptions were associated with longer recovery periods during complex modelling tasks. The relationship was less apparent during routine work. I haven't established the cause, but task complexity may be an important condition."

That's a useful result.

It preserves the pattern.

Identifies a possible boundary.

And creates a better question for the next experiment.

### A Final Thought: The Beauty of Being Wrong

There's something wonderful about discovering a pattern.

The moment when scattered observations suddenly appear connected.

When a problem becomes understandable.

When a relationship emerges that we hadn't noticed before.

Those moments can be exhilarating.

They motivate curiosity.

Encourage exploration.

And sometimes lead to genuine breakthroughs.

But there's another kind of discovery.

The moment when we realise that our explanation was wrong.

Perhaps the evidence doesn't fit.

Perhaps a counterexample changes everything.

Perhaps a simpler explanation becomes available.

That moment can be disappointing.

But it can also be liberating.

Because an inaccurate explanation, however beautiful, limits what we can understand.

And abandoning it creates room for something better.

The Meistro Method encourages us to value both experiences.

The excitement of recognising a pattern.

And the intellectual courage required to revise it.

Because learning doesn't consist only of adding new knowledge.

Sometimes it requires removing an explanation that no longer fits.

And that brings us to the next chapter.

Contradictions.

Surprises.

And changing your mind.

What happens when evidence challenges a belief that has become important to us?

Why can changing our minds feel like losing part of ourselves?

And how can we develop a relationship with uncertainty that makes revision possible?

That's where the living laboratory goes next.
