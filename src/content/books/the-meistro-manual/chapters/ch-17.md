# Chapter Seventeen

## Intuition: Knowing Before You Know Why

The strange experience of recognising an answer before you can explain it, how experience becomes rapid judgement, and why learning to investigate your instincts may be more valuable than simply learning to trust them.

### The Connection That Looked Wrong

Imagine a steel detailer examining a complicated assembly.

The drawings are complete enough to begin work.

The dimensions appear reasonable.

The components are familiar.

Nothing immediately announces itself as a problem.

But the detailer stops.

Something doesn't look right.

He zooms in.

Rotates the model.

Examines the connection from another direction.

Still, he cannot immediately identify the issue.

Someone asks what he's looking for.

He hesitates.

"I don't know yet. Something's off."

Now imagine two possible outcomes.

In the first, he continues investigating and discovers that a plate interferes with an adjacent component.

The initial impression was useful.

In the second, he checks the geometry and finds that everything is correct.

The initial impression was mistaken.

Both outcomes begin with the same subjective experience.

A feeling that something is wrong.

But only one produces a verified problem.

This distinction is fundamental.

An intuitive impression is an event in the mind. Its accuracy is a separate question.

During our investigations, Mark has described something he calls his first feeling.

A sense of direction.

An impression that an idea is worth following.

Sometimes a recognition that something fits.

Sometimes a concern that something doesn't.

He has described allowing certain decisions to develop rather than forcing an immediate conclusion.

That experience is worth investigating.

But what does it mean?

Is the first feeling a product of accumulated experience?

A response to subtle information?

A preference shaped by emotion?

An expectation?

A guess?

Or a combination of processes?

And how could we tell the difference?

The answer begins with understanding what intuition actually is.

### 1. The Problem With the Word Intuition

Intuition is one of those words that seems clear until someone asks us to define it.

We use it to describe many experiences.

A mechanic hears a machine and suspects a fault.

A musician anticipates the next note.

A chess player recognises a promising move.

A parent notices a change in a child's behaviour.

A designer senses that an arrangement is unbalanced.

A person meets someone new and experiences an immediate impression.

All these experiences may be called intuition.

But they don't necessarily arise through the same processes.

Some may depend on extensive domain-specific learning.

Others may involve rapid emotional evaluation.

Others may reflect familiar associations.

Some may be influenced by stereotypes.

And some may be guesses that happen to feel convincing.

The Meistro Method therefore avoids treating intuition as one mysterious faculty.

Instead, we investigate particular intuitive experiences.

What judgement appeared?

How quickly?

What information was available?

What previous experience might have been relevant?

How confident did the person feel?

And what happened when the judgement was tested?

These questions transform intuition from an explanation into something we can examine.

Because saying "I used my intuition" tells us surprisingly little about how the judgement was produced.

### 2. The Knowledge You Didn't Know You Were Using

Consider an experienced mechanic.

A machine begins operating.

The mechanic listens.

After a few seconds, they suspect a bearing problem.

They haven't dismantled anything.

They haven't measured the components.

They haven't performed a complete diagnosis.

But something about the sound seems familiar.

Now imagine asking:

"How did you know?"

The mechanic might answer:

"I've heard that before."

Or perhaps:

"It just doesn't sound right."

The second explanation may feel mysterious.

But the underlying process could involve learned associations.

Through repeated experience, the mechanic has encountered different operating conditions.

They've heard normal sounds.

Abnormal sounds.

Failures.

Repairs.

And the changes that occur when problems are corrected.

Over time, certain patterns may become recognisable.

The mechanic may not consciously compare every feature.

But their judgement may still depend on experience.

This is one way intuition can develop.

Repeated exposure to meaningful patterns can support rapid recognition.

But there's an important qualification.

Experience alone doesn't guarantee accurate intuition.

The environment must provide useful information.

The person must have opportunities to learn from outcomes.

And feedback must be sufficiently reliable.

Someone can accumulate years of experience while reinforcing incorrect assumptions.

The Meistro Method asks not merely how long someone has performed an activity, but what they've learned from it.

### 3. The Meistro Case Study: A Lifetime of Mechanical Feedback

Mark's technical history provides an interesting opportunity to investigate learned intuition.

His experience spans practical mechanical work, fabrication, structural detailing, programming, and software systems.

These domains differ.

But many provide relatively concrete feedback.

A machine operates or fails.

A component fits or interferes.

A program executes or produces an error.

A model reveals a geometric conflict.

A fabrication method works or requires revision.

The feedback isn't always immediate or unambiguous.

But compared with some other domains, technical work can offer substantial opportunities to compare predictions with observable outcomes.

This may matter.

Imagine encountering hundreds of mechanical arrangements over many years.

Certain relationships become familiar.

Certain errors recur.

Certain design decisions produce predictable complications.

A person may begin recognising those patterns more rapidly.

What once required deliberate analysis may eventually produce an immediate impression.

But we shouldn't assume that every intuitive judgement Mark makes is based on technical expertise.

An intuition about a steel connection and an intuition about another person's intentions involve different evidence.

Success in one domain doesn't automatically establish accuracy in another.

This is one of the most important lessons of the chapter.

Intuition may be highly useful within a familiar domain without becoming a general-purpose detector of truth.

### 4. The Environment That Trains the Instinct

Imagine learning to throw a ball into a basket.

You throw.

The ball misses.

You adjust.

You throw again.

The ball lands closer.

You repeat the process.

Over time, your movements may become more accurate.

You no longer need to calculate every angle consciously.

Your performance reflects learning through repeated interaction with the environment.

Now imagine trying to predict the outcome of an event influenced by thousands of hidden variables.

You make a prediction.

The outcome occurs months later.

But you cannot determine exactly which factors caused it.

Feedback is delayed.

Incomplete.

And difficult to interpret.

Developing reliable intuition in the second environment is much harder.

This distinction is important.

Some environments are sufficiently regular that experience can support skilled intuitive judgement.

Others are noisy, unpredictable, or difficult to learn from.

Psychologists have studied the conditions under which intuitive expertise develops, emphasising the importance of valid cues and opportunities for learning.

The Meistro Method turns this into a practical question.

What kind of environment trained this particular intuition?

If you're making a judgement about a familiar mechanical system, your experience may provide useful evidence.

If you're predicting how a stranger will behave, your confidence may exceed what the available information supports.

If you're evaluating a complex social situation, feedback may be ambiguous.

If you're choosing between creative possibilities, the judgement may reflect preference rather than factual accuracy.

These distinctions help us decide how much weight an intuition deserves.

### 5. The First Feeling

Let's return to Mark's description.

The first feeling.

Sometimes an idea appears and seems worth pursuing.

Sometimes a direction feels right.

Sometimes a possible connection produces immediate interest.

He has described allowing that experience to develop rather than forcing a premature decision.

Now imagine investigating it.

We might begin by distinguishing several kinds of first feelings.

One is recognition.

Something resembles a familiar pattern.

Another is preference.

One possibility feels more appealing than another.

Another is concern.

Something appears inconsistent or potentially problematic.

Another is curiosity.

A possibility attracts attention because it promises something interesting.

And another is certainty.

A conclusion feels correct before the person can explain why.

These experiences may overlap.

But they aren't interchangeable.

A feeling of curiosity doesn't establish that an idea is true.

A feeling of preference doesn't establish that a choice is objectively best.

A feeling of concern doesn't establish that a problem exists.

And a feeling of certainty doesn't guarantee accuracy.

The Meistro Method encourages us to identify the kind of experience before deciding what to do with it.

Instead of asking only:

"Should I trust this feeling?"

Ask:

"What is this feeling telling me to investigate?"

That question preserves the usefulness of intuition without granting it authority it hasn't earned.

### 6. The Difference Between a Signal and a Verdict

Imagine a warning light on a machine.

The light illuminates.

Something requires attention.

But the light doesn't necessarily tell you the complete cause.

It may indicate a sensor reading.

A threshold being exceeded.

Or a condition that requires further inspection.

You wouldn't necessarily dismantle the entire machine based solely on the light.

You'd investigate.

Now consider an intuitive impression.

Something feels wrong.

That feeling may be useful.

It may direct attention towards a relevant feature.

But the feeling doesn't establish the explanation.

The Meistro Method treats intuition as a potential signal rather than a verdict.

A signal invites examination.

A verdict closes the case.

This distinction can prevent two opposite mistakes.

The first is dismissing every intuition because it lacks an immediate explanation.

That may cause us to overlook useful information.

The second is accepting every intuition as truth because it feels compelling.

That may cause us to overlook errors and biases.

A more disciplined approach lies between them.

Notice the impression.

Describe it.

Identify possible sources.

Seek evidence.

And revise the judgement when necessary.

The goal isn't to eliminate intuition.

It's to integrate it into a process of investigation.

### 7. The Confidence Trap

Now let's examine one of intuition's most dangerous features.

Certainty.

Imagine making a decision.

The answer feels obvious.

You can't explain every reason.

But you're convinced.

Now imagine discovering that the decision was wrong.

How could such a strong feeling produce an incorrect judgement?

Because subjective confidence and objective accuracy are not the same thing.

Confidence is part of the experience of judgement.

Accuracy concerns whether the judgement corresponds to reality.

A person can be confident and correct.

Confident and wrong.

Uncertain and correct.

Or uncertain and wrong.

The relationship varies across tasks and individuals.

The Meistro Method encourages us to separate these dimensions.

When recording an intuitive judgement, ask two questions.

How certain do I feel?

And what evidence supports the conclusion?

The first concerns subjective experience.

The second concerns justification.

Over time, comparing confidence with outcomes can reveal patterns.

Perhaps you're well calibrated in familiar technical tasks.

Perhaps you're overconfident when interpreting ambiguous social situations.

Perhaps you underestimate your accuracy in certain unfamiliar tasks.

These possibilities require observation.

But the distinction between confidence and accuracy is essential.

Because a strong feeling isn't automatically a strong argument.

### 8. The Meistro Case Study: When Something Just Fits

During our investigations, Mark has described moments when ideas seem to connect naturally.

A conversation moves through several subjects.

A possibility emerges.

And suddenly, a relationship becomes apparent.

The experience may feel immediate.

The connection seems obvious once recognised.

But what exactly happened?

Perhaps several ideas shared a structural similarity.

Perhaps one subject reminded him of another.

Perhaps existing technical knowledge provided a useful analogy.

Perhaps the conversation created a new combination.

Or perhaps the apparent connection was superficial.

The Meistro Method encourages us to examine the relationship.

What are the actual similarities?

Where does the analogy work?

Where does it fail?

What new predictions does it suggest?

And can those predictions be tested?

This is especially important when exploring philosophical or scientific questions.

An analogy can be illuminating.

But it doesn't automatically establish a causal mechanism.

A steel connection may provide a useful way to think about relationships between components.

But that doesn't prove that consciousness, memory, or social connection operates according to the same principles.

The value of an analogy lies partly in the questions it helps generate.

Its limitations become apparent when we examine what the comparison cannot explain.

Intuition may help us notice a promising connection.

Evidence determines how far the connection can responsibly be taken.

### 9. When Intuition Becomes Bias

Now we need to confront a less comfortable possibility.

Some intuitive impressions are shaped by cognitive biases.

Imagine meeting someone for the first time.

Within seconds, you develop an impression.

They seem trustworthy.

Or arrogant.

Or intelligent.

Or unfriendly.

The impression may feel immediate and convincing.

But it may depend on limited information.

Facial expression.

Tone of voice.

Appearance.

Similarity to someone you've known.

Or expectations shaped by previous experiences.

These cues don't necessarily provide reliable evidence about the person's character.

Yet the impression can influence subsequent interpretation.

If you initially believe someone is unfriendly, you may interpret ambiguous behaviour as further evidence.

If you believe someone is trustworthy, you may overlook warning signs.

This is related to confirmation bias.

The tendency to favour information that supports existing beliefs.

But bias isn't limited to social judgement.

Technical experts can also develop expectations that influence what they notice.

A familiar diagnosis may become the default explanation.

A preferred design may receive less critical scrutiny.

A previous success may create excessive confidence.

The Meistro Method encourages us to ask:

What would I notice if my initial impression were wrong?

That question can help counteract the tendency to search only for confirmation.

### 10. The Intuition That Was Right for the Wrong Reason

Here's an interesting complication.

An intuitive judgement can produce the correct answer even when the person's explanation is mistaken.

Imagine predicting that a machine will fail.

The machine fails.

You conclude that your intuition was accurate because you recognised a particular sound.

But perhaps the sound was unrelated.

Perhaps another condition caused the failure.

Perhaps the prediction was a coincidence.

The outcome alone doesn't establish the mechanism.

This is an important distinction.

Correct predictions provide evidence about performance.

But understanding why a prediction was correct requires additional investigation.

The same applies to personal decisions.

Someone may choose a successful career path.

They may later explain that they followed their intuition.

But many factors contributed to the outcome.

Opportunities.

Skills.

Relationships.

Economic conditions.

Chance.

And decisions made after the original choice.

The Meistro Method encourages us to resist turning successful outcomes into proof that our original explanations were correct.

A judgement can be accurate without the person understanding why.

And a good decision can sometimes produce a poor outcome because of factors beyond the person's control.

Evaluating intuition requires more than celebrating successes.

It requires examining the quality of the judgement process.

### 11. The Intuition That Was Wrong but Useful

Now imagine the opposite.

You suspect that a technical assembly contains an error.

You investigate.

The assembly is correct.

Your intuition was mistaken.

Was the experience useless?

Not necessarily.

Perhaps the investigation revealed an important design constraint.

Perhaps it improved your understanding.

Perhaps it confirmed that a particular detail was correct.

Perhaps it exposed a weakness in your assumptions.

An incorrect intuition can still lead to useful inquiry.

This is one reason the Meistro Method separates the value of a question from the accuracy of the initial impression.

Curiosity may direct attention towards something worth examining.

The investigation may produce knowledge even if the original concern was unfounded.

But we should also recognise costs.

Repeatedly investigating weak impressions can consume time.

It can create unnecessary work.

And in some contexts, it may reinforce unhelpful patterns of checking.

The goal is proportion.

How consequential is the judgement?

How expensive is verification?

How reliable has this kind of intuition been?

And what would happen if we ignored it?

These questions can help determine an appropriate response.

### 12. The Body's Contribution

People often describe intuition through physical sensations.

A feeling in the stomach.

Tension.

Relaxation.

Excitement.

Unease.

A sense of attraction or resistance.

These experiences can involve interoception, the perception and interpretation of internal bodily signals.

Interoceptive processes contribute to emotional experience and can influence decision-making.

But the relationship is complex.

A bodily sensation doesn't provide a direct reading of external truth.

A racing heart might reflect excitement.

Anxiety.

Physical exertion.

Caffeine.

Or several factors together.

A sense of calm might accompany a well-considered decision.

Or it might reflect familiarity with an incorrect assumption.

The Meistro Method encourages us to observe bodily experiences without assigning them automatic meanings.

What sensation occurred?

What was happening at the time?

What interpretation did you make?

What other explanations are possible?

And what happened afterwards?

These questions can help us distinguish the sensation from the story constructed around it.

Because the body contributes information to experience.

But interpreting that information requires care.

### 13. The Difference Between Intuition and Impulse

Now consider two experiences.

In the first, a person examines a complicated problem.

They recognise a promising direction.

They pause.

Consider it.

And investigate.

In the second, a person experiences a sudden urge to act.

They respond immediately.

Without considering consequences.

Both may feel spontaneous.

But they're not necessarily the same.

An intuitive judgement concerns an impression or conclusion.

An impulse concerns a tendency towards action.

The two can interact.

An intuitive impression may motivate an action.

But recognising the impression doesn't require acting immediately.

This distinction is especially useful for people who experience strong curiosity.

An interesting idea appears.

The possibility is exciting.

The mind wants to pursue it.

But the timing may be inconvenient.

Perhaps another project requires attention.

Perhaps the idea needs more information.

Perhaps acting immediately would create unnecessary costs.

The Meistro Method encourages us to separate recognising an idea from committing to it.

You can capture the idea.

Record the intuition.

Investigate its promise.

And decide later whether to act.

This preserves curiosity without requiring every interesting possibility to become an immediate obligation.

### 14. The Meistro Case Study: Letting the Decision Arrive

Mark has sometimes described decisions as something he allows to develop.

He doesn't always experience a deliberate sequence of explicit choices.

Instead, a direction may gradually become apparent.

He has described allowing the process to unfold naturally.

This is an interesting subjective observation.

But we should be cautious about interpreting it.

The experience of not consciously deliberating doesn't mean no evaluation occurred.

A person may be influenced by prior knowledge, preferences, emotional responses, and contextual information without consciously articulating each factor.

The decision may feel spontaneous even when it reflects substantial learning.

But the opposite possibility also exists.

Sometimes a decision may be based on incomplete information or momentary preference.

The Meistro Method encourages us to investigate the process without assuming that its apparent naturalness guarantees wisdom.

What information was available?

How long did the decision develop?

Were alternatives considered?

What emotional state accompanied it?

What happened after the choice?

And would the person make the same decision under different conditions?

These questions may reveal patterns.

Perhaps certain decisions benefit from allowing time.

Perhaps others require explicit analysis.

Perhaps the best approach depends on the domain and consequences.

The goal isn't to force every decision into conscious deliberation.

It's to understand when different decision processes are useful.

### 15. The Intuition Calibration Experiment

Now we're ready for one of the most useful exercises in this chapter.

For the next two weeks, record ten intuitive judgements.

Choose situations where outcomes can reasonably be checked.

For example:

A technical problem you suspect has a particular cause.

A software bug you think originates in a specific component.

An estimate of how long a familiar task will take.

A prediction about which design alternative will work.

Or a judgement about whether a particular approach will produce the desired result.

Before checking the outcome, record:

The intuition: What do you think?

The confidence: How certain do you feel, from 0 to 100 percent?

The evidence: What information is available?

The experience: What previous knowledge might be relevant?

The alternative: What else could explain the situation?

Then investigate.

Record the result.

Was the judgement correct?

Was the confidence appropriate?

Did the explanation hold?

And what did you learn?

Ten examples won't establish a complete measure of intuitive accuracy.

But the exercise can begin revealing patterns.

Over time, a larger record may help distinguish domains where your intuitions are useful from those where they require more caution.

### 16. The First Feeling Journal

This exercise focuses on subjective experience rather than prediction accuracy.

For one week, notice moments when a first feeling appears.

Perhaps something seems right.

Wrong.

Interesting.

Uncomfortable.

Promising.

Or worth investigating.

Record the experience as soon as practical.

What happened?

What did the feeling seem to indicate?

Was it accompanied by an image?

A thought?

A bodily sensation?

A memory?

Did you act?

Did you wait?

Did the feeling change?

And what evidence eventually became available?

The purpose isn't to prove that the feeling possesses special authority.

It's to understand what kinds of experiences you group under the word intuition.

You may discover that several different processes have been sharing the same label.

Some may concern recognition.

Others preference.

Others curiosity.

Others uncertainty.

Distinguishing them can make your observations more precise.

### 17. The Counter-Intuition Exercise

Now let's examine a technique for testing strong impressions.

Choose an intuitive judgement that feels convincing.

Write down the conclusion.

Then ask:

What would I expect to observe if this intuition were correct?

List specific evidence.

Next ask:

What would I expect to observe if it were wrong?

List alternative possibilities.

Then ask:

What information would most clearly distinguish the explanations?

This exercise encourages us to move from subjective certainty towards testable claims.

For example, suppose you suspect a software problem originates in a database connection.

If that explanation is correct, certain tests should produce predictable results.

If the problem originates elsewhere, the results may differ.

The same approach can be applied to many practical decisions.

But some intuitions concern values or preferences rather than factual predictions.

In those cases, verification may involve examining consequences, priorities, and trade-offs rather than identifying a single correct answer.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise the difference.

Not every feeling can be tested in the same way.

### 18. The Seventeenth Meistro Principle

We're now ready to establish the seventeenth principle.

Principle Seventeen: Intuition deserves attention, but confidence must be earned through evidence.

An intuitive impression may reflect experience.

Pattern recognition.

Emotional evaluation.

Expectation.

Or processes that remain partly outside conscious awareness.

Sometimes it provides a useful starting point.

Sometimes it misleads.

And sometimes it raises questions that produce valuable discoveries even when the original impression is wrong.

The Meistro Method encourages us to investigate intuition rather than worship or dismiss it.

What appeared?

What information was available?

What previous experience might have contributed?

How confident did the judgement feel?

What evidence supports it?

And what happened when it was tested?

These questions help us develop a more calibrated relationship with our own impressions.

Because the goal isn't to stop having first feelings.

It's to become better at understanding what those feelings can and cannot tell us.

### 19. The Connection Revisited

Let's return to the steel detailer.

The assembly is on the screen.

Something looks wrong.

He stops.

Rotates the model.

Examines the geometry.

And begins investigating.

Perhaps he finds an interference.

Perhaps he discovers that the arrangement is correct.

Either way, the process produces information.

The intuitive impression directed attention.

The investigation tested the impression.

And the result updated understanding.

Now imagine repeating that process over many years.

A person encounters patterns.

Makes judgements.

Tests them.

Learns from outcomes.

And gradually develops a more refined sense of which impressions deserve attention.

That's one possible path towards skilled intuition.

Not an infallible internal compass.

Not a mysterious source of guaranteed truth.

But a learned relationship between experience, judgement, and feedback.

The Meistro Method encourages us to make that relationship visible.

Because intuition becomes more useful when we understand its limits.

And perhaps the most important lesson is that a first feeling doesn't need to be correct to be worth noticing.

Sometimes it points towards a problem.

Sometimes it reveals a preference.

Sometimes it exposes an assumption.

And sometimes it simply tells us that something has captured our curiosity.

The next step is investigation.

For each entry, record:

The situation: What was happening?

The confidence: How certain were you?

The domain: Was this technical, social, practical, creative, or something else?

The evidence: What information supported the judgement?

The alternatives: What other explanations were possible?

The outcome: What happened?

The lesson: What did the result reveal?

After collecting the entries, examine them.

Were your intuitions more accurate in familiar domains?

Don't attempt to prove that you're especially intuitive.

Don't attempt to prove that intuition is unreliable.

Simply investigate.

Because the most valuable result may be a more precise understanding of when your first impressions deserve confidence.

### A Final Thought: The Feeling Before the Explanation

Somewhere in the ordinary activity of the mind, judgements are forming.

A familiar pattern becomes recognisable.

A possible error attracts attention.

A new idea feels promising.

A decision begins to take shape.

And sometimes, before a complete explanation is available, a person experiences a sense of knowing.

The Meistro Method doesn't ask us to deny that experience.

It asks us to understand it.

To distinguish feeling from fact.

Recognition from certainty.

Preference from evidence.

And curiosity from conclusion.

Because intuition can be useful without being infallible.

And understanding its limitations doesn't diminish its value.

It makes it easier to use responsibly.

Mark's description of his first feeling provides a useful starting point.

Not because it proves that he possesses a special faculty.

But because it identifies an experience that can be observed, compared, and investigated.

And perhaps the most interesting question isn't whether the feeling should be trusted.

It's what information, experience, and expectations contributed to its appearance.

That question leads us towards the next chapter.

A chapter about connections.

How ideas become associated.

How one subject leads to another.

How knowledge from different domains can interact.

And how a mind can move through a sequence of seemingly unrelated thoughts before arriving at a new possibility.

Because intuition may help us recognise a promising relationship.

But understanding how ideas become connected requires another investigation entirely.
