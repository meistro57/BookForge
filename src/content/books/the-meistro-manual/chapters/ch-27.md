# THE MEISTRO MANUAL

Volume One: The Art of Investigating Your Own Mind

# PART V: THE LIVING LABORATORY

# Chapter Twenty-Seven

## The Art of Asking Better Questions

Why the question you ask determines what you are likely to discover, how hidden assumptions can quietly steer an investigation, and why learning to question the question itself may be one of the most powerful skills a person can develop.

### The Question That Wasn't About the Machine

Imagine a man standing in front of a machine that refuses to operate.

The motor is silent.

The controls appear normal.

The power is connected.

And the machine worked yesterday.

Someone asks:

"Why won't the motor start?"

A reasonable question.

The man checks the motor.

He measures voltage.

Examines the wiring.

Inspects the overload protection.

Everything appears normal.

He becomes frustrated.

The motor should work.

So he asks a different question.

"What conditions must exist before this machine is allowed to start?"

Now the investigation changes.

He examines the control circuit.

The emergency-stop system.

The safety interlocks.

The programmable controller.

And eventually, he discovers that a guard-position sensor isn't reporting the expected state.

The motor isn't necessarily defective.

The system is refusing to energise it.

The original question directed attention towards the motor.

The revised question directed attention towards the conditions required for operation.

Same machine.

Same symptoms.

Different investigation.

And a different result.

Now consider what happens when we apply this idea to ourselves.

A person asks:

"Why am I so lazy?"

The question directs attention towards laziness.

Another asks:

"Why can't I concentrate?"

The question assumes concentration is the problem.

Someone else asks:

"Why do I always ruin things?"

The question quietly treats a sweeping negative judgement as established fact.

But what happens if we change the questions?

"Under what conditions do I find it difficult to begin?"

"What kinds of tasks hold my attention, and which ones don't?"

"What actually happened, and what part of the outcome was within my control?"

The questions become more specific.

They invite evidence.

They allow alternative explanations.

And they create possibilities for action.

This is the subject of Chapter Twenty-Seven.

Not simply asking more questions.

But learning to recognise how the structure of a question shapes the investigation that follows.

Because a question isn't merely a request for information.

It is also a way of directing attention.

And sometimes, the most important discovery occurs before an answer is ever found.

### 1. A Question Is a Search Instruction

Let's begin with a simple experiment.

Look around the room you're in.

Notice everything that is blue.

Now stop.

Without looking again, try to remember everything that is red.

You may remember some objects.

But perhaps fewer than you would have if you'd been looking specifically for red.

The demonstration isn't a formal test of attention.

Its result will vary depending on the room and the person.

But it illustrates an important idea.

What we search for influences what we notice.

Questions can operate similarly.

Imagine asking yourself:

"What went wrong today?"

Your attention may move towards mistakes.

Frustrations.

Interruptions.

And unfinished tasks.

Now ask:

"What helped me make progress today?"

You may notice useful tools.

Successful decisions.

Support from others.

And moments when a difficult problem became easier.

Neither question provides a complete picture.

Each selects a different part of experience.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise this selective function.

A question establishes a search direction.

And the direction can influence the evidence we collect.

This doesn't mean that every question determines its answer.

Reality can surprise us.

But questions affect where we look, what we measure, and which possibilities we consider.

### 2. The Question That Smuggles in an Answer

Consider this question:

"Why do I always abandon my projects?"

It appears to ask for an explanation.

But examine its assumptions.

It assumes projects are abandoned.

It assumes the behaviour occurs consistently.

And it implies that abandonment is the correct description of what happened.

What if some projects were experiments?

What if others were completed prototypes?

What if some were deliberately paused?

What if certain projects were abandoned because their original purpose was no longer relevant?

The question may be concealing several different situations.

Now consider a revised version.

"What happens to my engagement with projects after the most interesting technical problem has been solved?"

That question doesn't automatically establish that the projects were failures.

It identifies a possible transition.

And it creates opportunities for observation.

The Meistro Method encourages us to inspect the assumptions inside a question.

What does the wording already treat as true?

Is that assumption supported?

Could the same experience be described differently?

And would a different description change what we investigate?

Sometimes a question needs answering.

Sometimes it needs repairing.

### 3. The Meistro Case Study: Ninety-Nine Repositories

Imagine opening a GitHub account containing approximately ninety-nine repositories.

What question should we ask?

One possibility is:

"Why does this person start so many projects?"

That question focuses on quantity.

Another is:

"Why doesn't this person finish everything?"

That question focuses on completion.

But consider a different question.

"What does the history of these repositories reveal about the development of this person's technical interests and capabilities?"

Now the repositories become evidence of a possible learning trajectory.

We might examine their creation dates.

Programming languages.

Technical domains.

Dependencies.

Project structures.

Commit histories.

And relationships between earlier and later work.

Perhaps we would discover recurring interests.

Perhaps we would identify transitions from simple scripts to larger systems.

Perhaps certain tools appeared repeatedly.

Or perhaps the history would be more irregular than expected.

The important point is that the question determines what we examine.

If we're looking for unfinished work, we'll focus on completion.

If we're investigating technical evolution, we'll focus on changes in capability and method.

Neither investigation is automatically invalid.

But they answer different questions.

And the Meistro Method requires us to know which question we're asking.

### 4. Questions Have Hidden Boundaries

Now imagine asking:

"How can I become more productive?"

It sounds practical.

But what does productivity mean?

More tasks completed?

More hours worked?

More valuable results?

Less wasted effort?

Greater reliability?

Or more time available for activities that matter?

Without defining the outcome, the investigation becomes difficult.

A person could increase the number of tasks completed while producing less valuable work.

They could work longer hours while becoming exhausted.

They could automate repetitive tasks and accomplish more with less effort.

Or they could deliberately reduce output in one area to improve something else.

The Meistro Method encourages us to identify the boundaries of a question.

What exactly are we trying to understand?

What counts as success?

What is outside the scope?

And what trade-offs matter?

A question becomes more useful when its terms are clear enough to guide observation.

### 5. The Difference Between a Big Question and a Useful Question

Some questions are enormous.

"What is consciousness?"

"Why do people behave the way they do?"

"What makes a meaningful life?"

These questions can inspire entire fields of inquiry.

But they may be difficult to investigate directly.

Now consider a smaller question.

"What changes in my reported experience when I solve a problem using mental imagery rather than a written diagram?"

That question is narrower.

It identifies a specific experience.

It suggests possible comparisons.

And it may produce useful observations.

The Meistro Method doesn't reject big questions.

In fact, curiosity often begins with them.

But large questions frequently need to be divided into smaller investigations.

A broad question provides direction.

A specific question provides something we can examine.

The two serve different purposes.

### 6. The Question Ladder

Imagine beginning with a broad question.

"How does my mind work?"

Now move one level down.

"How do I solve unfamiliar problems?"

Then another.

"What do I notice first when I encounter a mechanical problem?"

Then another.

"Do I initially imagine the arrangement of components, recall similar experiences, or begin examining the physical system?"

And finally:

"During the next five unfamiliar mechanical problems, what is the first conscious experience I can reliably report?"

Each step narrows the investigation.

The question becomes more observable.

But notice something important.

The narrower question doesn't explain the entire mind.

It investigates one part of experience.

The Meistro Method encourages us to move between levels.

Begin with curiosity.

Identify a manageable question.

Collect evidence.

Then return to the broader investigation with new information.

This prevents the research from becoming either too vague or too narrow.

### 7. Questions That Describe Versus Questions That Explain

Now let's examine two kinds of questions.

Descriptive questions ask what happens.

Explanatory questions ask why it happens.

For example:

"What do I experience when a new idea appears?"

That's descriptive.

"Why do ideas appear in that form?"

That's explanatory.

Both matter.

But explanation often requires reliable description.

Imagine attempting to explain why you think visually before examining when visual imagery actually occurs.

You might construct an elaborate theory around an incomplete observation.

The Meistro Method encourages us to begin with description when the phenomenon is poorly understood.

What happens?

When?

How often?

Under what conditions?

What changes?

Only then do we begin asking why.

This doesn't mean explanations must wait until every detail is known.

But the quality of an explanation depends partly on the accuracy of what it's attempting to explain.

### 8. The Meistro Case Study: The Picture Before the Words

Mark has described experiences in which a visual representation seems to arrive before a verbal explanation.

That observation invites several questions.

One question is:

"Why am I a visual thinker?"

But that question assumes a stable category and asks for its cause.

A more careful investigation might begin differently.

"When does visual imagery appear during problem-solving?"

Then:

"What kinds of problems produce the clearest imagery?"

Then:

"What happens when I attempt to describe the image?"

Then:

"Does explaining the image change how I understand it?"

And eventually:

"Can external diagrams improve the accuracy of my reasoning?"

Notice how the investigation develops.

We begin with reported experience.

Move towards conditions.

Then examine consequences.

The Meistro Method encourages us to build questions that reveal structure rather than merely reinforce labels.

### 9. The Dangerous Comfort of "Why"

The word why is powerful.

It invites explanation.

But it can also encourage premature certainty.

Imagine asking:

"Why did I get angry?"

You may immediately construct a story.

"Because the other person disrespected me."

Perhaps that's accurate.

But perhaps the situation was more complicated.

You may have been tired.

Frustrated.

Confused.

Or responding to a misunderstanding.

Now consider a sequence of questions.

"What happened immediately before I became angry?"

"What did I interpret the other person's behaviour to mean?"

"What physical or emotional state was I already experiencing?"

"What alternative interpretations were possible?"

"What response did I choose?"

These questions don't eliminate the need for explanation.

They improve the information available for constructing one.

The Meistro Method encourages us to use why carefully.

Sometimes the better first question is simply:

"What happened?"

### 10. The Question That Creates a False Choice

Imagine someone asking:

"Am I a creative person or a practical person?"

The question assumes the two categories are alternatives.

But why?

A person may be both.

They may use creativity to solve practical problems.

Or practical knowledge to develop creative ideas.

Now consider another question.

"Do I think in pictures or words?"

Again, the question suggests a choice.

But mental experience may involve multiple forms of representation.

The balance may change with the task.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise false alternatives.

Does the question assume only two possibilities?

Could both be true?

Could neither be adequate?

Is there a third explanation?

Or does the distinction itself need reconsideration?

Sometimes the most useful answer to a question is:

"The categories are wrong."

### 11. The Power of "Under What Conditions?"

Now let's introduce one of the most useful question forms in the Meistro Method.

Under what conditions does this happen?

Consider:

"Why can't I concentrate?"

Now revise it.

"Under what conditions does concentration become difficult?"

The revised question invites comparison.

Perhaps concentration is easier during technical exploration.

Harder during repetitive administration.

Easier when interruptions are limited.

Harder when the next step is unclear.

Now consider:

"Why do I procrastinate?"

Revised:

"Under what conditions do I postpone tasks I've already decided to complete?"

Again, the question becomes more specific.

It invites evidence.

And it suggests possible interventions.

The Meistro Method encourages us to use conditional questions when investigating recurring behaviour.

Because behaviour often changes across contexts.

And those differences may reveal more than a global judgement.

### 12. The Power of "Compared With What?"

Now consider another essential question.

Compared with what?

Imagine someone saying:

"I work better at night."

Better than what?

Morning?

Afternoon?

A noisy workplace?

A quiet weekend?

And what does better mean?

Faster?

More accurate?

More enjoyable?

More creative?

Without comparison, the statement remains vague.

The Meistro Method encourages us to identify a reference point.

What is the alternative condition?

What outcome are we comparing?

And are the situations similar enough for the comparison to be meaningful?

This question is especially useful when evaluating personal experiments.

An improvement means little unless we understand what it improved relative to.

### 13. The Power of "What Would Change My Mind?"

Now let's examine a question that challenges our attachment to explanations.

What would change my mind?

Imagine believing that you learn best by doing.

You have many examples.

Mechanical repairs.

Programming experiments.

Hands-on technical work.

But what evidence would challenge the belief?

Perhaps discovering that certain subjects are learned more effectively through reading before practice.

Perhaps finding that direct experimentation sometimes produces persistent misunderstandings.

Perhaps noticing that instruction and practice work best together.

The Meistro Method encourages us to identify what evidence could modify a conclusion.

If no possible observation could change the belief, we may no longer be treating it as an investigable hypothesis.

It may have become an identity commitment.

That doesn't automatically make it false.

But it changes how we're using it.

### 14. The Question That Reveals a Missing Variable

Imagine investigating why a project stalled.

You ask:

"What went wrong?"

You identify several technical obstacles.

But the project may also have changed in importance.

Perhaps the original problem was solved another way.

Perhaps the requirements became irrelevant.

Perhaps a more valuable opportunity appeared.

The Meistro Method encourages us to ask:

"What changed that I haven't accounted for?"

This question can reveal missing variables.

Time.

Priorities.

Resources.

Emotional investment.

External dependencies.

Or changes in the goal itself.

The same principle applies to personal behaviour.

If an explanation doesn't fit the evidence, perhaps an important factor is missing.

The investigator should remain open to that possibility.

### 15. The Meistro Case Study: The Economics of Curiosity

Consider the phrase economics of curiosity.

It emerged from a question about how curiosity influences the allocation of time and effort.

But the phrase itself doesn't establish a theory.

It creates a research direction.

What resources does curiosity consume?

What benefits can exploration produce?

What opportunities are sacrificed?

How do people decide when to continue investigating?

And when does curiosity become costly?

Each question opens a different part of the subject.

Some concern behaviour.

Others concern value.

Others concern decision-making.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish the questions before combining them into a broader explanation.

Because an interesting phrase can become a powerful organising concept.

But it must not become a substitute for evidence.

### 16. Questions That Reveal Assumptions About Other People

Now let's examine interpersonal questions.

Imagine asking:

"Why doesn't this person understand me?"

The question assumes that the problem lies entirely with the other person.

Perhaps they genuinely misunderstand.

But perhaps the explanation was unclear.

Perhaps the participants use words differently.

Perhaps the subject requires background knowledge.

Or perhaps the conversation occurred under poor conditions.

A revised question might be:

"Where did our interpretations begin to differ?"

That question invites investigation.

It doesn't assign blame automatically.

Now consider:

"Why are they being difficult?"

Revised:

"What are they trying to accomplish, and how does it differ from what I'm trying to accomplish?"

Again, the question changes the investigation.

The Meistro Method encourages us to examine how our questions represent other people's intentions.

Because we rarely have direct access to those intentions.

We infer them.

And those inferences can be mistaken.

### 17. The Question That Protects the Ego

Here's an uncomfortable possibility.

Sometimes we ask questions that protect our preferred self-image.

Imagine a project fails.

We ask:

"Why didn't the tools work?"

Perhaps the tools genuinely failed.

But perhaps our assumptions were wrong.

Perhaps the design was incomplete.

Perhaps we underestimated the difficulty.

The Meistro Method encourages us to ask questions that include our own role without automatically assigning blame.

"What factors contributed to the outcome?"

"What assumptions did I make?"

"What information did I overlook?"

"What would I do differently?"

These questions may reveal more than an investigation focused entirely on external causes.

But the opposite error is also possible.

We shouldn't assume every failure is our fault.

The goal is accurate attribution.

Not self-protection.

And not self-condemnation.

### 18. The Question That Changes the Emotional Temperature

Now imagine a difficult conversation.

Someone says:

"Why do you always do that?"

The wording contains accusation.

It suggests a recurring defect.

The other person may become defensive.

Now imagine asking:

"What happened there? I want to understand."

The emotional tone changes.

The second question doesn't guarantee cooperation.

But it may create more room for explanation.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise that questions influence relationships as well as information.

A question can invite exploration.

Or imply judgement.

It can create pressure.

Or allow uncertainty.

And it can encourage correction.

Or make disagreement feel dangerous.

The goal isn't to eliminate difficult questions.

Sometimes direct accountability is necessary.

But the wording should serve the purpose.

If the goal is understanding, the question should make understanding possible.

### 19. The Difference Between Curiosity and Interrogation

Now consider two people asking many questions.

One is curious.

The other feels interrogative.

What's the difference?

Perhaps the questions serve different purposes.

Curiosity seeks understanding.

Interrogation may seek confirmation, control, or a particular admission.

But the distinction isn't always obvious.

A person may ask sincere questions in a way that feels overwhelming.

Another may ask few questions but listen carefully.

The Meistro Method encourages us to examine both intention and effect.

Why am I asking?

What am I hoping to learn?

Does the other person have room to answer?

Am I willing to hear something unexpected?

And am I respecting their boundaries?

A useful question doesn't automatically entitle us to an answer.

Especially when the subject involves another person's private experience.

Curiosity is valuable.

Consent and respect remain essential.

### 20. The Question That Opens a New Domain

Sometimes a question changes the direction of an entire project.

Imagine working on a fabrication problem.

You're trying to improve a repetitive workflow.

You begin by asking:

"How can I perform this operation faster?"

Then you ask:

"Why does this operation need to be performed manually?"

Now the investigation changes.

Perhaps the task can be automated.

Perhaps the data already exists elsewhere.

Perhaps the workflow can be redesigned.

Or perhaps the operation can be eliminated entirely.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise questions that change the level of analysis.

Instead of improving an existing step, we investigate whether the step is necessary.

Instead of optimising a tool, we examine the process.

Instead of solving a symptom, we investigate the system.

This is a powerful form of questioning.

Because sometimes the most valuable improvement doesn't make the original task easier.

It removes the need for the task.

### 21. The Meistro Case Study: The Click That Shouldn't Exist

Mark has repeatedly demonstrated an interest in reducing unnecessary operations.

Extra clicks.

Repetitive commands.

Manual transfers of information.

These preferences provide a useful example of question design.

Imagine asking:

"How can I click this button faster?"

That question assumes the button must be clicked.

Now ask:

"Why am I clicking this button at all?"

Perhaps the action is necessary.

Perhaps it can be automated.

Perhaps the interface can be redesigned.

Or perhaps the underlying workflow is inefficient.

The second question expands the investigation.

It challenges the structure of the task.

The Meistro Method encourages us to apply the same reasoning to personal behaviour.

Instead of asking only how to become better at tolerating a difficulty, we might ask whether the conditions creating the difficulty can be changed.

Not every obstacle can be removed.

But some exist because nobody has questioned the process.

### 22. The Question That Cannot Be Answered Yet

Now let's examine uncertainty.

Some questions are meaningful but currently unanswerable.

Others are too vague.

Some require information we don't possess.

And some may depend on concepts that haven't been defined clearly enough.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish these situations.

An unanswered question isn't necessarily a bad question.

But we should understand why it remains unanswered.

What information is missing?

What concepts need clarification?

What methods are available?

And what smaller questions could make progress possible?

This is especially important in philosophical investigations.

Questions about consciousness, identity, and experience can be intellectually valuable.

But the available evidence may not support definitive conclusions.

The investigator must remain willing to preserve uncertainty.

Not every mystery needs an immediate answer.

### 23. The Question That Produces Better Experiments

Now let's return to the living laboratory.

Suppose you want to investigate whether interruptions affect your problem-solving.

A vague question is:

"Why do interruptions ruin my thinking?"

A better question is:

"How does interruption frequency relate to the time required to complete complex modelling tasks?"

Now we have something more observable.

We can define interruption frequency.

Measure task duration.

Record task complexity.

And compare conditions.

But even this question has limitations.

Perhaps interruptions affect accuracy more than speed.

Perhaps different interruptions have different effects.

Perhaps the person compensates by working longer.

The Meistro Method encourages us to refine questions iteratively.

A good research question isn't necessarily perfect at the beginning.

It improves as we learn what can be observed and measured.

### 24. The Question Review

Before beginning an investigation, examine the question itself.

Ask:

Does it contain an unsupported assumption?

Are the important terms defined?

Does it suggest a false choice?

Is it too broad to investigate?

Does it identify something observable?

What alternative explanations remain possible?

And what evidence would change the conclusion?

This review doesn't need to become a complicated procedure.

Sometimes thirty seconds is enough.

But the habit can prevent entire investigations from being built around misleading premises.

### 26. The Question That Reveals What Matters

Not every question concerns causes.

Some concern values.

Imagine asking:

"What should I work on next?"

The answer depends partly on what matters.

Income.

Curiosity.

Commitments.

Learning.

Relationships.

Rest.

Or long-term goals.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish factual questions from value questions.

A factual question might ask:

"How much time will this project require?"

A value question might ask:

"Is this project worth that time?"

Evidence can inform both.

But values cannot always be derived from facts alone.

A project may be technically feasible and still not be worth pursuing.

Another may offer little financial return but substantial personal meaning.

The investigator must recognise which kind of question is being asked.

Otherwise, they may attempt to solve a value conflict through technical analysis alone.

### 27. The Question Behind the Question

Now let's examine a deeper possibility.

Sometimes the question we ask isn't the question we actually need answered.

Imagine asking:

"How can I become more productive?"

Perhaps the underlying concern is:

"Why do I feel dissatisfied even when I accomplish a great deal?"

Or:

"How can I create more time for the things I care about?"

Or:

"How do I know when I've done enough?"

The original question concerns productivity.

The underlying question may concern meaning, priorities, or self-evaluation.

The Meistro Method encourages us to ask:

What am I really trying to understand or change?

This doesn't mean every practical question conceals a deeper psychological issue.

Sometimes a productivity question is simply about productivity.

But when repeated attempts to answer a question fail to resolve the underlying concern, it may be worth examining whether the question itself is misdirected.

### 28. The Twenty-Seventh Meistro Principle

We're now ready to establish the twenty-seventh principle.

Principle Twenty-Seven: Before searching for an answer, investigate what your question assumes and what answering it would actually tell you.

Questions direct attention.

They influence which evidence we collect.

They can expose possibilities.

But they can also conceal assumptions.

Create false alternatives.

And reinforce inaccurate self-descriptions.

The Meistro Method encourages us to examine the structure of our questions.

What am I asking?

What am I assuming?

What can be observed?

What alternatives exist?

And what evidence would change my understanding?

A better question doesn't guarantee a correct answer.

But it can create better conditions for discovery.

### 29. The Machine Starts

Let's return to the workshop.

The machine refuses to operate.

The man asks:

"Why won't the motor start?"

He examines the motor.

Everything appears normal.

Then he changes the question.

"What conditions must exist before the system will allow the motor to start?"

He follows the control logic.

Checks the safety circuit.

And discovers the missing signal.

The machine starts.

Now imagine that the original question had never been revised.

Perhaps the man would have continued replacing components.

Testing connections.

And investigating a motor that wasn't defective.

The problem wasn't necessarily a lack of technical knowledge.

It was the direction of the investigation.

And that's the lesson of this chapter.

Sometimes we don't need more effort.

More information.

Or more complicated explanations.

Sometimes we need a question that points towards the right part of the problem.

The same principle applies to self-understanding.

Instead of asking why we are defective, we can investigate the conditions under which difficulties occur.

Instead of assuming our identities are fixed, we can examine how our capabilities develop.

Instead of treating feelings as explanations, we can ask what they reveal and what remains uncertain.

And instead of demanding immediate answers, we can learn to preserve questions that deserve further investigation.

Because a question can be more than a request for information.

It can be the beginning of a new way of seeing.

## Chapter Twenty-Seven: Field Assignment

### The Seven-Day Question Refinery

For the next seven days, collect questions you naturally ask yourself.

Don't invent them.

Notice them.

Perhaps during work.

During conversations.

While solving problems.

Or while reflecting on your experiences.

Each day, choose one question.

Write it down exactly as it appeared.

Then examine it.

What assumptions does it contain?

What evidence would answer it?

Is it descriptive, explanatory, practical, or concerned with values?

Could it be made more specific?

And what alternative question might reveal something different?

At the end of the week, review the seven questions.

Look for patterns.

Do you frequently ask questions that contain negative self-judgements?

Do you assume causes before examining events?

Do you frame situations as false choices?

Do you ask broad questions that could be divided into smaller investigations?

Or do you naturally generate questions that invite experimentation?

The goal isn't to judge your questioning style.

It's to understand it.

Then choose one revised question to investigate during the following week.

### A Final Thought: The Door Hidden Inside the Question

Some questions are small.

How does this work?

Where did that file go?

Why did the machine stop?

Others are enormous.

What is consciousness?

What makes a person who they are?

Why do we experience the world as we do?

But the size of a question doesn't determine its value.

A small question can reveal an important principle.

A large question can remain useful even when no definitive answer is available.

And sometimes a question changes our lives not because we answer it, but because asking it changes what we notice.

The Meistro Method encourages us to remain curious about the questions themselves.

Where did they come from?

What do they assume?

What possibilities do they exclude?

And what might become visible if we asked differently?

Because the investigator's most important instrument may not be a notebook.

A computer.

A measuring device.

Or an artificial intelligence system.

It may be the ability to recognise when a question needs to change.

And once we've learned to ask better questions, we're ready for the next challenge.

Designing experiments that can help answer them.

Not elaborate laboratory procedures.

But small, thoughtful investigations that fit into ordinary life.

Experiments that compare conditions.

Test predictions.

Reveal limitations.

And occasionally surprise us.

Because curiosity gives us questions.

But evidence gives those questions something to answer to.
