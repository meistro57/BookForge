# Chapter Thirty-Six

## The Person You Haven't Met Yet

Why understanding yourself is not the same as finishing yourself, how the future changes the meaning of the past, and what happens when you stop treating identity as a completed structure and begin recognising it as something still under construction.

### The Drawing That Was Never Finished

Imagine an experienced steel detailer sitting alone in his office late one afternoon.

On the screen is a complicated structural assembly.

Columns.

Beams.

Connections.

Stiffeners.

Brackets.

And dozens of individual components that must eventually become a physical structure.

The model has taken weeks to develop.

He knows it intimately.

He knows which connections caused trouble.

Which dimensions required revision.

Which details demanded unusual solutions.

And which parts of the assembly were straightforward from the beginning.

He rotates the model.

Studies it.

And considers how much work has gone into making everything fit.

Then a message arrives.

The architect has revised the design.

A major element must move.

Several connections will need to change.

And some of the work he's already completed will have to be reconsidered.

For a moment, he stares at the screen.

He could become frustrated.

He could defend the existing arrangement.

He could argue that the design was already complete.

And technically, from the perspective of the previous requirements, it was.

But the requirements have changed.

The structure must now become something different.

So he begins investigating.

Which parts of the model remain valid?

Which relationships must change?

Can the existing geometry be adapted?

And what new possibilities become available because of the revision?

He starts moving components.

Testing arrangements.

And discovering that some of the previous constraints no longer apply.

A connection that was difficult before becomes simpler.

Another becomes more complicated.

And an unexpected opportunity appears.

By the end of the afternoon, the model has changed substantially.

It's still recognisable.

The history of the earlier design remains embedded in its structure.

But it's no longer the same assembly.

And perhaps the most interesting part is this.

The detailer couldn't have produced the revised model before receiving the new information.

Not because he lacked intelligence.

Not because he lacked imagination.

But because the conditions that made the new design meaningful hadn't existed yet.

The future introduced information that changed what was possible.

Now consider a human life.

We often imagine that somewhere inside us exists a finished identity.

A complete answer to the question:

"Who am I?"

We search for it.

Through memories.

Personality descriptions.

Psychological theories.

Relationships.

Achievements.

And explanations of our behaviour.

We hope that eventually we'll discover the final arrangement.

The definitive model.

The version of ourselves that explains everything.

But what if that expectation is mistaken?

What if the person we're trying to understand isn't a completed structure waiting to be uncovered?

What if the person is still developing?

Still learning.

Still responding to unfamiliar conditions.

Still encountering experiences that change what becomes possible.

And still capable of surprising even the person who knows their history best?

That possibility brings us to the final investigation of Volume One.

The person you are today is real. Your history matters. Your patterns matter. But none of them can completely describe the person you may become.

### 1. The Problem With a Final Answer

Think about the question we've been investigating throughout this book.

"How does my mind actually work?"

At first, it sounds like a question with a discoverable answer.

Perhaps we can identify the underlying mechanisms.

Map the patterns.

Explain the preferences.

And eventually produce a complete description.

But the investigation encounters a complication.

The object being studied is changing.

Every new skill alters what the person can do.

Every significant experience may influence expectations.

Every new relationship introduces unfamiliar perspectives.

And every deliberate experiment may change the behaviour being investigated.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise that self-understanding is necessarily provisional.

Not because accurate knowledge is impossible.

But because the person being described is not static.

We can discover reliable patterns.

We can identify meaningful continuities.

And we can develop useful explanations.

But those explanations describe a developing system.

They don't freeze its future.

### 2. The Difference Between Identity and History

Imagine someone saying:

"I've always been this way."

What does that mean?

Perhaps they've noticed a pattern extending across many years.

Perhaps the pattern genuinely reflects a stable tendency.

Or perhaps their memory has organised the past around their current understanding.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish historical continuity from permanent identity.

A person may have enjoyed mechanical problems since childhood.

That history matters.

It may reveal an enduring interest.

But it doesn't establish that mechanical work exhausts their capabilities.

Or that every future interest must resemble their earlier ones.

History provides evidence.

It does not define the boundaries of possibility.

This distinction becomes especially important when people use the past to predict what they cannot do.

"I've never been good at that."

"I've always struggled with this."

"I'm just not that kind of person."

Such statements may describe genuine difficulties.

But they can also become assumptions that prevent new learning.

The Meistro Method encourages us to examine the evidence behind them.

What exactly has been tried?

Under what conditions?

With what instruction?

And how much opportunity for development existed?

### 3. The Meistro Case Study: The Child and the Engineer

Imagine examining two photographs.

The first shows a child fascinated by a small mechanical device.

The second shows an experienced steel detailer working with sophisticated software.

Decades separate the images.

The tools are different.

The knowledge is different.

And the scale of the problems is different.

Yet we might notice a possible continuity.

An interest in understanding how things work.

But we should be careful.

The adult's abilities weren't fully contained in the child.

They developed through experience.

Instruction.

Practice.

Mistakes.

Opportunities.

And encounters with technologies that didn't exist during the child's earliest years.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise both continuity and development.

The child may have possessed tendencies that influenced later choices.

But the adult's capabilities were constructed through a history of interaction with the world.

And that history is still continuing.

### 4. The Future Is Not Merely More of the Past

Now imagine standing at a particular moment in your life.

You know your current abilities.

Your interests.

Your responsibilities.

And the technologies available to you.

You attempt to predict what you'll be doing twenty years later.

How accurate will you be?

Perhaps you'll correctly identify enduring interests.

But you may fail to anticipate technologies that haven't been invented.

People you haven't met.

Opportunities that haven't appeared.

And experiences that will change your priorities.

The Meistro Method encourages us to treat long-term self-predictions cautiously.

We can make plans.

Develop capabilities.

And prepare for likely possibilities.

But the future contains information unavailable to the present.

A useful personal operating manual should therefore preserve adaptability.

Not merely optimise existing patterns.

### 5. The Unexpected Capability

Imagine someone who has spent years working with physical machinery.

They understand mechanisms.

Sequences.

And practical constraints.

Then they encounter programming.

At first, the subject seems unfamiliar.

But gradually, they recognise connections.

A software function resembles a mechanical operation.

A control sequence resembles a machine cycle.

A data structure provides another way to organise relationships.

The person begins learning.

Experiments.

Makes mistakes.

And develops a new capability.

Was the programming ability hidden inside them all along?

Not necessarily in any completed form.

But earlier knowledge may have provided useful foundations.

The new ability developed through interaction between existing skills and new experience.

The Meistro Method encourages us to investigate how capabilities transfer.

What knowledge carries over?

What must be learned from the beginning?

And what becomes possible when familiar principles are applied in unfamiliar domains?

The person who emerges from the learning process may possess abilities that couldn't have been accurately predicted from their earlier work alone.

### 6. The Meistro Case Study: Technologies That Changed the Questions

Consider the technological changes across Mark's working life.

Mechanical systems.

Computer-aided drafting.

Three-dimensional modelling.

Programming.

Automation.

And artificial intelligence.

Each technology introduced new possibilities.

But perhaps more importantly, each changed the kinds of questions that could be asked.

A person working with manual drafting tools may ask how to produce a complicated drawing efficiently.

A person using parametric modelling may ask how to generate an entire family of related components.

A person using automation may ask which parts of the workflow should be performed by software.

And someone experimenting with AI may ask how language-based systems can help investigate, design, or coordinate those workflows.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise that technological development can change cognitive opportunities.

The person isn't merely acquiring new tools.

They're encountering new problem spaces.

And those problem spaces may reveal capabilities that previous environments never required.

### 7. The Difference Between Potential and Destiny

Now consider the word potential.

We often use it to suggest that a person's future is somehow already present.

Waiting to unfold.

But this can be misleading.

Human development isn't simply the release of a predetermined design.

It involves biological capacities.

Learning.

Environmental conditions.

Chance events.

Choices.

And interactions with other people.

The Meistro Method encourages us to treat potential as a range of possibilities rather than a fixed destination.

A person may possess the capacity to develop a skill.

But whether that skill develops depends on opportunities, practice, resources, and circumstances.

And new possibilities may emerge as other capabilities are acquired.

The future isn't entirely predetermined by the past.

Nor is it unlimited.

It's constrained, influenced, and open to development.

### 8. The Person Who Changes Their Mind

Now imagine holding a belief for many years.

Perhaps it concerns your abilities.

Your priorities.

Or the kind of life you want.

Then new evidence appears.

An experience challenges the belief.

You reconsider.

And eventually revise your understanding.

Have you become less authentic?

Not necessarily.

You may have become more accurate.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish consistency from integrity.

Consistency means maintaining the same position.

Integrity involves acting in accordance with values and evidence as we understand them.

Sometimes integrity requires changing a conclusion.

The person who refuses to revise an inaccurate belief may appear consistent.

But the consistency may come at the cost of understanding.

A developing identity should allow correction.

### 9. The Meistro Case Study: The Investigator Who Becomes the Experiment

There's a fascinating complication in the Reverse-Engineering Meistro project.

Mark began investigating his own thinking.

But the investigation itself may influence how he thinks.

He becomes more attentive to certain experiences.

Learns new language for describing them.

Develops methods for preserving observations.

And begins asking questions that might never have occurred without the project.

The investigator changes the conditions of the investigation.

This is not unique to self-study.

Researchers studying human behaviour must consider how observation and measurement may influence participants.

But in personal investigation, the effect is particularly direct.

The person being studied is also learning from the process.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise this.

The goal isn't to discover an untouched, original self that exists independently of every experience.

It's to understand a developing person whose self-investigation becomes part of their development.

### 10. The Myth of the Hidden True Self

Now consider a familiar idea.

Somewhere beneath our habits, expectations, and social roles lies a single true self.

If we could remove everything imposed by the world, perhaps we'd discover who we really are.

There's something appealing about this image.

But it raises difficult questions.

Which experiences count as external additions?

Which relationships are part of us?

Which learned skills belong to our identity?

And why should the earliest version of a person be considered more authentic than the person they've become?

The Meistro Method encourages us to approach authenticity differently.

Rather than searching for a perfectly preserved original identity, we can investigate whether our current actions align with our considered values.

Are we acting deliberately?

Are we acknowledging our experiences?

Are we allowing ourselves to learn?

And are we resisting pressures that would require us to misrepresent what we believe?

Authenticity need not mean remaining unchanged.

It can involve participating honestly in our own development.

### 11. The Difference Between Growth and Improvement

Now let's examine a subtle distinction.

Growth doesn't always mean improvement.

A person may acquire a new habit that makes life more difficult.

Develop a belief that turns out to be mistaken.

Or become more skilled at something that doesn't serve their values.

Change alone isn't evidence of progress.

The Meistro Method encourages us to evaluate development.

What changed?

What consequences followed?

Did the change improve capability?

Understanding?

Well-being?

Or the ability to act according to important values?

Sometimes growth involves acquiring a new skill.

Sometimes it involves abandoning an unhelpful strategy.

And sometimes it involves recognising that an earlier goal no longer matters.

The direction of development should be examined.

Not assumed.

### 12. The Meistro Case Study: The Repository Timeline

Imagine opening a chronological list of technical projects.

The earliest repositories contain relatively simple tools.

Later projects involve more complex systems.

Then appear integrations.

Automation platforms.

And experiments with artificial intelligence.

The timeline may reveal increasing technical complexity.

But what else could it reveal?

Perhaps changes in preferred programming languages.

Different approaches to architecture.

Recurring attempts to solve familiar problems.

Or shifts from building individual tools towards coordinating multiple systems.

The Meistro Method encourages us to investigate the evolution of capability through actual work.

Not merely through self-description.

A project history can provide evidence of what a person attempted.

What they learned.

And how their methods changed.

But it should also be interpreted carefully.

A repository doesn't reveal every reason a project began or ended.

The timeline is evidence.

Not a complete biography.

### 13. The Future Self as a Stranger

Now imagine meeting yourself twenty years from today.

What would that person know?

What experiences would they have encountered?

Which of your current beliefs would they still hold?

And which would seem incomplete?

Perhaps they'd recognise your interests.

Your habits.

And your values.

But perhaps they'd also possess unfamiliar skills.

Different priorities.

And explanations you haven't yet considered.

The Meistro Method encourages us to approach the future self with curiosity.

Not as someone whose behaviour can be completely predicted.

But as a person whose development will depend on experiences that haven't happened yet.

This perspective can change how we make decisions.

Instead of asking only:

"What do I want right now?"

We might also ask:

"What opportunities for learning and development does this decision preserve?"

### 14. The Danger of Over-Planning

Now imagine attempting to design the next twenty years of your life in detail.

Every project.

Every skill.

Every milestone.

Every decision.

The plan may provide direction.

But it may also become rigid.

New opportunities may appear.

Circumstances may change.

And the person making the plan may develop different priorities.

The Meistro Method encourages us to distinguish direction from prediction.

A direction identifies values and broad objectives.

A prediction attempts to specify what will happen.

We can choose to develop useful capabilities.

Maintain important relationships.

And pursue meaningful work.

Without pretending to know every future opportunity.

A flexible plan can preserve purpose while allowing adaptation.

### 15. The Meistro Case Study: Curiosity as a Future-Generating Process

Throughout this book, curiosity has appeared repeatedly.

But perhaps its most interesting feature is not simply that it attracts attention.

Curiosity can change the future.

Imagine encountering an unfamiliar subject.

You investigate.

Learn something unexpected.

And discover a connection to another interest.

That connection suggests a project.

The project introduces new people.

New tools.

And new questions.

Eventually, your life contains activities that didn't exist when the original curiosity appeared.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise curiosity as a possible source of developmental pathways.

Not every curiosity leads somewhere important.

Many investigations end without significant consequences.

But some create opportunities that couldn't have been planned in advance.

The practical challenge is to preserve room for exploration while respecting existing responsibilities.

### 16. The Economics of Becoming

Now let's revisit a concept from earlier in the book.

The economics of curiosity.

Every investigation has costs.

Time.

Attention.

Resources.

And opportunities not pursued.

But some investigations also produce unexpected benefits.

New skills.

Connections.

Understanding.

And possibilities for future work.

The Meistro Method encourages us to consider the economics of becoming.

What are we investing in our future selves?

Which capabilities are worth developing?

Which activities create options?

And which commitments consume resources without supporting important goals?

This doesn't mean every experience must produce measurable returns.

Play.

Rest.

And enjoyment matter in their own right.

But the concept helps us recognise that our choices influence the range of futures available.

### 17. The Person You Become Through Other People

Now consider the role of relationships.

A teacher introduces an unfamiliar idea.

A colleague challenges an assumption.

A friend encourages an experiment.

Or a collaborator reveals a different way of approaching a problem.

These interactions can influence development.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise that identity is shaped partly through relationships.

Not because other people determine who we are.

But because interaction introduces information, opportunities, and perspectives that may change our understanding.

The person we become may depend on conversations that haven't happened yet.

And people we haven't met.

This makes the future difficult to predict.

But also rich with possibility.

### 18. The Meistro Case Study: Collaboration Across Different Minds

Mark's projects involve collaboration across technical and creative domains.

Human collaborators.

Software systems.

And AI tools.

Each interaction may introduce unfamiliar perspectives.

A programmer may reveal a software constraint.

A fabricator may reveal an assembly problem.

A researcher may challenge an explanation.

And an AI system may generate alternative questions or useful language.

The Meistro Method encourages us to investigate what each collaboration contributes.

But also to preserve distinctions.

Human participants bring their own lived experiences, expertise, and intentions.

AI systems operate through computational processes and may generate useful responses without possessing humanlike understanding.

The value of collaboration should be examined through its actual contributions.

Not through assumptions about identical minds.

### 20. The Difference Between Being Wrong and Being Finished

Now consider an important possibility.

You may discover that a central belief about yourself was mistaken.

Perhaps a difficulty you attributed to lack of ability was actually influenced by poor instruction.

Perhaps a preference you considered permanent changes with experience.

Or perhaps a strategy that once worked becomes ineffective.

The Meistro Method encourages us to treat these discoveries as opportunities for revision.

Being wrong about a pattern doesn't mean the entire investigation has failed.

It means the model needs updating.

And a model that can be corrected is more useful than one that must always appear right.

The goal isn't to produce an explanation that survives every challenge unchanged.

It's to develop understanding that improves through contact with evidence.

### 22. The Value of Remaining a Beginner

Now consider an experienced expert.

They possess extensive knowledge.

Recognise familiar problems quickly.

And can often predict outcomes.

Expertise is valuable.

But it can also create a temptation to remain within familiar territory.

The Meistro Method encourages us to preserve opportunities for beginnerhood.

Not because ignorance is preferable.

But because unfamiliar domains expose us to new questions.

A beginner must notice details an expert may overlook.

Ask for explanations.

And tolerate uncertainty.

These experiences can support learning.

They may also reveal assumptions that have become invisible through familiarity.

The ability to become a beginner again is a form of adaptability.

### 23. The Meistro Case Study: Learning Something That Doesn't Fit

Imagine Mark beginning a subject that doesn't immediately connect with his existing technical knowledge.

Perhaps a new branch of mathematics.

A language.

Or an unfamiliar artistic practice.

At first, the learning process feels awkward.

The usual analogies don't help.

The familiar tools aren't sufficient.

And progress is slower than expected.

The Meistro Method encourages us to examine the experience without immediately concluding that the subject is unsuitable.

What foundations are missing?

What instructional methods are available?

How much practice has occurred?

And what would count as meaningful progress?

Sometimes the subject truly isn't worth pursuing.

But sometimes the difficulty reflects the absence of familiar supports.

The future self may develop through learning that initially feels uncomfortable.

### 24. The Difference Between Reinvention and Continuity

Now imagine making a major change.

A new career.

A new field of study.

Or a different way of organising life.

People sometimes describe such changes as reinvention.

But the new version of the person doesn't emerge from nothing.

They bring previous knowledge.

Values.

Habits.

And experiences.

The Meistro Method encourages us to recognise that development often combines continuity and transformation.

Some patterns remain.

Others change.

And new capabilities emerge through interaction with unfamiliar conditions.

The person is neither entirely new nor entirely unchanged.

They are developing from a history.

### 27. The Last Trap: Becoming an Expert on Yourself

Now consider the danger of self-knowledge.

After years of investigation, you may become confident that you understand your own mind.

You know your patterns.

Your preferences.

Your strengths.

And your difficulties.

That knowledge can be useful.

But it can also create a new form of rigidity.

You may begin explaining every experience through the existing model.

Rejecting unfamiliar possibilities.

And defending your conclusions because you've invested so much effort in developing them.

The Meistro Method encourages us to remain suspicious of complete explanations.

Especially explanations about ourselves.

The person who knows a great deal about their own patterns may still encounter surprises.

And those surprises deserve attention.

The investigator should never become the guardian of a theory that can no longer be questioned.

### 28. The Thirty-Sixth Meistro Principle

We're now ready to establish the final principle of Volume One.

Principle Thirty-Six: Understand the person you are today without assuming that your current understanding defines the person you can become.

Your history matters.

Your patterns matter.

And your current capabilities matter.

But they don't exhaust your future.

The Meistro Method encourages us to preserve curiosity.

Adaptability.

And willingness to revise.

A personal operating manual should support development.

Not restrict it.

And self-investigation should remain an ongoing process.

Not a search for a final, unchanging identity.

## Epilogue: The Investigator Remains

We began this book with a question.

How does a mind actually work?

Not the human mind in the abstract.

Not a statistical average.

Not a personality category.

But a particular mind.

The one through which a person experiences the world.

The one that remembers.

Imagines.

Learns.

Decides.

Feels.

And sometimes surprises its owner.

We began by questioning the stories we tell about ourselves.

We examined childhood.

Curiosity.

Learning.

Failure.

Memory.

Attention.

Intuition.

And the relationships between ideas.

We investigated emotions.

Motivation.

Connection.

And the conditions that influence action.

Then we built a living laboratory.

We learned to ask better questions.

Design experiments.

Recognise patterns.

Examine contradictions.

And use conversation as a thinking instrument.

Finally, we began constructing a personal operating manual.

A practical record of what helps.

What interferes.

What remains uncertain.

And what might be worth investigating next.

But now we arrive at an interesting conclusion.

The investigation hasn't produced a final answer.

It has produced a method.

A way of observing.

Questioning.

Testing.

And revising.

And perhaps that is more valuable than a finished explanation.

Because a finished explanation can become obsolete.

But a useful method can continue producing understanding.

The Meistro Method doesn't promise that every mystery will be solved.

It doesn't claim that introspection reveals every cognitive mechanism.

And it doesn't suggest that a person can understand themselves completely through conversation or self-observation alone.

Human cognition is complicated.

Memory is imperfect.

Behaviour depends on context.

And many explanations require evidence beyond personal experience.

But the method offers something practical.

A disciplined way to remain curious about ourselves.

To respect our experiences without treating every interpretation as fact.

To recognise patterns without becoming imprisoned by them.

And to use what we learn to make better decisions.

The most important discovery may not be a particular cognitive trait.

Or a theory about identity.

Or even a completed personal operating manual.

It may be the realisation that we can participate deliberately in understanding and developing ourselves.

Not as passive observers.

Not as prisoners of our history.

And not as finished products.

But as investigators.

Builders.

Learners.

And people whose stories are still unfolding.

There will be more questions.

More mistakes.

More discoveries.

And more moments when something familiar suddenly appears different.

The Meistro Method encourages us to meet those moments with curiosity.

Because the person we haven't met yet may emerge through the questions we're willing to ask today.

And perhaps the most useful question of all is still the simplest.

"What am I not seeing yet?"

## The Thirty-Six Meistro Principles

We've arrived at the end of the main book. Here are the principles that emerged from the investigation, gathered into one reference.

PART I · BECOMING THE INVESTIGATOR

1. Investigate before you identify.
2. Observe the process, not just the result.
3. A description is a hypothesis about a pattern, not a sentence handed down for life.
4. Respect the experience. Investigate the explanation.
5. Notice the transition.
6. Uncertainty is information, not failure.

PART II · THE ARCHAEOLOGY OF YOU
7. Your childhood is evidence of development, not proof of destiny.
8. Follow what attracts your attention, then investigate why it holds you.
9. Your abilities have histories, even when you don't remember the lessons.
10. Failure becomes useful when we investigate the difference between expectation and outcome.
11. Other people influence your identity, but their descriptions do not determine its boundaries.
12. Your life story is a model of your experience, not the experience itself.

PART III · MAPPING YOUR COGNITIVE LANDSCAPE
13. The form in which a thought is experienced is not necessarily the form in which it must be expressed.
14. An internal picture can reveal possibilities, but understanding grows when the picture meets evidence.
15. A vivid memory is evidence of remembering, not automatic proof of what happened.
16. Attention is not simply the ability to stay focused. It is the ongoing allocation of mental resources among competing possibilities.
17. Intuition deserves attention, but confidence must be earned through evidence.
18. Follow the connection, but investigate the relationship.
19. Capability grows when action produces feedback that changes the next action.

PART IV · THE EMOTIONAL LANDSCAPE
20. Feelings are information about your experience, not automatic instructions for your behaviour.
21. Curiosity creates possibilities, but its value depends on what we learn, experience, and give up along the way.
22. When action is difficult, investigate the conditions before judging the person.
23. Take responsibility for what happened without turning the event into a permanent definition of who you are.
24. Connection becomes more reliable when recognition is joined by curiosity, verification, and respect for difference.
25. Protect your values, but allow your understanding of yourself to change.

PART V · THE LIVING LABORATORY
26. Treat your experiences as evidence to investigate, not conclusions to defend.
27. Before searching for an answer, investigate what your question assumes and what answering it would actually tell you.
28. Change one thing deliberately, observe more than one outcome, and remain cautious about what the result proves.
29. Recognise patterns freely, but earn confidence by examining counterexamples, missing information, and alternative explanations.
30. When evidence challenges your understanding, investigate the contradiction before defending the explanation.
31. Use conversation to expand and examine your thinking, but never confuse a convincing response with verified understanding.

PART VI · BUILDING YOUR PERSONAL OPERATING MANUAL
32. Investigate the conditions that make your capabilities available before judging yourself by the results produced under unfavourable conditions.
33. Design practical systems around observed behaviour, and make those systems easier to use, recover, and revise than the problems they are meant to solve.
34. Use your own mind as a starting point for understanding others, never as the final template for how they must think.
35. Build your personal operating manual as a living record of observed patterns, useful strategies, and unresolved questions, and revise it whenever better evidence becomes available.
36. Understand the person you are today without assuming that your current understanding defines the person you can become.
