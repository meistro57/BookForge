# APPENDIX A

## The Meistro Self-Discovery Interview

A guided investigation into the experiences, habits, capabilities, emotions, and patterns that make up your personal cognitive landscape.

### Before You Begin: You're Not Taking a Test

Imagine sitting across from someone who has never met you.

They don't know your history.

They don't know your accomplishments.

They don't know what you struggle with.

And they haven't already decided what kind of person you are.

They're interested in something much simpler.

They want to know what it's like to be you.

Not what your résumé says.

Not what a personality test says.

Not what your parents, teachers, employers, or friends have said.

They want to understand your experience.

What attracts your attention?

How do ideas appear?

What happens when you encounter something unfamiliar?

What makes you feel capable?

What creates resistance?

And what have you learned about yourself that you've never quite found the words to explain?

Now imagine that the interviewer isn't trying to diagnose you.

They aren't searching for a disorder.

Or attempting to place you in a category.

They're simply asking questions.

And when you answer, they become more curious.

"Can you tell me about a time that happened?"

"What did you notice first?"

"Was it always like that?"

"What happens when the conditions change?"

And occasionally:

"How do you know?"

That's the spirit of the Meistro Self-Discovery Interview.

It isn't a clinical assessment or a validated psychological test. It isn't intended to diagnose conditions, measure intelligence, or determine a personality type.

It's a structured exercise in self-observation and autobiographical reflection.

Some answers may reveal useful patterns.

Others may expose contradictions.

And some questions may lead somewhere completely unexpected.

All three outcomes are valuable.

The goal is not to produce a perfect description of yourself.

The goal is to begin collecting better evidence about how you experience, interpret, and interact with the world.

## How to Use This Interview

There are no points to earn and no ideal answers.

You can write your responses, record them aloud, discuss them with someone you trust, or work through them conversationally with an AI assistant.

You don't need to complete the interview in one sitting. In fact, several shorter sessions may produce more thoughtful observations than a single exhausting one.

For every question, you have four useful options: answer it, describe your uncertainty, provide an example, or leave it unanswered.

A question that doesn't make sense today may become meaningful later.

And an answer that feels obvious may become more interesting when you examine a specific experience.

### The five rules of the interview

1. Describe before explaining. Begin with what you remember experiencing or doing. Investigate the explanation afterwards.
2. Use examples whenever possible. A specific event often reveals more than a general description.
3. Preserve uncertainty. "I don't know" is a legitimate observation.
4. Look for exceptions. A pattern becomes more useful when you understand its boundaries.
5. Don't force a conclusion. The purpose is investigation, not producing an impressive personal profile.

One final recommendation: whenever a question brings up a memory, record approximately when it occurred and how certain you are about the details. Memories can be meaningful without being perfectly accurate.

# Interview One

## The Person Before the Explanation

Exploring childhood, early curiosity, and the experiences that preceded your current understanding of yourself.

We begin with childhood.

Not because childhood determines everything that follows.

And not because every adult characteristic must have an early explanation.

We begin because early experiences may reveal interests, learning opportunities, and behavioural patterns that are difficult to notice when examining only the present.

But remember: childhood memories are reconstructions. Treat them as useful starting points, not unquestionable recordings.

### Questions 1–10: Early Experience

1\. What is one of your earliest memories of being completely absorbed in something?

Where were you? What were you doing? What held your attention?

2\. As a child, what did you investigate without anyone asking you to?

Think about objects you dismantled, places you explored, questions you asked, or activities you repeated.

3\. What kinds of problems did you enjoy solving?

Were they physical, social, imaginative, mechanical, artistic, mathematical, or something else?

4\. What did adults frequently tell you about yourself?

Which descriptions seemed accurate? Which felt wrong? Which are still part of your self-image?

5\. What did you learn to do before anyone formally taught you?

Describe how the ability developed, if you remember.

6\. What did you find unusually difficult compared with the people around you?

How did others respond? What explanations did you form?

7\. When did you first remember feeling genuinely capable?

What happened that made you recognise your ability?

8\. What childhood activity would you have continued for hours if nobody interrupted you?

What happened when you were required to stop?

9\. What rules or expectations did you resist?

Were you resisting the rule itself, the reason behind it, the way it was enforced, or something else?

10\. What childhood experience do you suspect influenced you, but haven't examined carefully?

What evidence would help you understand its influence?

### The investigator's follow-up

After answering, choose two memories.

For each, ask:

- What do I actually remember?
- What meaning have I attached to it?
- Could there be another explanation?
- Does anything similar appear in my adult life?

Don't conclude that you've discovered your original personality.

You've begun investigating your developmental history.

That's enough.

# Interview Two

## Following the Trail of Curiosity

Investigating what attracts your attention, what sustains your interest, and how curiosity influences your choices.

Curiosity can be one of the most revealing aspects of a person's life.

But it's not a single, uniform force.

We may be curious because something is unfamiliar.

Because a problem seems solvable.

Because we sense a hidden relationship.

Or because understanding the subject could help us accomplish something meaningful.

This interview examines the structure of your curiosity.

### Questions 11–20: Attraction and Exploration

11\. What subjects can capture your attention almost immediately?

What do those subjects have in common?

12\. When you encounter something unfamiliar, what is your first impulse?

To examine it? Ask questions? Search for information? Experiment? Observe someone else?

13\. What kinds of questions are difficult for you to leave unanswered?

Why do those particular questions matter?

14\. Describe a time when curiosity led you somewhere you hadn't intended to go.

What was the original question? What did you discover along the way?

15\. When does curiosity become action?

What makes you move from wondering about something to actually investigating it?

16\. What happens when the mystery is solved?

Do you feel satisfied? Lose interest? Find a deeper question? Begin building something?

17\. Have you ever abandoned an important task because something more interesting appeared?

What made the new activity compelling? What were the consequences?

18\. What is the most unexpected subject you've become fascinated by?

Did it connect to an earlier interest?

19\. Do you prefer discovering something independently or being shown the answer?

Does your preference change depending on the subject?

20\. If you had an entire week with no obligations, what would you investigate?

Why that subject?

### The investigator's follow-up

Look for recurring features.

Does your curiosity respond most strongly to uncertainty, novelty, practical problems, beauty, relationships, or opportunities to create?

Then look for exceptions.

A person who enjoys technical mysteries may also become absorbed in music, history, or human relationships for entirely different reasons.

Don't force every interest into one explanation.

# Interview Three

## How Thought Appears

Exploring the forms in which you experience thinking before, during, and after deliberate reasoning.

This section requires unusual care.

We often assume that thinking feels roughly the same to everyone.

But people report different experiences of inner speech, imagery, bodily sensations, and other forms of conscious thought.

Your answers describe your subjective experience.

They don't, by themselves, establish the underlying neural mechanisms.

### Questions 21–30: The Languages of Thought

21\. When you think about a familiar place, what happens in your experience?

Do you see imagery? Recall details verbally? Sense the layout? Experience something else?

22\. When you're solving a problem, do words appear before the solution, after it, or throughout the process?

Can you describe a recent example?

23\. Can you voluntarily picture an object in your mind?

If so, how vivid is the experience? If not, how do you think about the object's properties?

24\. Do you experience an internal voice or verbal narration?

When is it most noticeable? Does it change with the activity?

25\. Have you ever understood something but struggled to explain it?

How did you eventually communicate the understanding?

26\. When you remember an event, what appears first?

An image? A feeling? A sequence? Words? A sense of place?

27\. What happens when someone asks you to explain an idea before you're ready?

Does the question help organise the thought, interrupt it, or produce another response?

28\. Do you think differently when speaking aloud than when thinking silently?

What changes?

29\. What kinds of problems are easier with diagrams, physical objects, or visual models?

Which problems are easier with words, symbols, or conversation?

30\. Describe a moment when a solution seemed to arrive suddenly.

What had you been doing beforehand? How did you verify the solution?

### The investigator's follow-up

Choose one familiar task and one unfamiliar task.

Observe how your thinking changes between them.

The important discovery may not be that you're a particular type of thinker.

It may be that you use different representations depending on what the task requires.

# Interview Four

## Learning, Skill, and the Capability Loop

Investigating how unfamiliar activities become things you can do.

Think of an ability you possess today that once seemed difficult.

Somewhere between those two states, learning occurred.

The question is how.

### Questions 31–40: Becoming Capable

31\. What is something you can do well that you never learned through formal instruction?

How did you acquire it?

32\. When beginning a new skill, what do you usually do first?

Read, watch, imitate, experiment, ask questions, or begin attempting the task?

33\. Describe a skill that took much longer to develop than you expected.

What eventually helped?

34\. How do you respond when your first attempt fails?

Does the failure increase curiosity, create frustration, or change your approach?

35\. What kind of feedback helps you improve most effectively?

Immediate results, detailed explanations, examples, correction, or independent comparison?

36\. Have you ever discovered that knowledge from one field helped you understand another?

What transferred between the two?

37\. When does repetition help you learn?

When does it become unproductive?

38\. Describe a time when someone taught you in a way that didn't work.

What was missing?

39\. How do you recognise that you've genuinely learned something?

Can you explain it, demonstrate it, predict outcomes, or apply it in unfamiliar circumstances?

40\. What would you like to learn that you've avoided beginning?

What do you believe is standing in the way?

### The investigator's follow-up

Select one capability and reconstruct its development.

What could you do at the beginning?

What did you attempt?

What feedback changed your approach?

And what can you do now?

This becomes your first personal capability-loop record.

# Interview Five

## Attention and the Conditions of Engagement

Investigating what attracts, holds, redirects, and interrupts your attention.

Attention is not simply the ability to concentrate.

It involves selecting among competing possibilities.

Your attention may behave differently depending on the task, environment, emotional state, and available feedback.

### Questions 41–50: Attention in Motion

41\. What activities make you lose track of time?

What do they have in common?

42\. What kinds of tasks are difficult to begin even when you know they're important?

Where does the resistance appear?

43\. What happens when someone interrupts you during complicated reasoning?

How long does it take to recover?

44\. Are there environments where concentration becomes noticeably easier?

What distinguishes them?

45\. What kinds of distractions are most difficult to ignore?

Sounds, conversations, ideas, notifications, movement, or something else?

46\. How do you respond to deadlines?

Do they sharpen your attention, create pressure, or produce different effects depending on the situation?

47\. What happens when a task becomes repetitive?

Does your attention change as familiarity increases?

48\. Can you deliberately redirect your attention when something interesting appears?

Which strategies help?

49\. Describe a time when you remained engaged with a difficult task for an unusually long period.

What supported that engagement? What was the cost afterwards?

50\. What is one change to your environment that might make an important task easier?

How could you test it?

### The investigator's follow-up

Compare two examples of the same task under different conditions.

Avoid immediately concluding that the task itself explains the difference.

Investigate the surrounding circumstances.

# Interview Six

## Intuition, Decisions, and Uncertainty

Exploring how you recognise possibilities, make judgements, and decide when an answer is reliable.

Some decisions involve deliberate comparison.

Others begin with a feeling that something is right or wrong.

Both can be useful.

Both can be mistaken.

The question is how you recognise the difference.

### Questions 51–60: The Decision Process

51\. When faced with a difficult decision, what do you notice first?

Possibilities, risks, emotions, practical constraints, or something else?

52\. Have you ever known what you wanted to do before you could explain why?

What happened when you examined the reasoning?

53\. Describe a time when your intuition was accurate.

What experience or information may have contributed?

54\. Describe a time when your intuition was wrong.

What did you learn?

55\. How do you decide that you have enough information to act?

What changes when the stakes are high?

56\. What kinds of uncertainty feel exciting?

What kinds feel uncomfortable?

57\. Do you tend to generate many possible solutions or settle quickly on one?

When does each approach help?

58\. How do you respond when new evidence contradicts your initial judgement?

Can you recall a specific example?

59\. What decisions do you prefer to make independently?

Which benefit from other perspectives?

60\. What is one belief about yourself that you currently hold with less certainty than you once did?

What changed your confidence?

### The investigator's follow-up

Choose a recent decision.

Separate the initial impression from the reasons you later developed.

Then ask whether the later explanation genuinely reflects what influenced the decision, or whether some of it may be a reconstruction.

# Interview Seven

## Emotions, Motivation, and Personal Meaning

Investigating what feelings communicate, how they influence behaviour, and what gives activities significance.

This section may be more personal.

You can skip any question.

You don't need to disclose sensitive experiences to another person or an AI system in order to benefit from the exercise.

The goal is to understand your experience, not expose every private detail.

### Questions 61–70: The Emotional Landscape

61\. What activities leave you feeling satisfied even when they're difficult?

What makes the effort worthwhile?

62\. What situations reliably produce frustration?

Is the frustration connected to uncertainty, restriction, unfairness, interruption, or something else?

63\. How do you recognise that you're becoming overwhelmed?

What changes in your thinking, behaviour, or physical experience?

64\. What helps you regain perspective after an emotionally difficult experience?

How reliable are those strategies?

65\. When do you feel most capable?

What conditions surround that feeling?

66\. What kinds of recognition matter to you?

Praise, understanding, trust, practical appreciation, or something else?

67\. Have you ever continued pursuing something after it stopped being meaningful?

Why did you continue?

68\. What activities would you still value even if nobody else knew you were doing them?

What does that suggest about their importance?

69\. When have you confused a strong feeling with certainty about what was happening?

What did you discover later?

70\. What do you want more of in your life that isn't adequately represented by productivity or achievement?

Why does it matter?

### The investigator's follow-up

Choose one recurring emotional response.

Describe what happens before it, during it, and afterwards.

Then distinguish the feeling from the explanation you attach to it.

Remember the principle from Chapter Twenty:

Respect the feeling. Investigate the story. Choose the response.

# Interview Eight

## Connection and Other Minds

Exploring communication, collaboration, misunderstanding, and the experience of being understood.

We learn about ourselves partly through other people.

But their experiences differ from ours.

This section investigates how those differences influence connection.

### Questions 71–80: Understanding and Being Understood

71\. When do you feel that another person genuinely understands you?

What do they do that creates that experience?

72\. What is something you often struggle to explain to other people?

What makes it difficult to communicate?

73\. Do you prefer thinking independently before discussing an idea, or developing it through conversation?

Does the preference change with the subject?

74\. What kinds of questions help you think more clearly?

What kinds interrupt your thinking?

75\. Describe a disagreement that turned out to involve different assumptions rather than fundamentally different goals.

How did you discover the difference?

76\. Have you ever assumed another person experienced something the same way you did?

What revealed the difference?

77\. What do you value most in a collaborator?

Complementary expertise, curiosity, reliability, patience, challenge, or something else?

78\. When someone disagrees with you, what makes the disagreement productive?

What makes it difficult?

79\. What have other people helped you discover about yourself?

Which observations have you verified independently?

80\. What is one thing you'd like people to understand about how you think or communicate?

How could you explain it without expecting them to think identically?

### The investigator's follow-up

Choose one relationship or collaboration that works particularly well.

Investigate what makes it effective.

Don't assume the answer is simply that you're similar.

Sometimes complementary differences are the source of the benefit.

# Interview Nine

## Failure, Change, and the Stories You Carry

Investigating how experiences become beliefs about identity and how those beliefs change.

A person doesn't simply remember events.

They interpret them.

Over time, interpretations can become stories about who they are.

Some stories are useful.

Others become restrictive.

### Questions 81–90: The Personal Narrative

81\. What failure taught you something important?

What did you learn, and how do you know the lesson was useful?

82\. What experience changed how you understood your own abilities?

What did you believe before and afterwards?

83\. What label have you carried for years?

Where did it come from? Does it still fit?

84\. What is something you once believed you couldn't do but later learned?

What changed?

85\. Have you ever mistaken an environmental difficulty for a personal limitation?

What helped you recognise the difference?

86\. What story do you tell most often about your past?

Why has that story become important?

87\. What part of your history do you think you've oversimplified?

What details might complicate the familiar explanation?

88\. What is one experience you interpret differently today than you did when it happened?

What new information changed the interpretation?

89\. What recurring difficulty have you been treating as a character flaw rather than investigating as a process?

What would a more precise description look like?

90\. What would you tell your younger self about something they misunderstood?

What would you avoid claiming to know about their future?

### The investigator's follow-up

Choose one long-standing self-description.

Write the evidence supporting it.

Then write the evidence that complicates it.

Finally, replace the broad label with a more specific, testable description.

# Interview Ten

## The Person You're Becoming

Bringing the investigation into the present and opening it towards the future.

This final interview is different.

We're no longer looking only for explanations of existing patterns.

We're examining how self-knowledge might influence future choices.

### Questions 91–100: Development and Possibility

91\. What capability would you most like to develop over the next year?

Why that one?

92\. What part of your current life supports the person you want to become?

What part makes development more difficult?

93\. What have you recently discovered about yourself that surprised you?

How has it affected your behaviour?

94\. What activity would you try if you didn't feel obligated to be immediately good at it?

What would the first experiment look like?

95\. What personal pattern would you like to understand more accurately?

What evidence is missing?

96\. Which of your current beliefs about yourself might be outdated?

What could challenge them?

97\. What kind of environment would help you develop your next important capability?

Which parts can you create?

98\. What do you hope remains important to you ten years from now?

What might reasonably change?

99\. What question about yourself are you most curious to investigate next?

Why has it become important now?

100\. If you could leave one question for your future self, what would it be?

And what would you hope they'd discover by attempting to answer it?

# After the Interview

## Turning One Hundred Answers Into Useful Knowledge

Completing the questions is not the end of the exercise.

It's the beginning of the analysis.

You now have a collection of observations, memories, interpretations, and possible patterns.

The next task is to organise them without forcing them into a single explanation.

### Step One: Identify recurring observations

Review your answers.

Notice subjects or behaviours that appear repeatedly.

Perhaps experimentation appears across several learning experiences.

Perhaps interruptions appear in descriptions of difficult working conditions.

Perhaps certain kinds of conversations repeatedly help you clarify ideas.

Record the repetitions.

But don't immediately interpret them as permanent traits.

### Step Two: Separate observation from explanation

Consider this answer:

"I lose interest when a project becomes repetitive."

That's a self-reported pattern.

Now consider:

"I lose interest because my brain is wired for novelty."

That's an explanation.

The explanation may be plausible, but it requires additional evidence.

The Meistro Method encourages you to keep the two statements separate.

You can use a simple three-column record.

| Observation | Possible explanation | What could test it? |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| I delay starting certain routine tasks. | The starting steps may be unclear or unrewarding. | Compare task initiation with and without a defined first action. |
| I become absorbed in unfamiliar technical problems. | Exploration and immediate feedback may support engagement. | Compare similar tasks with different feedback conditions. |
| I sometimes struggle to explain a spatial idea immediately. | Verbal translation may require additional time. | Compare explanations with and without a sketch or model. |

These examples are hypotheses, not diagnoses.

### Step Three: Look for contradictions

Suppose you wrote:

"I prefer working alone."

But elsewhere you described your best ideas emerging through conversation.

Don't immediately choose one statement and discard the other.

Investigate the difference.

Perhaps independent work supports detailed implementation.

While conversation supports exploration.

Or perhaps the preferred arrangement depends on the collaborator.

A contradiction may reveal that your original description was too broad.

### Step Four: Identify practical consequences

Ask what you can actually do with the information.

Can you change your environment?

Improve a workflow?

Choose a more effective learning strategy?

Explain a communication preference?

Or design an experiment?

If an observation doesn't yet suggest an action, that's fine.

Preserve it for future investigation.

Not every discovery needs immediate practical value.

### Step Five: Create your first research summary

Your initial summary doesn't need to be elaborate.

A useful format is:

## My First Meistro Research Summary

Three observations I want to preserve

Two possible patterns worth investigating

Conditions that appear to influence my behaviour

One important uncertainty or contradiction

One small experiment I could conduct

## A Note for Interview Partners

If you're guiding someone through this interview, your responsibility is not to explain them to themselves.

It's to help them examine their experience.

Ask for examples.

Clarify meanings.

And notice when an interpretation has been introduced without sufficient evidence.

Avoid leading questions that suggest a preferred answer.

Don't treat emotional disclosure as permission to make a diagnosis.

And don't turn an interesting personal observation into a claim about hidden cognitive mechanisms.

The person being interviewed should be free to disagree with your interpretation.

They should also be free to decline questions, stop the session, and decide what happens to their responses.

A useful interviewer is curious, careful, and willing to be corrected.

The interview belongs to the person whose experience is being investigated.

## A Note for Using AI as an Interview Partner

An AI assistant can be useful during this process.

It can ask follow-up questions.

Organise responses.

Suggest possible patterns.

And identify contradictions.

But it should not be treated as an authority on the user's internal experience.

AI systems can produce persuasive explanations that are unsupported by evidence.

They may also misunderstand context or reinforce an attractive interpretation.

If you use AI, consider beginning with an instruction such as:

> Help me investigate my own thinking using the Meistro Self-Discovery Interview. Ask one question at a time. Encourage specific examples. Keep my observations separate from your interpretations. Identify alternative explanations and exceptions. Do not diagnose me or claim to know my internal experience better than I do. At the end, summarise what we've observed, what remains uncertain, and what might be worth testing.

If you're discussing private experiences, consider where your responses will be stored, who may have access to them, and whether you want the information retained.

You can also complete the interview entirely offline.

The method doesn't depend on AI.

It depends on careful investigation.

## Closing Reflection: The Question That Opens the Door

One hundred questions.

And perhaps you're expecting that somewhere among the answers lies the secret to who you are.

Maybe a hidden pattern.

A defining trait.

Or a single explanation that makes everything fall into place.

But that isn't what this interview promises.

You may discover recurring tendencies.

You may recognise conditions that support your abilities.

You may identify stories about yourself that need revision.

And you may find questions you didn't know you wanted to ask.

That's the beginning of useful self-understanding.

The most important answer may not be the one that feels most impressive.

It may be the one that makes you stop and think:

"Wait. I've never noticed that before."

Because that moment changes the investigation.

Something previously invisible has become available for examination.

You can observe it again.

Compare it with other experiences.

Test an explanation.

And perhaps use what you learn to make a practical change.

That's how the Meistro Method begins.

Not with a diagnosis.

Not with a label.

And not with a finished theory of the self.

But with a question.

Followed by another.

And another.

Until the person asking begins to see their own experience with greater clarity.

You don't need to know who you are before beginning the investigation.

You begin by becoming curious about what you actually do, feel, notice, remember, and understand.

And from there, the work continues.
