# CHAPTER 14
## Relationships

There's a conversation I've had more than once, and if you're in a long relationship you've
probably had your own version.

It starts with something small. A thing I said I'd do and didn't. And it escalates fast,
because we're not actually talking about the thing — we're talking about the ninety
previous things, and about what it means that there have been ninety, and about what she's
supposed to conclude from the pattern.

And I'm standing there knowing two facts at once. I love this person. And I have given her
a hundred pieces of evidence that I don't.

That's the chapter. Not communication tips. That.

---

### The trap

Here's the thing that damages these relationships more than any other, and most books
somehow don't name it.

**Your partner becomes your manager.**

It happens gradually and nobody chooses it. You forget something. They remind you. That
works, so next time they remind you earlier. Then they start tracking the things you're
likely to forget, because it's easier than dealing with the fallout. Then they're carrying
the calendar, the appointments, the bills, the mental list of what needs doing — not
because they wanted the job, but because the alternative was watching things fall.

Now look at what both of you have.

**They're exhausted and quietly furious**, because they've acquired a second full-time job
they never applied for, and because being the person who reminds is a lonely, thankless
position that makes them feel like a nag in their own home.

**And you're humiliated**, because you're an adult being managed, and every reminder lands
as *we both know you can't be trusted with this*, and you can't even argue because the
record supports it.

So they resent you for needing it. You resent them for providing it. And the actual
relationship — the two people who liked each other — has been slowly replaced by an
administrative arrangement neither of you agreed to.

**Nobody is the villain here.** That's what makes it so hard to fix. They started
reminding because they love you. You started resenting it because you're not a child. Both
of those are reasonable, and together they will hollow out a marriage.

---

### Their side, honestly

Let me stop talking to you for a minute, because there's a decent chance your partner is
reading this chapter, possibly before you do.

If that's you: what you're dealing with is real, and it isn't unfair of you to find it
hard.

You're not imagining that you carry more. You're not being unkind when you feel done. The
frustration of explaining something for the fourth time, of being the only one who knows
when the insurance is due, of watching them lose six hours to a project while the thing
you asked for goes untouched — that's a legitimate grievance and no diagnosis erases it.

You've probably been told to be more understanding. Perhaps you've read that their brain
works differently and you should adjust your expectations. Some of that's true. But
understanding isn't infinite, and it isn't a substitute for the person actually doing
something.

**A diagnosis explains the pattern. It doesn't obligate you to absorb it forever.**

If you're at the end of your patience after years of this, you're not a bad person. You're
a tired one.

---

### Back to us: explanation is not a permission slip

Now the hard part for our side, and I'm not going to be gentle about it.

Once you have the language in this book, there's a serious temptation to use it as a
shield. *I have ADHD. Time blindness is a real thing. My brain doesn't work that way.*

Every one of those sentences is true, and used at the wrong moment every one of them is a
way of making your problem into their problem.

Here's the test I use, and it's simple: **an explanation is followed by a change. An excuse
is followed by a repeat.**

If you explain time blindness to your partner and then buy the timer and start using the
multiplier, that's an explanation. If you explain time blindness to your partner and then
show up late in exactly the same way next week, you didn't explain anything. You just
found a more sophisticated way to say *deal with it.*

They can't tell the difference from the outside except by what happens next. Neither can
you, honestly.

---

### The thing that isn't your fault but breaks hearts anyway

One more pattern, because it causes an enormous amount of quiet pain and almost nobody
explains it.

At the start of a relationship, you were probably *extraordinary*. Total attention. Endless
interest. Hours of conversation. The person felt more seen than they'd ever been.

That was real. It was also, partly, novelty — and novelty is one of the four things that
switch your system on.

Then it faded. Not your feelings, your *attention*. And from where they're standing those
are indistinguishable. They got the most attentive person they'd ever met, and then over a
couple of years they got someone half-listening with a phone in his hand, and the obvious
conclusion is that you stopped loving them.

You didn't. The novelty ran out, which it does for everyone — it's just that for us the
drop is much steeper, because more of the attention was novelty-driven to begin with.

**Knowing this doesn't fix it. But it needs saying out loud to the person you love**, because
otherwise they are carrying a completely wrong explanation for something that hurt them
badly.

And then it needs deliberate attention, on purpose, imported from outside — because it is
not coming back on its own.

---

### What actually helps

Four things, and they follow directly from Chapter 13.

**Stop promising. Start building.**

"I'll remember" is worthless currency between you now. You've spent it. They've heard it a
hundred times and watched it fail, and one more promise reads as one more thing that won't
happen.

A system is a real offer. *"I'm putting a shared calendar on the fridge and I'll check it
every morning with my coffee."* That's specific, it's external, and they can see whether
it's happening. It also transfers the work from your memory — the broken part — to a wall.

**Take the job back, one piece at a time.**

You can't undo the manager dynamic in a conversation. You undo it by removing items from
their load and *keeping them removed*. Pick one thing. Own it completely, with a system.
Do it for two months without being reminded.

One thing done reliably rebuilds more than ten promised.

**Ask for the reminder you actually want.**

Most reminders land badly because of the form, not the content. Work out what doesn't sting
and ask for it explicitly: *"Don't ask if I did it. Just say 'Thursday' and I'll know."*
That gives them a way to help that doesn't require becoming your parent, which is what they
want too.

**Repair fast and without a defence.**

You'll still fail at things. The difference between a relationship that survives this and
one that doesn't is very often the repair — how quickly you say *I got that wrong, here's
what I'm changing*, with no paragraph about your brain attached.

Fast, specific, no defence. The explanation can come later, when nobody's bleeding.

---

### Friends, briefly

Same principles, lower stakes, so shorter section.

You'll forget birthdays and go quiet for four months and reappear with no sense that time
passed. The good friendships survive it — the ones that last are with people who understand
that you'll vanish and that it means nothing.

Two things worth doing. Set the reminders, because caring is not the same as remembering
and only one of those is visible. And say it plainly to the people who matter: *if I go
quiet, it isn't about you — reach out, I'll always be glad.* That single sentence has saved
more of my friendships than anything else I've done.

---

### What I want you to take from this

The wiring makes some parts of loving people harder. It doesn't make you bad at it, and it
doesn't mean the people around you are wrong for finding it difficult.

Both of those are true, and the whole of this chapter lives in holding them together.

**They have a legitimate grievance. You have a legitimate explanation. And an explanation
only counts if something changes.**

Next chapter is where you spend most of your waking hours, and where the mismatch either
costs you everything or finally starts paying.
