# CHAPTER 8
## Famous Minds

Every book like this one has this chapter, and most of them do it badly. I'm going to
tell you how, then do it a different way, because the standard version has quietly hurt a
lot of people and I'd rather not add to that.

---

### The problem with the usual list

Open almost any ADHD book to the inspiring-figures chapter and you'll find the same
names. Leonardo da Vinci. Thomas Edison. Einstein, usually. Mozart if the author is
feeling bold.

Leonardo da Vinci died in 1519.

Nobody examined him. Nobody assessed him. The concept did not exist and would not exist
for four hundred years. What we have is that he left unfinished projects and notebooks
full of scattered ideas — which is offered as evidence of ADHD, as though unfinished
projects were rare among human beings, or as though "was curious about many things" were
a diagnostic criterion.

Same with Edison. Boundless energy, unorthodox methods, lots of experiments. That
describes ADHD. It also describes a great many driven people who never had it.

This kind of retroactive diagnosis is unfalsifiable — you cannot check it, and the dead
cannot object — which is exactly why it's so popular. It lets a writer attach your
condition to a genius at no cost.

Here's why I'm not willing to do it. **A book that will make one claim it can't support
will make others.** If I hand you Leonardo in Chapter 8, you have no reason to trust me in
Chapter 12 when I tell you something about hyperfocus that actually matters. I'd rather
have a shorter chapter and keep the credit.

---

### The bigger problem: you're only shown the winners

Even with people who genuinely have it, the list has a deeper flaw, and this is the one I
want you to see clearly because it's been used against you.

**You are only ever shown the ones who made it.**

The billionaire. The Olympian. The Grammy winner. Nobody writes the chapter about the
people with identical wiring who lost the house, or never got out of a job that was
killing them, or are forty-three and still hearing the same three sentences from their
childhood.

Those people exist in far greater numbers. They aren't in the chapter because their
stories don't sell books.

So what does the standard chapter actually communicate to a reader who's struggling?

*Other people with your brain became billionaires and Olympians. What's your excuse?*

That's not encouragement. That's the same message you've been getting since third grade,
wearing a nicer jacket. I've felt it reading those chapters and I refuse to write one.

---

### The version that's actually useful

So let's do something different with the same material.

**Stop looking at who they are. Look at what kind of work they do.**

That's where the information is, and once you see it you can't unsee it.

Take the people who have genuinely spoken about their own diagnosis — Richard Branson has
talked openly about his for years. Solange Knowles has discussed how it shapes her
creative process. Simone Biles's diagnosis became public in 2016 when her medical records
were stolen and published, and she responded by talking about it plainly instead of hiding
— which took more nerve than any list of superpowers.

*(Verify every name before this goes to print. Self-disclosure is the only standard I'd
accept, and even then people's public statements shift. Nobody gets diagnosed by an
author.)*

Now look at the work itself.

Entrepreneurship: no fixed structure, constant novelty, high stakes, you set the schedule,
and the thing that gets rewarded is spotting what nobody else spotted.

Elite gymnastics: intense physical engagement, immediate feedback, high stakes, and a task
that demands total absorption for very short bursts.

Music and visual art: self-directed, sensory, driven entirely by interest, no correct
sequence to follow.

Notice the pattern? Not one of them is *sit at a desk and process low-interest material in
order for eight hours.*

**These aren't people who beat ADHD. They're people who ended up in rooms where the wiring
pays.** Some of them chose it deliberately. Most of them, I'd bet, wandered into it and
stayed because it was the first place they weren't failing.

That's not inspiration. That's evidence — and it's the same evidence Chapter 5 pointed at.
The game is placement.

---

### The role model I'd actually recommend

Here's the thing nobody tells you: **you probably don't need a billionaire. You need
somebody whose life you could plausibly have.**

So let me offer myself, not because I'm impressive but because I'm reachable.

I'm a steel detailer. I have never been famous. I don't have a degree — I don't have a
high school diploma. My career high point in the trade was a set of canopies I built at
twenty-four that some later owner tore out without knowing what they were.

And that work ended up in a Schiffer collector's book, on the opening page of a chapter,
facing a 19th-century masterpiece at Monte Carlo. I carried that hardcover into meetings
as my resume, because I had nothing else — no portfolio, no credentials, no letters. Just:
*here, look at page 42.* It opened the door to every major project I've worked on since.

That's not a story about greatness. It's a story about one guy finding the specific kind
of work his brain would actually do, and then getting a bit lucky with a phone call
nobody else wanted to answer.

That's available to you. Not the fame — the fit.

**Find the people one or two steps ahead of you in a direction you could actually walk.**
The tradesman who found his niche. The nurse who moved to the ER and stopped drowning. The
teacher who switched to shop class. Those people will teach you more in one conversation
than a chapter about Leonardo ever will, and unlike Leonardo, you can buy them a coffee
and ask.

---

### What to do with this

Three things, and they're all small.

**One: stop measuring against the outliers.** Branson's success is not a debt you owe.
Somebody with your wiring being extraordinary tells you exactly nothing about what you're
obligated to be.

**Two: when you read a success story, skip the person and study the conditions.** What did
their day actually look like? How much of it was self-directed? How fast was the feedback?
How much novelty? That's transferable. Their personality isn't.

**Three: find your local examples.** Real people, in reach, in your field or one you could
get to. They're not hard to find once you're looking — this wiring is everywhere in the
trades, in emergency work, in kitchens, in startups, in any job where the day doesn't
repeat.

---

The famous ones prove ADHD isn't disqualifying. Fine. Worth knowing.

But they don't tell you what to do on Monday, and the version of this chapter that leaves
you feeling inadequate next to an Olympian has done you harm rather than good.

What tells you what to do on Monday is the pattern underneath their success — the shape of
the work, not the size of the name.

Which brings us to Part Three, where we stop describing your brain and start building
around it. Everything up to now has been the diagnosis. The rest of the book is the
drawing set.
