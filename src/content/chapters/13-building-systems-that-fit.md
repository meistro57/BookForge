# CHAPTER 13
## Building Systems That Fit

Everything in this part of the book has been a separate tool. A visual timer. Body
doubling. The hour before you reply. Putting the real work where you'll trip over it.

This chapter is where they stop being tricks and become one thing.

Because there's a principle underneath all of them, and once you see it you can generate
your own instead of borrowing mine — which matters, because mine were built for my life
and you have a different one.

---

### The principle

Every technique in this book has done the same job:

**Move the work out of your head and into the world.**

The timer moves duration out of your head, where you can't feel it, onto a desk where you
can see it. Body doubling moves accountability out of your head, where it doesn't
generate, into a room where somebody else supplies it. The multiplier moves estimation out
of your gut, which is reliably wrong, onto paper. The hour delay moves the decision out of
the moment, where you're at four times volume, into later, where you're not.

None of these make you better at anything.

They all do the same thing: **they stop requiring you to be good at the thing you're not
good at.**

That's the whole method. That's what a system is, for us. Not organization. Not discipline.
**Anything that means a specific weakness no longer has to be a strength.**

---

### Why "just try harder" was always going to fail

Look at what most advice actually asks.

Remember appointments. Estimate accurately. Stay on task. Regulate your reaction. Notice
the time. Keep the details in mind.

Every one of those is asking you to do internally, by effort, a thing your system doesn't
do well. It's advice built by people whose brains do it automatically, who assume you're
not applying enough will.

Thirty years of applying more will taught me that the well is not the input. You cannot
willpower your way to a sense of duration you don't have, any more than you can concentrate
your way to better eyesight.

**Nobody tells a person with poor eyesight to focus harder. They give them glasses.**

Every technique in Part Three is glasses. That's all. And once you stop trying to fix your
eyes, you can start building spectacles for anything.

---

### How to build your own

Here's the process. It's four steps and it's genuinely this simple.

**Step one: find the mismatch.**

Not "what am I bad at" — that list is long and mostly makes you feel worse. The specific
question is: **what am I currently forcing myself to do internally that could be done
externally?**

Where are you relying on remembering? On noticing? On feeling? On wanting to?

Those four verbs are your search terms. Anywhere one of them is load-bearing, you have a
system waiting to be built.

**Step two: build the ugliest version that works.**

This is where most people lose, and it's a very ADHD way to lose: you decide to fix the
problem, and then you spend nine hours designing the perfect solution, and then you never
use it, and then you conclude systems don't work for you.

Systems work for you. Elaborate ones don't.

**A sticky note beats an app you'll abandon.** A whiteboard beats a methodology. A
spreadsheet beats a project management platform. The correct first version of anything is
embarrassing, and if it isn't, you've overbuilt it.

I say this as someone who genuinely enjoys building tools and has wasted entire weekends
building the tool instead of doing the thing. **Building the system is a legitimate form of
procrastination and it's one of our favorites.** Ugly and running today beats elegant and
theoretical forever.

**Step three: put it where the problem happens.**

A system you have to go and consult is a system that has failed. The whole point is that it
works without you deciding to use it — because deciding is the broken step.

The timer lives on the desk. The keys live in the bowl by the door. The note is on the
door itself, not in an app behind a lock screen. Whatever it is, it has to be **in the
path**, physically, at the moment of the problem.

**Step four: expect it to die, and don't take it personally.**

Here's the thing nobody says: **your systems will stop working, and it's not a character
failure.**

Novelty is part of what makes something work for us. When it's new, it's stimulating and
you use it. Six weeks in it's invisible, and you stop. Every person with this wiring has a
graveyard of abandoned systems and treats it as proof they're hopeless.

It isn't. It's the expected lifespan of the tool.

The fix isn't finding the one permanent system. It's accepting a rotation. When something
dies, replace it with a different one that does the same job. New timer, new location, new
format. **You're not failing to maintain a system. You're refreshing a component that's
consumed by design.**

That single reframe saved me more than any individual technique in this book.

---

### What I built, and what I'd tell you to build

I've built a lot of tools. Software for fabrication work. Systems for organizing my own
notes so that things I learned two years ago are still reachable. I built them because
nothing that existed fit the way my head works — every off-the-shelf option assumed a user
who remembers things and works in sequence.

That's not a brag and I'm not suggesting you learn to code. I want to make a narrower
point:

**I stopped trying to be someone who remembers, and built things that remember for me.**

The result is that my working memory limitations barely matter anymore. Not because they
improved — they didn't — but because almost nothing important now depends on them.

For you, that might mean a whiteboard. A paper notebook you never close. Voice memos. A
person you tell things to. The medium is irrelevant. The move is identical.

**Externalize memory. Externalize time. Externalize accountability. Externalize the
decision.**

Four systems, and between them they cover most of what this wiring makes hard.

---

### Placement is the biggest system of all

One last thing, and it's the largest lever in this book, so it goes here at the end of the
practical section.

Everything above is a system inside a life. But **the life itself is the system**, and it's
the one most worth engineering.

Chapter 5 said the game is placement. Chapter 8 showed you what it looks like from the
outside — that the people who did well weren't beating this thing, they were in rooms where
it paid.

All the timers and anchors in the world won't save a job that's eight hours of low-interest
sequential work in a quiet room. That's not a system problem, that's a *pairing* problem,
and there is no tool that fixes it. I've watched people build magnificent scaffolding to
survive a job that was never going to fit, when the actual answer was a different job.

I'm not telling you to quit anything. Most people can't, and I've got no patience for
advice that assumes otherwise. But I'd ask you to notice the difference between a problem
that needs a tool and a problem that needs a change of room — because we're very good at
building scaffolding, and sometimes the scaffolding is how we avoid noticing what it's
holding up.

The rest of this book is about rooms. Relationships, work, family, the shape of a life.

---

### This week

**Pick one thing you're currently doing with remembering, noticing, feeling, or wanting
to.** One. The most expensive one.

**Build the ugly version today.** Not the good version. Today's.

**Put it in the path.**

And when it stops working in six weeks, come back and build a different ugly thing. That's
not failure. That's how this works.
