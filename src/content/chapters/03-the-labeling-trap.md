# CHAPTER 3
## The Labeling Trap

I said at the end of the last chapter that the diagnosis was right and the framing was a
choice. I want to prove that to you now, because if you don't believe it, nothing else in
this book will hold.

Here's the proof. **The name has been changed roughly every twenty years, and every time,
it was changed by people making a judgment call.**

Start of the twentieth century, a British doctor named George Still describes a group of
children with what he called a defect of moral control. Moral. That's the word. These
kids were understood to have something wrong with their character.

Mid-century, it becomes **Minimal Brain Damage.** Then somebody points out there's no
demonstrable damage, so it's softened to **Minimal Brain Dysfunction.** Still broken,
just less specifically.

1968: **Hyperkinetic Reaction of Childhood.** Now it's about being too much, and it's a
kid thing, because adults apparently didn't have it.

1980: **Attention Deficit Disorder.** The focus swings from the body to the attention.
Now you're short of something.

1987: **Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder.** Both. You're short *and* you're too
much.

Then decades of refinement — subtypes, presentations, eventually a grudging admission
that adults have it too, which surprised no adult who had it.

Look at that list again. Moral defect. Brain damage. Brain dysfunction. Hyperkinetic
reaction. Deficit. Disorder.

**Same people. Same brains. Six different stories in a hundred years.**

The underlying thing being described stayed put the whole time. What changed was what
the describers thought it *meant* — and every single version, without exception, was
named for the problem it caused other people.

That's not a conspiracy. Nobody was being malicious. It's just what happens when the
people writing the definition are the people the behavior inconveniences.

*(One housekeeping note: I'm working from memory on those dates and I'd get them
verified before this goes to print. The shape is right — the name kept changing — but if
you're going to put history in a book, get the history right.)*

---

Now look at how the thing is actually diagnosed.

Read the criteria sometime. Really read them. They're a list of behaviors — loses things,
doesn't follow through, difficulty remaining seated, talks excessively, interrupts,
struggles with sustained mental effort.

Notice what almost all of them have in common. They're only problems in a room.

Difficulty remaining seated is a problem if you're required to remain seated. Talks
excessively is a problem where quiet is the rule. Doesn't follow through on instructions
is a problem in an instruction-following environment. Sustained mental effort on tedious
material — the criteria usually say something close to that — is a problem where the
material is tedious and sustained effort is the job.

Two settings generate nearly all of it: **the classroom and the office.**

I'm not saying the impairment isn't real. It's real. I've lived it and so have you, and I
have no patience for people who wave that away. Missing your kid's appointment is real.
Losing the job is real. The pile of unopened mail is real.

What I'm saying is narrower and it matters:

**The condition is defined by friction with two specific environments, and then the name
locates the problem entirely inside you.**

That's the sleight of hand. A mismatch between a person and a room gets recorded as a
defect in the person. Nobody ever diagnosed a classroom.

---

So what do you do with a bad name?

Most books at this point hand you a list of nicer words. You've seen it. *Instead of
impulsive, say spontaneous. Instead of distracted, say curious.*

I'm going to give you a version of that list, because it's useful. But first I want to
put a rule on it, because without the rule it's just flattery, and flattery won't hold
weight when you're actually struggling.

**The rule: a reframe has to be more accurate than the thing it replaces. Not nicer.
More accurate.**

If it's only nicer, you'll know. Some part of you will roll its eyes, and it'll be right,
and you'll trust the whole project less. I've been handed a lot of nicer words over the
years and they slid straight off, because I could feel somebody trying to make me feel
better about a thing they still thought was a defect.

Accurate is different. Accurate sticks, because you can test it against your own life.

So, with that rule:

**"Easily distracted"** describes a system that samples widely instead of narrowly. That
is a genuine cost when you need to hold one thing. It's a genuine advantage when the
important information is off to the side — which, in a shop, is most of the time. The
guy who notices the wrong sound from the far bay is the same guy who can't stay on the
paperwork. It's one trait, and which end you get depends entirely on the room.

**"Impulsive"** describes a shorter gap between having a thought and acting on it. That
costs you money, occasionally dignity, and sometimes more. It also means that when the
moment is short — and some moments are very short — you're already moving while the
careful people are still deciding.

**"Can't sit still"** is often a body that thinks better in motion. I have worked out more
connection problems walking the shop floor than I ever have sitting at the desk. The
motion isn't a distraction from the thinking. For some of us the motion *is* the
thinking.

**"Inconsistent focus"** is the big one, and it's the one that gets called laziness most
often. It isn't inconsistent. It's *conditional* — reliably conditional, on interest,
novelty, urgency, and challenge. That's a real limitation and I'm not dressing it up. But
it is a limitation with a shape, and things with shapes can be worked with. "Lazy" has no
shape. "Lazy" gives you nothing to do on Monday morning.

Notice that every one of those keeps the cost in the sentence. That's deliberate. A
reframe that deletes the cost is a lie, and you'll catch it eventually and throw out the
whole book.

---

There's a version of self-acceptance I want to warn you about.

The soft version says: love yourself, celebrate your uniqueness, you're perfect as you
are. I understand why people say it. I don't think it works, at least not for us, and I
think for a lot of us it makes things worse — because it asks you to *feel* differently
without giving you any reason to.

If you spent thirty years collecting evidence that you're defective, someone telling you
to feel good about it is asking you to override the evidence with sentiment. Our brains
are very bad at that. We're pattern machines. Sentiment loses to pattern every time.

The version that worked for me was colder and more useful:

**Stop arguing about whether you're broken. Start collecting data on where you work.**

Not "am I a good person." Not "do I love myself." Just: what were the conditions the last
five times I did something genuinely good? What was the room like? What was the deadline?
Who else was there? What was at stake?

That's answerable. And the answers accumulate into something you can actually act on,
which is a lot more than affirmations ever gave me.

Self-acceptance showed up eventually, but it showed up as a *byproduct* — a thing that
happened while I was busy building conditions that worked. It never arrived when I went
at it directly.

---

One more thing about the word "disorder," and then we'll move on.

You don't have to fight it. It's a clinical term, it's how you get accommodations, it's
how insurance works, it's how a doctor communicates with another doctor. It has a job.
Let it do its job.

Just don't let it be your self-description.

There's a real difference between *I have a diagnosis of ADHD* and *I am disordered.* The
first is a fact about a file. The second is a story about a person. They sound similar
enough that you can carry the second one around for years thinking you're just being
accurate.

I carried it for three. Not one useful thing came of it.

---

So that's the trap: a name chosen by people you inconvenienced, describing friction with
two rooms, quietly reassigned to your character.

The next part of this book is about the better description. Not a nicer one — a more
accurate one, one that explains why the same brain fails at the form and excels on the
floor.

It starts with a metaphor that took me twenty years to find and about six words to say.
