# CHAPTER 1
## The Kid in the Hallway

I spent most of third grade in the hallway.

Not as punishment, exactly — more like a permanent arrangement everyone had quietly
agreed to. There was a desk out there, just outside the classroom door, and the door
stayed open so I could hear the lesson. That was the compromise. I got to attend class.
Class got to happen without me in it.

I couldn't stay quiet. I couldn't stay still. My classmates thought I was hilarious,
which did not help. My teacher had a different view, and honestly, she wasn't wrong — I
was a professional at disrupting the peace, and I was good at my job.

Nobody used the word ADHD. It was the early eighties in Grayslake, Illinois, and the
available explanations were shorter. Doesn't apply himself. Too much energy. Bright but
disruptive. Every one of those means the same thing, which is: *we don't know what to do
with you, so we're going to describe you until you go away.*

So they described me. And the system kept passing me along anyway, grade after grade,
because I wasn't failing exactly — I was just impossible to teach in the way they knew
how to teach.

Then came the Constitution test.

---

In Illinois in the eighties, you couldn't get out of eighth grade without passing a test
on the U.S. Constitution. State requirement. No way around it, no creative resistance
available, no teacher willing to pass me along on this one. Everybody had to sit down and
know the thing.

I failed it.

Not because I couldn't have learned it. Because I could not make myself care about it, and
at that age I hadn't yet figured out that those are two different problems. My brain
treated the Constitution the way it treated most assigned material: as noise from a
system I hadn't opted into. It slid off. I sat down to study and my mind went somewhere
more interesting, which was anywhere.

The rest of the class passed. They got a field trip — the reward for following the
program.

I got the library, alone, to retake the test.

For the record: I still didn't care about the Constitution.

---

There was a television on in the library.

Somebody had it tuned to the space shuttle launch — there were a few seventh graders in
there too, and the broadcast was running because a teacher was going up. Christa
McAuliffe. Every school in America had some version of that TV on that morning.

I was supposed to be taking a test. I was drawn to the screen instantly, the way I was
drawn to anything that moved, and I watched.

Seventy-three seconds after liftoff, Challenger came apart.

January 28, 1986.

I didn't understand what I was seeing right away. Nobody did. There was a shape in the
sky that wasn't the shape it was supposed to be, and then a long silence in the library,
and then the slow arrival of the fact.

Here's the part I've thought about for forty years:

Every other eighth grader in my school was on a bus. They had followed the program, they
had earned the reward, and they were somewhere else entirely. As far as I know I was the
only kid in my grade in Grayslake who saw it happen live.

I was there because I failed.

---

I want to be careful here, because there's a version of this story that's too neat and I
don't believe it. I'm not going to tell you the universe put me in that library. I'm not
going to tell you failing the test was secretly good, or that watching seven people die
was a gift, because it wasn't, and it isn't the kind of thing you spin.

But I'll tell you what I actually took from it, which took me another twenty years to
put into words:

*The reward for following the program is not the same thing as being where the truth is.*

That's it. That's the whole lesson. Everybody else got the field trip. I got the thing
that actually happened. Neither of us chose it. But it was the first time I noticed that
the path I kept failing to stay on and the path where things were actually occurring were
not always the same path.

I spent the next two decades not knowing what to do with that observation. I want to be
honest about the shape of those decades, because this is the part inspirational books
skip. Being the kid in the hallway is not a fun origin story while you're living it. It's
a slow accumulation of evidence that something is wrong with you, delivered by people who
mostly mean well, in a vocabulary that gets more clinical as you get older. *Doesn't apply
himself* becomes *unreliable* becomes *doesn't take this seriously* becomes, eventually,
a set of things you say about yourself before anyone else can.

I got out of school. I went into steel.

And steel, it turned out, was the first place anybody handed me a problem my brain was
actually built for.

---

I started as a fabricator. Hands on the metal — cutting, fitting, welding, learning by
touch what holds and what only looks like it holds. It's loud, it's physical, it changes
every day, and it does not care even slightly whether you can sit still. What it cares
about is whether the thing you made is right.

Turns out I was good at that.

Not because I'd finally learned discipline. Because the work matched the wiring. A shop
floor is a high-input, fast-changing, physically demanding environment where you're
tracking eleven things at once and the consequences of being wrong are immediate and
visible. That is not a hard environment for a brain like mine. That's the environment my
brain was apparently designed for, running at last on native hardware instead of in
emulation.

Nobody told me that's what was happening. I just noticed I stopped being the problem.

Thirty years later I'm a detailer. I do the drawings now — I take what somebody else
imagined and work out exactly how it goes together, bolt by bolt and dimension by
dimension, so the steel fits when it lands on site. Navy Pier. Soldier Field. The
Washington/Wabash station. Pieces of this city are standing up because I sat down and
figured out how they connect.

The kid who couldn't pass a test on how the government fits together spends his life
working out how buildings fit together.

I don't think that's ironic. I think that's the actual finding, and it's most of what
this book is about: **it was never a general defect. It was a specific mismatch.** Put
that same brain against a problem it's built for and it doesn't just cope. It outperforms.

---

I was thirty-two when someone finally gave it a name.

I'll tell you about that in the next chapter, including the part where having the name
made things worse before it made them better, which nobody warns you about.

But I want to end this chapter where it started, in the hallway, because if you're
reading this you were probably out there too. Maybe not literally. Maybe your version was
the daydreamer by the window, or the one who tested well and turned in nothing, or the
girl nobody flagged at all because you went quiet instead of loud and quiet doesn't get
referred. There are a lot of doors to that hallway.

Here's what I want you to hear from someone forty years down the road:

You were not out there because something was wrong with you.

You were out there because you were running fast hardware on a system that only had
software for one kind of mind — and rather than write new software, it moved your desk.

That's not a character flaw. That's an infrastructure problem.

And infrastructure problems can be solved. I've spent twenty years solving mine, badly at
first and then better, and what I found is in the rest of this book.

Let's go get your desk back in the room.
